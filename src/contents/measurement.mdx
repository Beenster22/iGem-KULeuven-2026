The lack of established measurement methods for *Phocaeicola vulgatus* DSM 1447 did not stop us. It defined the challenge we set out to solve. With almost no published genetic tools or standardized protocols to build on, we developed and systematically characterized a measurement toolkit for this non-model, strictly anaerobic organism.

Our work went beyond measuring individual outcomes. We established the conditions for reliable <Link to="/results#growth-curves-and-medium-comparison">growth and medium selection</Link>, characterized <Link to="/results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus">antibiotic susceptibility</Link>, quantified <Link to="/results#conjugation-into-p-vulgatus">conjugation efficiency</Link>, and compared <Link to="/results#a-characterized-promoter-library-for-p-vulgatus">promoter activity</Link>. Crucially, these experiments were connected: growth optimization informed our cultivation strategy, antibiotic susceptibility guided selection conditions, and the conjugation and promoter assays built on the conditions established in the preceding experiments. Each step helped us refine the next and identify limitations that would otherwise have remained hidden.

We documented the reasoning behind our methods, including controls, calibration decisions, detection limits, unexpected results, and unsuccessful approaches. This makes our work more than a collection of experimental results. It provides a transparent, reproducible starting point for future teams working with *P. vulgatus*, allowing them to build on our findings rather than start from scratch.

We believe outstanding measurement is not just about obtaining a result, but about making that result reliable, interpretable, and useful beyond our own project. By turning a largely uncharacterized measurement landscape into a documented experimental pipeline, we aim to give future researchers a stronger foundation for engineering this organism. Every limitation we identified and every method we refined contributes to that foundation.

<ExpandableText title="Q1 · What is measured, and in what units?">


Kinetic OD600 absorbance in 96-well microplates, read every 30 minutes for 24–48 h under strict anaerobic conditions, fitted by Gaussian-process (GP) regression with the open-source DGrowthR package. One experiment (panel D) was read at OD630; its absolute values are not pooled with the others, though relative medium rankings are expected to be unaffected since every condition on that plate shares the wavelength.

<div className="measure-table">

| **Symbol** | **Parameter** | **Unit** | **Transfers between instruments?** |
|---|---|---|---|
| µmax | Maximum specific growth rate | h⁻¹ | Yes, absolute |
| t<sub>d</sub> | Doubling time | h | Yes, absolute |
| λ | Lag time | h | Yes, absolute |
| ΔOD | Total optical density increase | OD600 | Within this series only |
| AUC | Area under the fitted ln(OD) curve | ln(OD)·h | Ranking only |

</div>

**The first three are absolute.** They are derived from the shape of ln(OD) against time, so multiplying every reading by a constant adds a constant to ln(OD) and vanishes when the derivative is taken. Another laboratory with a different reader and no pathlength correction will measure the same ~46m if the biology is the same. ΔOD scales with pathlength and does not transfer that way, which is why we say which is which rather than describing all six as standard units.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q2 · How is it calculated?">


**Why a Gaussian process rather than a logistic fit.** Growth curves have a lag phase, an exponential phase and a plateau. Parametric models impose a fixed functional form on all three, which biases the estimated rate whenever the real curve departs from it, and in a non-model organism we had no grounds to assume it would not. A Gaussian process (GP) assumes no functional form: it fits a smooth curve and returns a posterior at every point. Because the fit is to ln(OD), the derivative gives the instantaneous rate µ(t) with confidence bounds. The exponential phase is identified from the data rather than chosen by eye, and measurement uncertainty propagates into µ.

<div className="measure-eq">
<div className="eq-grid">
<div className="eq-row"><span className="eq-lhs">ΔOD</span><span className="eq-eq">=</span><span className="eq-rhs"><i>OD</i><sub>max</sub> − <i>OD</i><sub>initial</sub></span></div>
<div className="eq-row"><span className="eq-lhs">ln(<i>OD</i><sub>t</sub>)</span><span className="eq-eq">=</span><span className="eq-rhs">ln(<i>OD</i><sub>0</sub>) + μ<i>t</i><span className="eq-note">so the slope of ln(OD) against time is μ</span></span></div>
<div className="eq-row"><span className="eq-lhs"><i>t</i><sub>d</sub></span><span className="eq-eq">=</span><span className="eq-rhs"><span className="eq-frac"><span>ln 2</span><span>μ<sub>max</sub></span></span></span></div>
<div className="eq-row"><span className="eq-lhs">λ</span><span className="eq-eq">=</span><span className="eq-rhs">time at which the tangent at μ<sub>max</sub> meets the baseline ln(OD)</span></div>
<div className="eq-row"><span className="eq-lhs">AUC</span><span className="eq-eq">=</span><span className="eq-rhs">integral of the fitted ln(OD) curve over the measurement</span></div>
</div>
</div>


The GP fits to ln(OD/OD₀), where OD₀ is the first blank-corrected reading for that condition. The log-transform is computed after medium-specific blanking, never before. ΔOD is computed independently from the same blank-corrected trace with no fitting, and is reported beside every fitted parameter as the model-free check on it.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q3 · What controls were used, and what does each one exclude?">


<div className="measure-table">

| **Control** | **Role** | **Alternative it rules out** |
|---|---|---|
| Cell-free wells of each medium, on every plate | Defines the zero for that medium | That medium color is read as growth, or that real growth is masked by a blanking artifact |
| mGAM carried on all five plates | Cross-plate growth reference | That drift between plates is read as a medium effect |
| Two strains: type strain and fecal isolate | Generalization check | That a result holds only for one laboratory stock |

</div>

**Why per-medium blanking is not optional.** The four media differ substantially in intrinsic absorbance. A single global blank fails in one of two directions, and both act toward the conclusion we drew: under-blanking a dark medium attributes color to growth, inflating ΔOD for exactly the medium that looks best; over-blanking a pale medium masks real growth, scoring a usable medium as dead.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q4 · How was it calibrated, and what are the limits?">


No pathlength correction was applied and no OD-to-CFU calibration exists for DSM 1447, so optical density is a biomass proxy on our own scale. The rate parameters are unaffected, for the reason given in Q1.

- **† marginal** — conditions with ΔOD at or below 0.03. At that level the growth signal is close to the noise of the measurement, so the fitted parameters are unreliable however confident the fit looks.
- **‡ not fitted** — conditions the package could not fit, because they contained three or fewer distinct optical densities.

**The trap these exist to catch.** A tight standard deviation describes the model’s confidence in its own fit, not whether the fit reflects real growth. A GP returns a numeric fit for almost any input, including smooth noise, and reports it with a small posterior. For † rows the error bars must be read alongside the ΔOD value rather than on their own.

**The flags fall on our own best result.** The optimized MM formulation reached ΔOD = 0.044, which sits at the edge of that threshold. We therefore report the factorial’s ranking of factors, which the design supports, and we do not report MM as a working medium for *P. vulgatus* or quote a growth rate for it.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q5 · How many replicates, and of what kind?">


- **Technical replicates** — wells on the same plate from the same inoculum: six in the pilot plates, three in the final run. These measure pipetting and reading precision.
- **Biological replicates** — independent plates prepared on separate days from separate precultures. These are what support a reproducibility claim.
- **Cross-plate anchor** — mGAM on all five plates, reproducing within ΔOD 0.40–0.49 on four of them.
- **Reported uncertainty** — the posterior standard deviation returned by the Gaussian process, read alongside ΔOD rather than on its own.

Because biological replication exists here, the doubling times in Table 1.1 are reproducibility claims and not merely precision claims. That distinction is stated explicitly because it does not hold everywhere in this document: Section 4 rests on technical replicates alone and says so.

</ExpandableText>

<div className="measure-table">

| **Limitation** | **What the data still permits** | **Mitigation** |
|---|---|---|
| No pathlength correction | µ, t<sub>d  </sub>and λ unaffected; ΔOD valid for ranking within this series | Which parameters transfer is stated in Q1. Pathlength correction is a software setting on future runs. |

</div>

<ExpandableText title="Q7 · What does this give another team?">


- **Blank each medium against its own cell-free wells, on every plate.** With media that differ in color, a global blank biases the comparison toward whichever medium is darkest — usually the richest, and usually the one you hope wins.
- **Carry one medium on every plate as a cross-plate anchor, and publish the run where it failed.**
- **Report a model-free metric beside the model-based ones.** ΔOD costs nothing, assumes nothing, and is what tells you when a confident-looking fit is fitting noise.
- **Say which of your parameters transfer.** Rates and times in h⁻¹ and h are absolute; optical densities are not.
- **Set the marginal threshold before you see the results**, and let it fall on your own best condition when it does.

</ExpandableText>

## 2. Antibiotic susceptibility

A selection plate is a measurement instrument, and its calibration is the inhibitory concentration. Nine antibiotics, two strains, reported as IC90 in **mg/L** (the unit EUCAST and the clinical literature use) so the numbers can be read against published values and not only against each other. The same data answers two questions at once: what we can select on, and what would stop an engineered *P. vulgatus* if its growth ever escaped control. It also found a ceiling in its own readout, treated in Q4.

<ExpandableText title="Q1 · What is measured, and in what units?">


The reported quantity is **IC90, in mg/L**: the concentration giving 90 % growth inhibition, defined on OD630 read after 48 h, prepared from weighed stocks. mg/L is the unit EUCAST and the clinical literature use, which lets our values be compared with published ones, although published data on *P. vulgatus* specifically remains scarce.

**Why IC90 rather than MIC.** The MIC of a compound is the lowest concentration that inhibits visible growth: a threshold judged by eye, a yes/no at each dilution step, on which two readers can disagree. IC90 is computed from optical density and is quantitative rather than visual [ref: Veiga, A., Toledo, M. D. G. T., Rossa, L. S., Mengarda, M., Stofella, N. C. F., Oliveira, L. J., Gonçalves, A. G., & Murakami, F. S. (2019). Colorimetric microdilution assay: Validation of a standard method for determination of MIC, IC50%, and IC90% of antimicrobial compounds. Journal of Microbiological Methods, 162, 50–61. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mimet.2019.05.003].

- **What this shows.** Reproducibility between observers, a value that can be interpolated between two-fold steps rather than pinned to one, and an uncertainty, because the underlying measurement is continuous.
- **What it costs.** IC90 depends on the math of the normalization for what counts as 0 % and what counts as 100 % inhibition. An MIC read by eye has no such dependency. That is exactly where the calibration problem in Q4 comes from, and the reason we report at two cutoffs.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q2 · How is it calculated?">


Every well is converted to a percentage against two references on its own plate before any concentration is interpolated.

</div>

<dt><i>OD</i><sub>well</sub></dt><dd>the antibiotic-containing well, OD630 at 48 h</dd>

<dt><i>OD</i><sub>positive</sub></dt><dd>inoculated mGAM growth control on the same plate</dd>

**Three rules, fixed before any plate was read.**

1. **Cutoff.** 90 % pre-defined as the primary endpoint, with the analysis to be repeated at 85 % regardless of what the 90 % analysis showed.
1. **Repeats.** Each two-fold dilution series repeated three times; whenever the IC90 fell outside the range tested, the experiment was repeated with an altered range rather than reported as greater or less than the range.
1. **Reporting across replicates.** The reported IC90 is the value occurring most often across replicates. Where two concentrations occurred equally often, the higher was reported — the conservative direction for a selection concentration.

A cutoff chosen after seeing the data can always be made to produce a cleaner result, and a reader cannot tell whether it was. Because both cutoffs were planned in advance, the agreement or disagreement between them is evidence about the assay rather than a choice about the answer.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q3 · What controls were used, and what does each one exclude?">


<div className="measure-table">

| **Control** | **Role** | **Alternative it rules out** |
|---|---|---|
| Inoculated-mGAM growth-control row, every plate | Defines 0 % inhibition on that plate | That poor growth on a plate is read as antibiotic effect |
| Per-antibiotic solvent dilution series (ddH₂O, DMSO, 50/95/100 % ethanol) | Defines the blank at every dilution step | That solvent toxicity is attributed to the antibiotic |
| 96-pin replicator from a single master plate | Equalizes starting density across every series on the plate | That a difference between antibiotics is a difference in inoculum |
| Same-plate, same-read scoring | Keeps the reference contemporaneous with the sample | That drift between plates or reads is read as susceptibility |

</div>

<ExpandableText title="Why a pin replicator rather than a multichannel pipette?">

We chose throughput and batch control over per-well precision, deliberately. A multichannel pipette would have transferred a better-defined number of cells into each plate. The pin replicator transfers a less precisely known volume but let every plate be made in one session from a single master plate, which removes the dominant error term in a multi-plate panel: plates made at different times from different master dilutions start at different densities, and that difference propagates into every IC90 on them. The cost is that we do not know the starting inoculum in CFU/mL — see Q6.

</ExpandableText>

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q4 · How was it calibrated, and what are the limits?">


The 90 % cutoff is not a neutral convention here. It is a calibration decision, and auditing it changed how the data are reported.

The blank in the equation above is **solvent in ddH₂O**, while the sample wells are in **mGAM** (absorbs at 630 nm). A fully inhibited well therefore never reads down to the blank.

</div>

Every term on the right is measured, so the ceiling is computable rather than estimated. A 90 % cutoff is applied one to four points below a ceiling that itself moves by about two points from plate to plate. That is the quantitative reason 7.3 % of all 699 computed well values fall between 85 % and 90 %, and why replicates of genuinely inhibited antibiotics were discarded at 90 %.

<div className="measure-table">

| **Reporting the 85 % analysis alongside is therefore not a rescue.** At 85 % the cutoff clears the ceiling band; at 90 % it does not. **This is a calibration failure, not a measurement failure.** Nothing was measured incorrectly — every optical density is right. What is wrong is the reference point the percentages are computed against, chosen before any reading was taken. **The error is one-directional.** The assay systematically understates inhibition, so a compound that works can be scored as not reaching the cutoff, but a compound that does not work can never be scored as working. No reported IC90 is too low; some are simply missing. **The fix for future runs** is one reagent change: blank against sterile, uninoculated mGAM with matching solvent instead of solvent in ddH₂O. That puts the ceiling near 100 % and makes a 90 % cutoff mean what it says. |
|---|

</div>

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q5 · How many replicates, and of what kind?">


- **Technical replicates.** The three series for an antibiotic within a round sit on the same plate. They reduce batch effects, but they are wells, not independent experiments.
- **Biological and temporal replication.** Compounds were re-run on different days from different overnight cultures.
- **Re-runs on range failure.** Series whose IC90 fell outside the tested range were repeated over an altered range rather than censored.
- **Reported value.** The modal IC90 across replicates; ties resolved upward.

**How to read the Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn results.** The test treats replicate wells as independent and does not model the plate as a blocking factor. Wells on one plate are not independent as they share an inoculum, a master plate, a reader pass and an incubation. So, treating them as independent inflates the effective sample size and makes p-values smaller than they should be. It is therefore best interpreted as a **descriptive ranking rather than a strict significance test**, and that is how it is reported. A mixed-effects model with plate as a random effect, or a Friedman test on plate-matched data, would use the same wells and support inference.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q6 · What are the limitations, and how are they mitigated?">

|---|---|---|

</div>

<ExpandableText title="Q7 · What does this give another team?">


- **Report in mg/L.** It costs nothing and makes the data readable against the clinical literature.
- **Blank against the matrix you measured in.** If the sample is in rich medium and the blank is in water, a fully inhibited well cannot reach 100 % and every percentage cutoff silently moves. This applies to any plate-reader assay with a percentage endpoint.
- **Compute your ceiling before you choose your cutoff.** It takes the sterile-medium reading, the blank and the growth control, all of which you already have.
- **Pre-register the cutoff and the sensitivity analysis together.** The second cutoff is only evidence if it was planned before the data were seen.
- **Give every solvent its own dilution series.** Correcting only at the top concentration leaves a gradient of uncorrected solvent down the plate.

</ExpandableText>

## 3. Conjugation

(2010). Efficient electrotransformation of Bacteroides fragilis. Applied and Environmental Microbiology, 76(10), 3325–3332. https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.02420-09]. We tested the protocol against six strains and counted the CFUs/mL, which gives a range of expected yields across the species, widening its applicability.

<ExpandableText title="Q1 · What is measured, and in what units?">


The reported quantity is the **titer of transconjugants in the resuspended mating lawn, in CFU/mL** is an absolute, dimensioned unit, obtained by counting colonies at a dilution inside the countable window and multiplying back.

**Titer, not efficiency.** A conjugation efficiency is transconjugants per recipient (or per donor): a dimensionless ratio that requires plating the recipient on non-selective medium in the same experiment. **Titer** depends on how much lawn was scraped and the volume it was resuspended in, both fixed by our protocol. Our titers are therefore directly comparable to each other. **Efficiency** is independent of how much material was harvested, because the same scraping appears in numerator and denominator. An efficiency would transfer to another laboratory; a titer ranks. One additional dilution series per mating, plated on non-selective mGAM, converts every titer here into a true efficiency.

**A failed control, reported.** An earlier attempt used ParB primers to confirm species identity. Only DSM 1447 amplified. The root cause sits one step earlier: the candidate sequence was checked *in silico* against only two genomes — DSM 1447 and *E. coli* DH5α. A search including the other five recipients would have shown the gene missing before any primers were ordered.

<ExpandableText title="Q4 · How was it calibrated, and what are the limits?">


**The dilution series is its own calibration.** If the suspension is uniform and the dilutions were made correctly, the titer back-calculated from every countable dilution must be the same number. Disagreement is evidence that something other than transconjugants is being counted. This costs nothing beyond plates already poured, and it is what identified the one unusable series in our data (Table 3.2).

**The selection concentration is not calibrated for every strain.** Plates carried 25 µg/mL erythromycin. Section 2 measured what that does to each recipient, and the answer is not uniform.

- **DSM 1447** is inhibited far below the plate concentration, so its colonies cannot be unconjugated recipient. Its count stands.
- **RCC 1806** tolerates roughly [256 mg/L] an order of magnitude above the plate concentration. Unconjugated RCC 1806 can grow on these plates, so its count is an upper bound, not a titer — and it is currently the highest-ranked strain.
- **The remaining four DSM strains** have not been checked against 25 µg/mL. Each one’s IC90 needs comparing to the plate concentration before its count is reported as a titer.

It was a mistake to assume at the outset that unconjugated *P. vulgatus* could not grow on the selection plates. The correction is stated per strain rather than as a blanket caveat, because a blanket caveat would wrongly discount DSM 1447 along with RCC 1806.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q5 · How many replicates, and of what kind?">


- **Six recipient strains**, each conjugated with the same donor under the same protocol.
- **Independent repeat** for DSM 1447, which is what exposed the inconsistency in the first run.
- **Multiple countable dilutions per strain**, used as an internal consistency check rather than averaged blindly.
- **Two negative controls per run**, both returning zero colonies.

**Protocol deviations, recorded as they happened.** RCC 1806 and DSM 108228 had not grown overnight, so colonies were taken directly from plates into fresh cultures; those did not grow either, and the original overnights had by then turned turbid and were used instead. Those cells were therefore most likely in lag rather than early log phase. No gentamicin was added at the dilution step, only mGAM. Both deviations touch RCC 1806, the top-ranked strain.

**What the repeat separated.** An earlier run recovered no transconjugants from two strains. Raising the recipient density to OD600 0.4 from 0.2 recovered all of them, which supports the dilution explanation over the gentamicin one. The counts then separated two cases a presence/absence readout cannot: DSM 108228 conjugates perfectly well (3.3 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) and was genuinely lost to over-dilution; DSM 108234 does not (5.9 × 10³ CFU/mL) and conjugates poorly even when the protocol works. Plating only 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ would have missed it entirely.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q7 · What does this give another team?">


- **Count several dilutions and check they agree.** The back-calculated titer must be constant across the series. It costs nothing, needs no extra plates, and is the only thing that caught our worst data point.
- **Plate further than you think you need to.** A strain that conjugates poorly is invisible if you only plate the dilutions that worked for your best strain, and you will mistake it for a protocol failure.
- **Check your selection concentration against your recipient’s inhibitory concentration.** A selection plate only selects if the antibiotic actually inhibits what you are selecting against. Ours did not, for one strain of the six.
- **Validate primers against every template you will use them on.** A negative band from a primer that cannot anneal is not information.
- **Expect the spread.** Across six strains of one species, yields differed by five orders of magnitude. A conjugation protocol validated on one strain is not validated for the species.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q2 · How is it calculated?">



1. **Average the control wells.** Medium-only (mGAM), LB-only and plasmid-free DSM 1447, separately per channel and for OD600.
1. **Apply the outlier rule.** Discard any well whose blank-corrected OD600 is below 0.5, and record which wells this removes.
1. **Correct and normalize each well.** Subtract the matched medium blank from both the channel reading and the OD600, then divide. A control in LB is corrected against the LB blank, never the mGAM blank.
1. **Reference to the wild type.** Subtract the identically processed plasmid-free value, so that a non-expressing well reads zero.
1. **Estimate the noise at zero.** Pool the within-construct variances of the constructs sitting at background.
1. **Set the limits.** LOD = 3 s₀, LOQ = 10 s₀, per channel.
1. **Classify.** Above LOQ, report the value with its standard deviation. Between LOD and LOQ, report as detected and not quantified. Below LOD, report the LOD as an upper bound and never the point value.
1. **Cross-check the luminescence independently.** Divide raw construct RLU by raw medium-plus-substrate RLU. This uses no normalization and should agree with step 7; on this plate it does.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q4 · How was it calibrated, and what are the limits?">


**LOD**, the limit of detection, is the smallest signal that can confidently be called something other than background. Below it the only defensible statement is "less than the LOD" and never "zero", and never the measured point value, which at that level is mostly noise. **LOQ**, the limit of quantification, is the smallest signal that can carry a number; between the two a signal is real but its magnitude is not reliable, so it is reported as detected, not quantified.

The conventional multipliers are three standard deviations of the blank for LOD and ten for LOQ. Three corresponds to roughly a one percent chance of calling pure background a signal; ten is about where the relative error on the value falls to ten percent. Both are properties of the assay in the matrix it is run in, not of the instrument.

<ExpandableText title="Q5 · How many replicates, and of what kind?">


Each construct is three wells seeded from one overnight culture; each control is two. These are **technical replicates**. The standard deviations quoted throughout are the spread among wells of the same culture, so they measure pipetting, seeding and reading precision. A standard deviation across wells of one culture bounds the within-plate precision of the assay. It says nothing about whether a separate colony grown on a separate day would give the same number. **So we report precision and we do not report reproducibility** unlike Section 1, where independent plates on separate days do support a reproducibility claim.

The protocol specifies three separate colonies per construct; this plate was not run that way, and the 102 % coefficient of variation on BT–NanoLuc shows that even well-to-well variation can be severe at low signal. **Precision where there was range:** coefficients of variation of 2.5 %, 5.2 %, 14.6 % and 22 % for the four constructs that clear detection. Large replicate spreads appear only below the detection limit, which is what a detection limit predicts.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Q7 · What does this give another team?">


The transferable result is not our promoter ranking. It is the decision it implies, and the cost of getting it wrong.

- **Read the medium before choosing the reporter.** One plate of medium-only wells across every channel, measured against the reader’s usable window, costs an afternoon and would have told us in advance that KOFP-7 was unusable in mGAM and that sfGFP had little room left.
- **Set the gain on the blank, not on a bright well, and give it an acceptance criterion.** Automatic gain adjustment in a strongly autofluorescent medium calibrates the detector to the broth. Our rule for next time: adjust on the medium-only blank and require it to read below about a quarter of full scale.
- **In a dark autofluorescent broth, prefer luminescence.** NanoLuc gave three to four orders of magnitude of range where the fluorescent channels gave none, because its background is chemical rather than optical.
- **If fluorescence is required, go as red as the biology allows.** Detectability tracked spectral distance from the medium’s autofluorescence, not reporter brightness on paper.
- **Report a detection limit, not a zero.** Three blank or near-blank replicates per channel convert every null into an upper bound another group can test against.

The confirmation run this section points to is small and specific: the same constructs with an empty-pNBU2 control added, three separate colonies per construct, gain and focal height fixed manually once and held for the whole run, and a dilution series of one strong NanoLuc lysate to establish linear range and saturation.

</ExpandableText>

## Conclusion

Our output is not only ranking promoters and characterizing reporters. It is the method that produced these rankings: what to measure, in what order, against what controls, and what each step tells the next one. Building in an organism without precedent means that knowledge (the practical knowledge of how measurements relate to each other in this system) is itself a contribution. **We offer it here not as a finished toolkit, but as a documented path through the work of making one.**
