## Wiki Writing

Writing your iGEM wiki is one of the most important parts of the competition: it's how you
communicate your experiments, results, and ideas to the judges and to everyone else. Many teams
don't have anyone with web development experience, so we put together a set of guides on how to
get started.

There are three standard templates iGEM offers to start a wiki from: Markdown, HTML/CSS/JS, and
React. That order roughly tracks two things at once, difficulty and creative freedom. Markdown is
the simplest option, but also the most limited for realizing an ambitious design. HTML/CSS/JS is
more advanced and can get you almost anywhere you want if you're willing to put in the work.
React is the most complex of the three, but if you already have programming experience, it makes
building a polished, interactive wiki far more streamlined.

Below are guides for each of those three templates, plus two of our own: how our team combined
React with Markdown to split the work by skill level, and how to host a copy of the wiki yourself
for faster, private previews in the run-up to the wiki freeze.

## How to build a brand identity for your iGEM team

### Why this contribution

Many iGEM teams have strong science but struggle to explain it to people outside the lab. Our team faced the same problem: PMOS is common, yet information about it is often hard to understand or hard to trust. We asked ourselves one question: "How can scientific knowledge about PMOS be communicated in an accessible and scientifically reliable way to a broad audience?"

To answer it, we built a complete brand identity for EMPOWER, following a professional branding process. This page turns that process into a guide that any iGEM team can reuse, whatever their project. It includes what we did in each phase, a template for every step, and the mistakes and lessons we would pass on.

**Who it is for:** teams that want a consistent, recognisable identity across their wiki, posters, social media, merchandise and video, and teams working on Education, Human Practices or Communication.

### Phase 1 · Research

*Start with the audience and the problem, not with the logo.*

#### What we did

- **Briefing.** We wrote down client, deliverable, problem statement, goal and scope. Scope included what we would not do: we did not develop a medical treatment or clinical identity, only branding, communication, visual implementation and awareness.
- **Research question.** One question that guides every later decision (see above).
- **Desk research.** Four insights: PMOS is among the most common hormonal conditions in women; clear information is hard to find; scientific communication is often complex; there is room for a brand that translates complex knowledge.
- **Competitor analysis.** We looked at three types of players: a scientific platform for PMOS (strong in credibility, weak in accessibility), a community organisation (strong in engagement, weaker in science), and academic research projects including iGEM teams (strong in research, hard to understand for a broad audience).
- **Benchmarking.** We placed them on three axes: science, accessibility, and innovation. No competitor combined all three with a strong visual identity. That gap became EMPOWER's position.
- **Interview.** One semi-structured interview focused on health communication. It confirmed that PMOS is unfamiliar to many people, that reliability is essential, that medical language is seen as complex, and that a university name raises trust.
- **Consumer insight.** We summarised everything in four boxes (see template).

### Phase 2 · Strategy

*Strategy turns research into decisions you can check later: if a design does not fit the strategy, you change the design.*

- **Brand values.** Reliability (evidence-based and validated), accessibility (easy to understand), warmth (human and supportive), innovation (future-focused), education (knowledge as empowerment).
- **Archetype.** We combined The Sage (knowledge, research, understanding) with The Caregiver (support, guidance, empathy): scientific expertise with a warm approach.
- **Tension fields.** Where does the brand sit between two poles? Scientific ↔ human; academic ↔ accessible; educational ↔ engaging; complex ↔ clear; serious ↔ warm. We chose a deliberate balance and did not pick a side.
- **Positioning.** Purpose, USP, ESP and one statement (below).

### Phase 4 · Logo development

- **Sketches.** Many symbols to translate the values into something recognisable.
- **Concepts.** Several directions, each focused on one aspect: women's health, scientific research, awareness, empowerment.
- **Feedback and refinement.** The team liked the combination of uterus shape, lily and DNA, but found the gut microbiome missing. The next round integrated *Phocaeicola vulgatus* (*P. vulgatus*) into the logo.
- **Final logo.** Colour variants were tested for a consistent, recognisable identity.

**Typography:** Acid Green for headlines, titles and statements; Poppins for body text, information and education.

- **Colours.** Soft pastels for warmth, accessibility and trust, with a dark purple for contrast. Every colour has a meaning (health and growth, science and innovation, women's health and empathy, clarity and simplicity).
- **Typography.** One expressive font for headlines and one clean font for body text.
- **Visual elements.** Three recurring images that work without the logo: the lily (women's health, growth, resilience), *P. vulgatus* (gut health, research, innovation) and the PMOS ribbon (awareness, support, community).
- **Photography style.** Human, educational, scientific, warm.

### What we learned

- **Branding is more than a logo.** A strong identity starts with research and strategy, and with constant feedback from the team. Every design choice should have a reason that you can explain.
- **Plan for waiting.** The designer often had to wait for feedback, information, or decisions from the team and had to adjust the planning several times. Give one person the final say on brand decisions, and agree on deadlines for feedback.
- **Feedback improves the result.** The logo only got its *P. vulgatus* element after feedback said the microbiome was missing.
- **Use it quickly.** The identity was adopted fast in presentations, social media and the poster, and that is what makes it consistent.
- **Try motion early.** A short video forces you to check whether the identity works with movement, sound and real footage.

### Practical tips for your own team

- Involve someone with design skills from the start, and give them a written brief.
- Check the licence of every font and image before you share them or put them on merchandise.
- Follow the official iGEM logo rules when you place the iGEM or university logo next to yours.
- Make sure text has enough contrast with pastel backgrounds, so the wiki stays readable.
- Ask for consent before you show real people in photos, videos or quotes.
- Keep the brandbook short (about 14 pages is enough) and visual, so that every team member actually uses it.
