## Wiki Writing

Writing your iGEM wiki is one of the most important parts of the competition: it's how you
communicate your experiments, results, and ideas to the judges and to everyone else. Many teams
don't have anyone with web development experience, so we put together a set of guides on how to
get started.

There are three standard templates iGEM offers to start a wiki from: Markdown, HTML/CSS/JS, and
React. That order roughly tracks two things at once, difficulty and creative freedom. Markdown is
the simplest option, but also the most limited for realizing an ambitious design. HTML/CSS/JS is
more advanced and can get you almost anywhere you want if you're willing to put in the work.
React is the most complex of the three, but if you already have programming experience, it makes
building a polished, interactive wiki far more streamlined.

Below are guides for each of those three templates, plus two of our own: how our team combined
React with Markdown to split the work by skill level, and how to host a copy of the wiki yourself
for faster, private previews in the run-up to the wiki freeze.

## How to build a brand identity for your iGEM team

### Why this contribution

Many iGEM teams have strong science but struggle to explain it to people outside the lab. A brand identity gives your project one recognisable voice across your wiki, posters, social media, merchandise and video. This page turns the professional branding process we followed into a guide any team can reuse, whatever their project. It has the steps, a template for each, a checklist, and the lessons we would pass on. The framework was led by Jinne Vanderhoydonck, brand designer, whose expertise helped translate stakeholder insights and scientific concepts into a coherent and accessible visual identity.

**Who it is for:** teams that want a consistent, recognisable identity across their wiki, posters, social media, merchandise and video, and teams working on Education, Human Practices or Communication.

To see how we applied these steps to our own project, read <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-3-marketing-and-brand-identity">Entrepreneurship, section 2.3 (Marketing and brand identity)</Link>.

### Phase 1 · Research

*Start with the audience and the problem, not with the logo.*

- **Briefing.** Write down client, deliverable, problem statement, goal and scope. Include what you will not do.
- **Research question.** One question that guides every later decision.
- **Desk research.** Collect a few insights about your topic and how it is currently communicated.
- **Competitor analysis.** Look at the types of players already communicating about your topic, for example scientific platforms, community organisations and other research projects, including iGEM teams.
- **Benchmarking.** Place them on 2–3 axes that matter for your audience and look for the gap nobody fills.
- **Interview.** Hold at least one semi-structured interview with someone from your audience or a communication expert. Ask for consent.
- **Consumer insight.** Summarise everything in four boxes (see template).

### Phase 2 · Strategy

*Strategy turns research into decisions you can check later: if a design does not fit the strategy, you change the design.*

- **Brand values.** Pick 3–5 words that describe what your brand stands for.
- **Archetype.** Choose one or two brand archetypes (for example the Sage or the Caregiver) to set personality and tone.
- **Tension fields.** Decide where the brand sits between two poles, for example scientific ↔ human or serious ↔ warm. Choose a deliberate balance rather than a side.
- **Positioning.** Purpose, USP, ESP and one statement (see template).

### Phase 4 · Logo development

- **Sketches.** Make many symbols to translate your values into something recognisable.
- **Concepts.** Develop several directions, each focused on one aspect of the project.
- **Feedback and refinement.** Collect team feedback and ask what is missing before you refine.
- **Final logo.** Test colour variants for a consistent, recognisable identity.

### Applications: use the identity everywhere

A brandbook only proves itself in use. Apply the identity to:

- Your competition poster
- Presentations and the wiki
- Social media posts and team introductions
- Merchandise such as hoodies, T-shirts and stickers
- A short video, to check that the identity also works in motion

### What we learned

- **Branding is more than a logo.** A strong identity starts with research and strategy, and with constant feedback from the team. Every design choice should have a reason that you can explain.
- **Plan for waiting.** The designer often had to wait for feedback, information, or decisions from the team and had to adjust the planning several times. Give one person the final say on brand decisions, and agree on deadlines for feedback.
- **Feedback improves the result.** Our logo changed after one feedback round showed that an element was missing. Ask early and ask often.
- **Use it quickly.** The identity was adopted fast in presentations, social media and the poster, and that is what makes it consistent.
- **Try motion early.** A short video forces you to check whether the identity works with movement, sound and real footage.

### Practical tips for your own team

- Involve someone with design skills from the start, and give them a written brief.
- Check the licence of every font and image before you share them or put them on merchandise.
- Follow the official iGEM logo rules when you place the iGEM or university logo next to yours.
- Make sure text has enough contrast with pastel backgrounds, so the wiki stays readable.
- Ask for consent before you show real people in photos, videos or quotes.
- Keep the brandbook short (about 14 pages is enough) and visual, so that every team member actually uses it.

## A codon optimization tool for *Bacteroides*

While planning our constructs we found that no online tool could codon-optimize or harmonize a sequence for *Bacteroides*. Existing tools target hosts such as *E. coli*, yeast or mammalian cells, and their defaults are a poor fit for an AT-rich anaerobe. So we built one and made it free for anyone to use.

It is a Python command-line tool that takes in-frame coding sequences and returns versions that can realistically be expressed in *Bacteroides*. It supports both optimization (maximise expression) and harmonization (preserve the native folding rhythm). It runs on your own machine, and it is open source under the MIT License.
