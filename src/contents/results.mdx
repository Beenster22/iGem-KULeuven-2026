<SectionItem label="Strain selection">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Strain selection</h4>

{/* Text from the team's document "Experimental overview of the toolkit" (written up here with Claude Sonnet 5.5, 2026-10-21); links are the ones its comments ask for. */}

**Goal.** To get started with the wet lab work, we needed to obtain several _P. vulgatus_ strains. As the therapeutics pipeline focuses on the gut, we focused on finding fecal isolates to assess variation between them. We also needed at least one strain with annotated _bsh_ genes to serve as a query to annotate the _bsh_ genes of the remaining strains.

**Results.** DSM1447/ATCC8482, the type strain, is of fecal origin and has three annotated _bsh_ genes. DSM10822, DSM108234, DSM108228 and RCC1806 are fecal isolates, while DSM3289 is an isolate from a retroperitoneal abscess. They have no annotated _bsh_ genes. See the <Link to="/results#selecting-a-phocaeicola-vulgatus-strain-panel">strain panel</Link> below.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Antibiotic selection">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Antibiotic selection</h4>

**Goal.** Antibiotic susceptibility of _P. vulgatus_ is not well documented. Data in the literature are often only for clinical strains, which of course vary between isolates. By determining the inhibitory concentration (IC90) of _P. vulgatus_ ATCC8482/DSM1447, a laboratory strain, towards multiple antibiotics, we wished to fill in this gap. On top of this, we also determined the IC90 of _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806, a clinical strain of the Raes lab of Bioinformatics and (Eco-)Systems Biology.

**Experimental set-up.** Antibiotics used for susceptibility testing were ampicillin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, tetracycline, metronidazole, gentamicin, kanamycin and spectinomycin. Simvastatin, a statin, was also included. Two-fold serial dilutions in mGAM were made in triplicate and inoculated with _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 or RCC1806. After incubating anaerobically for 48 hours at 30 °C, OD600 was measured.

**Results.** Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and metronidazole were the most effective antibiotics, inhibiting both strains at low concentrations (4 mg/L or less). As the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) specifically recommends the use of metronidazole when the susceptibility is smaller than or equal to 4 mg/L, this is most encouraged. Ampicillin and erythromycin show more variability between strains and thus, use of these antibiotics is not encouraged. The full data are in the <Link to="/results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus">MIC results</Link>.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Reporter gene output">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Reporter gene output</h4>

**Goal.** Measuring promoter strength in _P. vulgatus_, when the standard reporter toolkit is unable to do so: this was our goal. Engineering this organism requires promoters of known strength to tune expression, but almost none have been characterized in it. By using a fluorescent or luminescent readout, different constitutive promoters could be ranked based on strength. The difficulty to overcome was the oxygen dependency of many reporter genes, as _P. vulgatus_ is a strictly anaerobic species, so we combined both detection principles in one library rather than relying on fluorescence alone.

**Experimental set-up.** Five constitutive promoters from literature (BfP1E6, BT1311, BT4001, cfxA and PAM3) were each paired with four reporters (GFP, mCherry, KOFP-7 and NanoLuc) as twenty constructs, so that every promoter could be compared across several emission spectra. The Gibson-assembled constructs are delivered via conjugation from _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir to _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447. Fluorescence and luminescence were read per OD600 and against a wild-type DSM1447 reference, with mGAM and LB blanks and a GFP-containing _E. coli_ positive control on the same plate. More information about the assay's design and normalization can be found on the <Link to="/measurement">measurements page</Link>.

**Results.** NanoLuc gave a clear, well-separated ranking of Cfx > Bfp > BT = PBT. The fluorescent reporters, on the other hand, were almost entirely uninformative: twelve of the thirteen fluorescent constructs failed to distinguish from zero in the GFP, mCherry and KOFP-7 channels once normalized. The single exception was Bfp–mCherry. This does not scale with promoter strength: if mCherry output simply followed the promoter, Cfx–mCherry should have been the brightest construct rather than one of the dimmest. The complete per-construct output, controls and growth data behind this ranking can be found on the <Link to="/results#a-characterized-promoter-library-for-p-vulgatus">promoter library results page</Link>.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Medium comparison">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Medium comparison</h4>

**Goal.** In order to work with a non-model organism and engineer it, our first priority was establishing reproducible, measurable growth. We tested four media under anaerobic conditions: rich and undefined (mGAM, BHI), lean and defined (MM), and WC as a middle ground. We also wanted to see whether our data was strain-dependent by testing two strains: type strain DSM1447 and fecal isolate RCC1806. Our final goal was to optimize the growth yield of DSM1447 in our minimal medium. We used three candidate supplements: soluble starch (carbon source), vitamin K (growth factor) and a pH adjustment to 7.2, and tested all eight combinations in a full 2³ factorial design to understand their influences and interactions.

**Experimental set-up.** For our main experiment, we built a 96-well plate layout with three replicate wells per condition for precision and high statistical power, blanks in triplicate to rule out random contamination, and a 48 h timeframe growing at 37 °C in the anaerobic chamber. The plate reader shook the samples before each OD600 read every 30 minutes, so that 96 time points were collected automatically, in the hope of capturing the bacterial growth phases. Before this, we ran two rounds of experiments on DSM1447 alone, repeating the procedure with freshly plated cultures and new media. For the supplementation study, we designed a second experiment with six wells per condition, measured at OD600 for 48 hours, including the eight supplement combinations and unsupplemented MM and mGAM as references.

**Results.** mGAM supported fast, dense growth in every well while WC grew consistently but far more slowly. BHI, despite carrying slightly more tissue extract than mGAM, did not sustain any growth, giving us an early hint that growth here depends on specific cofactors rather than raw nutrient richness. MM showed almost no signal for the first 20 h, but growth became clearly visible roughly six hours into the second day. The supplementation experiment showed that pH adjustment to 7.2 is necessary but not sufficient on its own: vitamin K and starch only contributed noticeably once the pH had been corrected. See the <Link to="/results#growth-curves-and-medium-comparison">growth curves and medium comparison</Link>.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Conjugation titer testing">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Conjugation titer testing</h4>

**Goal.** Electroporation and heat-shock transformation have previously been used to transform _Bacteroides_ species, however, this is generally done with difficulty, and not for _P. vulgatus_[ref: Ichimura, M., Nakayama-Imaohji, H., Wakimoto, S., Morita, H., Hayashi, T., & Kuwahara, T. (2010). Efficient electrotransformation of *Bacteroides fragilis*. *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*, *76*(10), 3325–3332. https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.02420-09]. Considering that conjugation is done more often, we wanted to optimize it for our _P. vulgatus_ strains. In the therapeutics pipeline, this would help us deliver our CRISPRi construct successfully.

**Experimental set-up.** All six _P. vulgatus_ strains were tested for conjugation titer from _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir in a <Link to="/engineering#conjugation">DBTL</Link> consisting of 3 cycles. After growing to an OD600 = 0.2, the cells were plated out together on mGAM, and subsequently grown overnight on mGAM with gentamicin and erythromycin to select for conjugated _P. vulgatus_. PCR of a ParB-like gene or 16S rDNA combined with BLAST was performed for additional verification. Colonies were counted to determine conjugation titer. <Link to="/engineering#fluorescence-microscopy-conjugation">Fluorescence microscopy was done to confirm the presence of the plasmid</Link>.

**Results.** _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 was found to be most efficient in conjugation, followed by RCC1806 and the rest of the strains. Further optimization is necessary. However, the <Link to="/results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus">MIC data</Link> revealed that the experiment should be repeated using a higher erythromycin concentration or another antibiotic resistance cassette, e.g. tetracycline, due to the possible presence of false positives.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Promotor screening">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Promotor screening</h4>

**Goal.** _P. vulgatus_ has no validated native promoters, and Bacteroidaceae (check out the <Link to="/description#phocaeicola-vulgatus-and-its-nature">project description</Link> to learn more about the name change) initiate transcription from −7 and −33 consensus elements rather than the −10/−35 elements of _E. coli_, so promoters taken from model organisms cannot be assumed to work. That is why we assembled a small panel of promoters borrowed from the closest well-characterized relatives, _B. thetaiotaomicron_ and _B. fragilis_, and paired them with different suitable reporters, so that we could determine expression strength.

**Experimental set-up.** A literature survey yielded twenty-four promoters validated in _Bacteroides_ spp., from which five were selected for a first pilot experiment: PBT4001, PBT1311, PPAM3, PcfxA and PBfP1E6. This selection spans different origins to limit the chance that all of them lose activity on transfer to a new host. Each promoter was combined with four reporters, giving a library of twenty constructs. A single ribosome binding site, rpiL*, described in the literature as "strong" and "reliable", was kept constant so as not to introduce more variables.

**Results.** The output of this step is our construct panel itself: five promoters in combination with four reporters, all with a constant RBS. Expression strength was then measured in the <Link to="/results#a-characterized-promoter-library-for-p-vulgatus">promoter–reporter screen</Link>.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Codon Optimization & Harmonization tool">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Codon Optimization & Harmonization tool</h4>

**Goal.** Until today no online tool performs codon optimization or harmonization for _Bacteroides_ species. Existing tools target _E. coli_, yeast or mammalian hosts, which are all a poor fit for an AT-rich anaerobe. That is why we built our own and made it freely available. Constructs can be adapted for _Bacteroides_ without the user needing to know the genus' detailed codon preferences. Optimization maximizes translation speed and thus also yield, while harmonization keeps the balance between slow and fast codons, which regulate the speed of protein synthesis.

**Results.** Our tool returns the redesigned sequence as a FASTA file, together with a report that shows, for each check, which problems were found, which the tool fixed, and which are still left. If a sequence cannot be cleaned up completely, it is still returned but flagged as only partially solved, so users can see what is wrong and decide whether they still want to use it.

**Limitations.** The tool has only been checked computationally: we did not yet grow _Bacteroides_ with a redesigned gene to see whether it gives more protein than the original. It also only optimizes codon choice; it does not account for mRNA stability or how well the ribosome can bind. Still, it is already a contribution to the scientific community, making non-model organisms better understandable and engineerable. See the <Link to="/dry-lab#1-3-codon-optimization-tool">tool on our Dry Lab page</Link>.

</SectionItem>

We asked which of four anaerobic media makes _P. vulgatus_ grow fastest and
most reproducibly, and which of mGAM's own ingredients can explain the gap.
Growth of each condition was characterized by fitting its optical density
(OD) data over time, which yields parameters describing how quickly cells

The main differences in medium components create differences in growth
performance. The following components are the most prominent differences
between the media, discussed here to work out which are responsible for the
higher-performing media.

<ExpandableText title="Hemin" tocIgnore>

WC contains 5 mg L⁻¹ hemin, whereas mGAM and MM contain 10 mg L⁻¹; BHI
contains no added hemin. Hemin supplies haem for haem-containing proteins
involved in electron transport. Hemin supplementation has been shown to
improve _P. vulgatus_ growth[^growth-lee]. Its importance may relate to
haem-dependent electron transport: in the related species _B. fragilis_,
hemin was required for functional fumarate reduction and substantially
increased growth rate and biomass yield[^growth-macy].

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Vitamin K compounds" tocIgnore>

mGAM contains vitamin K₁ (5 mg L⁻¹), whereas WC contains menadione
(0.5 mg L⁻¹); neither BHI nor MM contains an explicit vitamin K supplement.
These compounds are relevant because vitamin K₂ helps bacteria transfer
electrons to produce energy. However, _P. vulgatus_ can produce its own
vitamin K₂, and adding certain vitamin K compounds did not improve its growth
under the conditions tested[^growth-fenn]. Vitamin K₁ itself was not tested
there, so whether it improves growth in our medium needed to be determined
experimentally.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Starch" tocIgnore>

Only mGAM contains soluble starch, at 5 g L⁻¹. Starch has been shown to
support rapid growth and high bacterial yields in tested _P. vulgatus_
strains, which also produced more starch-digesting enzymes when grown on
starch than on glucose[^growth-mccarthy]. Starch may therefore improve growth
in mGAM by providing an additional source of carbon and energy.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Glucose and complex nutrients" tocIgnore>

Glucose concentrations are 2.0 g L⁻¹ in BHI, 1.0 in WC, 0.5 in mGAM and 0.2 in
MM. Glucose provides carbon and energy for _P. vulgatus_, which has been
successfully cultivated in a defined medium using glucose as its main carbon
source[^growth-keitel2024]. These differences may therefore affect growth,
but more glucose does not mean better growth — other nutrients must also be
considered, particularly starch in mGAM, which _P. vulgatus_ can use as an
additional carbon source[^growth-mccarthy].

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Bicarbonate" tocIgnore>

Only MM contains added sodium bicarbonate, at 1.25 g L⁻¹. Bicarbonate
provides a source of dissolved CO₂ and helps resist changes in pH, which is
relevant because _P. vulgatus_ requires CO₂ for growth: changing CO₂
availability has been shown to affect bacterial growth and organic acid
production[^growth-keitel2023]. Bicarbonate may therefore influence growth,
although CO₂ supplied through the cultivation atmosphere must also be
considered.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Reducing agents" tocIgnore>

mGAM contains cysteine hydrochloride and sodium thioglycollate, WC was
supplemented with cysteine hydrochloride, and MM contains cysteine. These
reducing agents help remove dissolved oxygen and maintain conditions suitable
for anaerobic growth. Increased oxygen exposure has been shown to impair
_P. vulgatus_ growth, with cysteine suggested to reduce oxygen-related
damage[^growth-keitel2023]. Differences in reducing agents may therefore
affect growth by changing the bacteria's exposure to oxygen.

</ExpandableText>

Because these media differ in several components simultaneously, superior
growth in one medium cannot be attributed to a single ingredient. Subsequent
experiments therefore varied starch, vitamin K₁ and pH individually to assess
their contributions.

mGAM was the clear winner: it grew fastest and reached a final OD of about
0.41, against only 0.13 in WC. It is hard to attribute this to a single
component. However, starch (both amylose and amylopectin) is a likely factor,
since it is known to give rapid growth and high yields in _P. vulgatus_[^growth-mccarthy].
Hemin may also have helped[^growth-lee], and the reducing agents would have
kept conditions properly anaerobic, though this experiment cannot separate out
what each ingredient contributed. Even so, mGAM's advantage was big enough to
make it the natural starting medium for testing starch, vitamin K₁ and pH one
at a time.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Why Gaussian process (GP) regression?" tocIgnore>

Bacterial growth curves have a lag phase, an exponential phase, and a
plateau. Parametric models such as the logistic impose a fixed shape on all
three, which can bias the estimated rate when the real curve deviates from
that shape. A GP makes no assumption about the functional form: it instead
fits a smooth curve to the data and returns a posterior distribution at every
time point. Because we fit to ln(OD), the derivative of the fitted curve
gives the instantaneous specific growth rate μ(t), with confidence bounds.
This lets us identify the exponential phase from the data instead of
choosing a window by eye, and it propagates measurement uncertainty into μ.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What does each variable describe?" tocIgnore>

- **ΔOD** is the total increase in optical density, which reflects the final
  biomass the culture reaches.
- **t<sub>d</sub>** is the time needed for the population to double at that
  rate, giving an intuitive measure of generation time.
- **μ<sub>max</sub>** is the fastest rate at which the population increases
  during exponential growth, reflecting how quickly cells can replicate under
  the given conditions — which depends on nutrient availability, stress, and
  the metabolic state of the cells.
- **Lag time (λ)** is the period after inoculation during which cells adjust
  to the new environment, for example by inducing enzymes or repairing
  damage, before they begin dividing; a longer lag usually indicates greater
  stress or a less favorable condition.
- **AUC** integrates the growth curve over the whole measurement, so it
  captures overall growth across lag, exponential and plateau phases in one
  value, combining how fast cells grow with how long they keep growing.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="How are the calculations performed?" tocIgnore>

ΔOD is the difference between the maximum and initial OD over the
measurement: ΔOD = OD<sub>max</sub> − OD<sub>initial</sub>.

Maximum specific growth rate (μ<sub>max</sub>) is the peak of the first
derivative of the fitted ln(OD) curve. During exponential growth, the
population grows as OD<sub>t</sub> = OD<sub>0</sub>e<sup>μt</sup>. Taking the
natural log linearizes this to ln(OD<sub>t</sub>) = ln(OD<sub>0</sub>) + μt,
so the slope of ln(OD) against time is the specific growth rate μ, in units
of inverse time, with no conversion factor. The doubling time follows
directly: t<sub>d</sub> = ln(2) / μ<sub>max</sub>.

Lag time λ is the time at which the tangent to the fitted ln(OD) curve at
μ<sub>max</sub> intersects the baseline ln(OD). Area under the curve (AUC) is
the integral of the fitted ln(OD) curve over the whole measurement.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What do the † and ‡ quality flags mean in Table 2?" tocIgnore>

**† (marginal):** conditions with a ΔOD of 0.03 or less. At this level the
growth signal is close to the noise of the measurement, so the estimated
parameters are unreliable.

**‡ (not fitted):** conditions the package did not fit because they contained
three or fewer distinct optical densities.

A tight standard deviation describes the model's confidence in its fit. It
does not confirm that the fit reflects real growth, since the GP will return
a numeric fit for almost any input. For † rows in particular, the error bars
are best read alongside the ΔOD value rather than on their own.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Discussion" tocIgnore>

**Experiment 1: Pilot** ([Figure 1A](#fig-growth-a)). The first pilot established that medium choice
mattered more than any other variable we tested. mGAM supported growth in
every well, reaching 0.40 OD above background with a doubling time of
~46 min, the fastest condition we recorded. This fits its reputation as a
medium that supports a broad range of predominant gut
anaerobes[^growth-hirano]. WC also grew in every well, but its exponential
phase started eight hours later, it doubled half as fast, and it reached
only a third of mGAM's density. WC nonetheless resolved all three growth
phases cleanly, so we would favour it where kinetic readouts matter more
than final biomass. Unsupplemented BHI and MM did not grow within the 24 h
window (+0.010 OD, marginal), but as the second pilot day showed, that
result reflects the assay length rather than a permanent failure.

**Experiment 2: Pilot continuation** ([Figure 1B](#fig-growth-b)). The second day changed how we read MM.
Its lag of ≈ 7 h into Day 2 means growth began roughly 31 h after
inoculation, and once started it gained +0.048 OD. A standard 24 h assay
would have scored it as a failure, which is why every later plate was run
for 42 to 48 h. mGAM and WC were already at plateau by the end of Day 1 and
gained only 0.028 and 0.012 OD on Day 2, so they showed no further growth,
and BHI stayed flat, as in Experiment 1. The result also shows that the lag
values from the GP fit are measured from the start of each day's read, not
from inoculation, so lags must be compared with that in mind.

**Experiment 3: Strain dependency** ([Figure 1C](#fig-growth-c)). This was the first place where the
faecal isolate (RCC 1806) and the type strain (DSM 1447) behaved
differently. The isolate reached higher densities in every medium, in
places threefold, but it started consistently later: in mGAM the type
strain was already dividing at the first reading (mean lag 0.6 h), while the
isolate waited 24.2 h. Most unexpectedly, the isolate grew in unsupplemented
BHI, where the type strain did not, on the same plate read at the same time.
Since RCC 1806 is recorded as a faecal isolate, the explanation for this can
only be an assumption without further experiments. The LB-based minimal
medium gave a divided result: two of its three DSM 1447 wells grew and one
did not, so the pooled +0.024 OD describes neither. Its median OD falls just
below the 0.03 OD marking line, while its mean falls just above. That is the
whole distance between reading the condition as dead and reading it as alive,
so we treat it as unreliable. Its 41.9 ± 1.1 h lag is
the latest in the study and fits the slow, inconsistent behaviour this
medium showed throughout.

**Experiment 4: Reducing lag times** ([Figure 1D](#fig-growth-d)). On <Link to="/human-practices#laurie-comstock">Laurie Comstock's</Link> advice that most
of an overnight culture was dead by the time it is subcultured, we inoculated
both strains in the morning from colonies on a fresh plate. This cut the
isolate's mGAM lag from 24.2 h to 6.0 h, a four-fold reduction, while the
type strain went from essentially no lag to 2.5 ± 1.9 h, an interval wide
enough that the change is not resolvable. Both then doubled quickly, at
0.83 ± 0.01 h and 0.69 ± 0.01 h. The preculture change also reversed which
strain grew in Wilkins–Chalgren and in the LB-based minimal medium relative
to Experiment 2: here DSM 1447 grew in both and RCC 1806 in neither, where
previously it had been the other way round. Preculture state is therefore
the largest uncontrolled variable in this study, and growth parameters are
not comparable between plates without it. This plate was read at 630 nm
rather than 600 nm, so its absolute densities cannot be compared with other
experiments, although rates and lags are unaffected since each curve shares
its wavelength and blank. The run was interrupted at 20 h, and every well
jumped by up to 0.2 OD across three readings before returning to its
previous trend, so those readings were excluded. Roughly half the BHI wells
then showed a sustained rise starting at the gap, most likely a residual
artefact of the disturbance rather than growth. BHI run at increased cell
density supported no growth at all, which supports our conclusion that
supplemented BHI is needed to sustain _P. vulgatus_.

**Experiment 5: Full factorial study** ([Figure 1E](#fig-growth-e)). Soluble starch, vitamin K and a pH
adjustment to 7.2 were tested in all eight combinations of a 2³ factorial in
the LB-based minimal medium, with six wells per condition over 48 h. Vitamin
K was chosen because many _Bacteroides_ and related species cannot
synthesise it[^growth-gibbons], and the pH adjustment was needed because the
medium as made came out at pH 8.7–8.8. All three supplements together gave
the largest gain (+0.047 OD), vitamin K with pH gave +0.044, and starch with
pH gave +0.032, while vitamin K alone gained only +0.014. The pH adjustment
appeared in all three working combinations and in none of the failures, so
it appears necessary but not sufficient. Mean doubling times in the working
combinations were long (9.5–18.4 h) with lags spanning up to ±10 h, so no
ordering among them is meaningful.

We found that the base medium was contaminated. Filter sterilisation appears
to have worked, since uninoculated wells drifted by only 0.002 OD over 48 h,
but compounds the contaminant had already consumed cannot be filtered out.
This is the most likely reason the unsupplemented medium, which had
supported growth in every earlier experiment, grew in none of its wells
here. A permutation test put the largest likelihood ratios where the
largest ΔOD differences were, but the strongest comparison (full combination
against starch plus vitamin K) gave p = 0.043 before and 0.151 after
Benjamini–Hochberg correction across seven comparisons[^growth-bh]. With six
wells per condition the design is underpowered for this
question[^growth-tonner], so the result reflects a limitation of the
experiment rather than evidence that the supplements do not differ.

</ExpandableText>

#### Conclusions and future directions

These four cycles were as much a learning curve for EMPOWER as for
_P. vulgatus_. They showed that the assay must match the organism, since a
standard 24 h read would have wrongly excluded the minimal medium, and that
preculture is a controllable variable that shifted lags more than strain
choice did. The factorial work points toward a minimal medium that can
support _P. vulgatus_ growth with pH adjustment and vitamin K, but it does
not yet match mGAM and still needs dose–response fine-tuning, so mGAM
remains our reference medium. Open questions include a clean re-run of the
unsupplemented control on uncontaminated base medium, a larger experiment to
rank the supplements, and a direct test of supplemented BHI, which the
literature suggests is a credible alternative[^growth-gibbons].

The MIC assay is part of the toolkit development, which has the aim of facilitating research with _Phocaeicola vulgatus_. The goal of the MIC assay is to determine which antibiotics are most effective at inhibiting growth of _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447/ATCC8482 and fecal strain _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806. In the therapeutics pipeline, the most effective antibiotic would serve to inhibit _P. vulgatus_ carrying the CRISPR construct in the case of uncontrolled growth.

<ExpandableText title="Do you know what EUCAST is?" tocIgnore>

EUCAST stands for the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing. It is a scientific committee and jointly organized by the European Society for Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), and European national breakpoint committees.

What do they do? They set different rules for testing antibiotics. They decide the exact dose needed to kill different bacteria and fungi, helping doctors choose the right medicine to treat infections successfully.

</ExpandableText>

#### Major takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>
  <Takeaway>
    Tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and metronidazole were found to be the most
    effective antibiotics against <em>P. vulgatus</em> DSM1447 and RCC1806.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    4 mg/L: both strains sit at the EUCAST breakpoint of 4 mg/L, where a
    therapeutic is likely to succeed. The only compound here with usable
    clinical guidance.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Ampicillin and erythromycin: both vary widely between strains, and EUCAST

  </Takeaway>

<ExpandableText title="Materials and methods" tocIgnore>

_P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 was obtained from the research group Molecular Biotechnology of Plants and
Micro-organisms from KU Leuven by <Link to="/human-practices#matthew-herdman">Matt Herdman</Link>. The clinical isolate _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806 was obtained from
the Raes lab of Bioinformatics and (Eco-)Systems Biology by <Link to="/human-practices#ruben-vazquez-uribe">Ruben Vazquez-Uribe</Link>. Ampicillin, chloramphenicol,
erythromycin, metronidazole, kanamycin, spectinomycin, tetracycline and gentamicin were tested, together with
simvastatin — not an antibiotic, but reported to inhibit the growth of _P. vulgatus_
DSM1447[^mic-escalante]. Starting concentrations for the two-fold dilution series in mGAM were taken from
literature[^mic-Hayashi], and each assay was run anaerobically in triplicate.

OD630 was read after 48 h at 30°C and expressed as % inhibition = 1 − (OD<sub>well</sub> −
OD<sub>blank</sub>) / (OD<sub>positive control</sub> − OD<sub>blank</sub>), with the inoculated antibiotic
solvent as blank and inoculated mGAM as positive control. The MIC of a repeat is the lowest concentration
inhibiting visible growth; the IC<sub>90%</sub>, the concentration giving 90% growth inhibition, is reported
as the quantitative measure[^mic-veiga] and taken as the consensus across repeats.

Full plate layouts, dilution ranges and run-by-run records are in the <Link to="/notebook">Notebook</Link>
(entries MIC-01 to MIC-04).

</ExpandableText>

The antibiotic with the lowest IC<sub>90%</sub> is the most effective and can be used in the pipeline to inhibit growth if needed. Tetracycline was most effective, followed by chloramphenicol and metronidazole (Figure 1). Ampicillin and erythromycin showed more variable results between the two strains. Gentamicin and kanamycin did not inhibit _P. vulgatus_ within the tested range, except for RCC1806, which had a IC<sub>90%</sub> of 256 mg/L for gentamicin.

While tetracycline had the lowest IC<sub>90%</sub>, the range spanned several orders of two for _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447, and there was only one replicate for RCC1806. In some dilutions, wells with a lower antibiotic concentration reached above 90% inhibition, while wells with the highest concentration in a dilution sometimes had values of 88% or 89%. These replicates, including the replicates of tetracycline, were discarded at a cutoff of 90%.

Repeating the analysis at an 85% cutoff gives a more nuanced figure (Figure 2), which justifies the choice of tetracycline, metronidazole, and chloramphenicol as the most effective antibiotics for both strains.

<ExpandableText title="Ampicillin" tocIgnore>

As a β-lactam, and with β-lactamases reported in _P. vulgatus_, resistance was expected[^mic-mahmood][^mic-parker][^mic-sabunwala]. Instead, the MIC is 4 mg/L (1–32) for DSM1447 and 64 mg/L for RCC1806, in line with earlier reports (Table 1). Possibly, β-lactamases are absent or less actively expressed in these strains than in the resistant clinical isolates. EUCAST advises against ampicillin for anaerobic bacteria when the MIC is higher than 0.5 mg/L[^mic-eucast-nobp], which together with the variability between strains, rules out the use of ampicillin as an effective antibiotic against _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 and RCC1806.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Metronidazole" tocIgnore>

For both strains the IC<sub>90%</sub> sits at the breakpoint of 4 mg/L, indicating that it is most likely an effective therapeutical antibiotic[^mic-eucast-bp]. A recent meta-analysis confirms resistance against metronidazole remains low[^mic-markowska].

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and erythromycin" tocIgnore>

No breakpoints or other clinical guidance are available for _P. vulgatus_, so these rest on the measured values alone.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Gentamicin and kanamycin" tocIgnore>

Uptake depends on the proton motive force, and so indirectly on oxygen, so no adverse effect on growth was predicted[^mic-krause][^mic-webster]. This is indeed confirmed by our results.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Conclusion" tocIgnore>

Tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and metronidazole were found to be the most effective antibiotics against
_P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 and RCC1806. As the EUCAST specifically recommends the use of metronidazole when the MIC

Our goal is to target the most active bile salt hydrolase (BSH) via CRISPRi. This CRISPR construct of course needs to be present in _P. vulgatus_. One method to achieve this is by conjugation: _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir would conjugate the CRISPR plasmid to _P. vulgatus_, which would then integrate into the genome and express the CRISPR system. Conjugation titer was determined, and is part of the toolkit development.

To investigate this part of the therapeutics pipeline, multiple conjugations were performed:

<Callout title="Important: the selection plates may have let false positives through" kind="warning" raised>

Our MIC data, which became available after these cycles were run, exposed an error in the design. The plates carried 25 µg/mL erythromycin, while RCC1806 has an IC<sub>90%</sub> of 256 µg/mL and DSM1447 has an IC<sub>90%</sub> of 4 µg/mL. The RCC1806 strain can therefore grow on the selection plates without ever receiving the pNBU2 plasmid. Colonies counted as transconjugants may include false positives.

Antibiotic susceptibility was not tested for the other strains, but it is possible that they have a similar IC<sub>90%</sub>. To verify the presence of the plasmid, and as such the presence of false positive colonies, integration should have been checked. We did not do this, as it is a suicide plasmid. We assumed _P. vulgatus_ could not grow on mGAM + Gm + Ery without containing the plasmid, which the MIC data now show to be a wrong assumption for RCC1806, and possibly for the other clinical isolates too.

**Recommendation for future researchers:** check integration, or at least test erythromycin susceptibility for all _P. vulgatus_ strains and use a higher concentration than the MIC on the plates. The conjugation titers should thus be interpreted extremely cautiously, and the experiment repeated once erythromycin susceptibility has been determined for all strains.

</Callout>

### Screening BSH activity on bile salt plates

{/* Added with Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), 2026-10-21, from the team's document "Therapeutics - part 1 - section 3a - BS ASSAY"; typos fixed, the doc's mixed "15 constructs" wording made consistent with 14 BSH constructs plus the sfGFP control. */}

<div className="results-standfirst">
  A standardized method for pouring conjugated bile salt plates, and a halo
  screen of 14 BSH constructs on GUDCA and TUDCA.
</div>

The halo assay is the standard plate method for detecting bile salt hydrolase activity, used in the literature as the first screen before ninhydrin or LC–MS[^bsplate-jones]. The principle is simple. A construct expressing an active BSH deconjugates the bile salt in the surrounding agar. The freed bile acid is insoluble and precipitates, producing a visible white halo around the colony. The diameter of that halo gives an estimate of relative enzyme activity.

A white halo cannot be read on a cloudy plate. Before any construct could be scored we therefore had to establish how to pour even bile salt plates and confirm that _E. coli_ grows on them.

#### Major takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>
  <Takeaway>
    Salt first, then LB: the preparation standard was determined. Bile salt is
    added directly to the well, followed by LB with CaCl₂, to avoid
    precipitation.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    H₂O is the working solvent: DMSO is unusable, as it precipitates the medium
    and makes it unfit for plate pouring.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    100 mg/mL: TUDCA dissolves in water at this concentration, GUDCA does not.
    GUDCA would not dissolve through heating or vortexing and needed a few
    drops of 5 M NaOH.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    14 / 14: no bile salt is inhibitory. Every construct grew on every GUDCA
    and TUDCA plate up to 1.5 % (w/v), so the plating layout works and
    tolerance is not the limiting factor.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    No halo, no signal: BSH-expressing colonies were indistinguishable from the
    sfGFP control. The drops are dense and the surrounding agar stays turbid.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    LC–MS, a shift towards a quantitative method: the negative plate result is
    what motivated the LC–MS deconjugation assay.
  </Takeaway>
</KeyTakeaways>

<ExpandableText title="Assay development: solvent and pouring order" tocIgnore>

Two pilots established the plate preparation standard. Can bile salts be dissolved or poured at all, and could we reduce precipitation by designing a correct pouring method?

**Pouring order.** Bile salt is added directly to the well first, followed by LB with CaCl₂, which avoids the precipitation that made earlier plates unreadable.

**Solvent.** Water is the working solvent: DMSO precipitates the medium and is unusable.

#### The halo screen

The full screen followed: 14 BSH constructs and an sfGFP control, drop-plated on LB + CaCl₂ + Kan containing GUDCA or TUDCA at 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 % (w/v), with a no-bile-salt control plate. Colony formation was followed with a time-lapse colony imaging system.

BSH3 is the one construct of the fifteen not included here. Plate capacity was limited, and a no-BSH negative control was the more valuable use of the slot. sfGFP took the C1 position in the first construct set. BSH3 had already been plated extensively on GUDCA and TUDCA during the pilot runs, so it was the construct whose plating behavior was best characterized and therefore the least costly to leave out.

#### Discussion

Two things could be determined from our work:

1. **The plate method itself was established.** A standardized procedure now exists for pouring conjugated bile salt media.
2. **The halo assay returned nothing usable.** BSH constructs did not differ from the control on any plate, at any concentration, with either substrate.

The negative result still gave information: the constructs grew, the layout worked, and the control behaved as a control should. What the assay could not do is resolve deconjugation against a turbid background. That sent the screen to a more sensitive, quantitative method: the LC–MS assay.

<ExpandableText title="Learn more: why the halo did not appear" tocIgnore>

The halo assay assumes that an active BSH deconjugates the bile salt in the surrounding agar, precipitating the free bile acid into a visible white ring. The method is well established and was used in many papers as the plate screen preceding ninhydrin or LC–MS detection[^bsplate-jones], so the expectation was reasonable.

When we observe the imaging results closely, turbidity around the TUDCA plates is more visible than around the GUDCA plates in general, and no halo was seen around the control medium. But where halo or turbidity formations did appear on the salt media, comparing the BSH strains against the control strains leaves the picture essentially unchanged. The constructs produced no additional precipitation.

Several explanations are compatible with this. The drops are dense enough that any local clearing is masked by the colony itself. Or the free bile acids released from GUDCA and TUDCA (both ursodeoxycholic acid conjugates) may simply be too soluble at the pH used to precipitate visibly.

</ExpandableText>

#### Future prospects

We proceeded with the next experiments (BSA-06 and BSA-07) as the plate assay did not meet our expectations. For a more precise and quantitative analysis we applied LC–MS to liquid cultures of the bile salt media with BSH constructs. See the <Link to="/protocols#halo-assay-failed-protocol">halo assay protocol</Link> and the <Link to="/engineering">Engineering page</Link> for the full cycle.

### Fitness cost of the BSH constructs in _E. coli_ DH5α

{/* Added with Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), 2026-10-21, from the team's document "Therapeutics - part 1 - section 3b - Fitness cost analysis"; typos fixed, wording otherwise kept. Figures fit-fig1 to fit-fig3 are to be uploaded via the uploads tool. */}

<div className="results-standfirst">
  A droplet-based screen of all fifteen BSH constructs against an sfGFP control,
  asking which inserts are costly enough to be lost without selection.
</div>

This fitness assay asks whether the BSH constructs in _E. coli_ DH5α differ in the burden they impose on the host, meaning the toxicity or metabolic cost of carrying the plasmid and expressing the gene product[^fit-snoeck]. Costly constructs are outcompeted or lost without selective pressure, which makes them poor candidates to carry forward[^fit-sanmillan]. The readout is colony-forming ability from a fixed starting OD. Because all constructs share the pNBU2 backbone, the assay measures burden _relative to one another_, not an absolute fitness cost.

#### Major takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>
  <Takeaway>
    Countable window established: from a culture at OD<sub>600</sub> 0.4–0.5,
    dilutions of 10<sup>−6</sup>–10<sup>−7</sup> give well-separated single
    colonies, while 10<sup>−1</sup> to 10<sup>−4</sup> are confluent lawns.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    The most costly constructs: BSH1, BSH3 and BSH10 plate below the sfGFP
    control at 10<sup>−5</sup>, as determined in the second run.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    BSH5, BSH6 and BSH7 exceed the countable ceiling wherever sfGFP is
    readable, at the same dilution on the same plate, and are thus the cheapest
    constructs.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Burden does not follow activity: both ends of the range mix active and
    inactive hydrolases, so catalytic output is not what drives the cost.
  </Takeaway>
</KeyTakeaways>

#### Finding the countable dilution

Before any construct could be compared, the dilution giving countable colonies had to be determined. A ten-fold series from a culture at OD<sub>600</sub> ≈ 0.5 should cross the countable range somewhere between 10<sup>−5</sup> and 10<sup>−7</sup>, giving separated single colonies rather than a lawn.

BSH6 and BSH9 were used for this run as the two constructs furthest apart phylogenetically. All fifteen constructs had been confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The reasoning behind picking two rather than one: if a construct with a heavy burden plates an order of magnitude lower, its most diluted drop will be empty whatever dilution is chosen, so the window has to hold for constructs at both ends of the range.

#### Conclusions and future perspectives

The inserts differ in what they cost the host, which is consistent with burden being gene-specific[^fit-snoeck] and with plasmid cost lowering competitiveness in the absence of selection[^fit-sanmillan]. For the therapeutic pipeline this matters directly: BSH1, BSH3 and BSH10 are the inserts a population would shed first.

**Burden does not track deconjugation activity.** The obvious hypothesis, that producing a working hydrolase is what costs the host, does not align with the data. Among the four costliest inserts, two are active hydrolases (BSH1, BSH3) and two show no measurable deconjugation (BSH8, BSH10). Among the cheapest, two are active (BSH5, BSH6) and five are not. If catalytic output were the source of the burden, the inactive constructs should all plate well, and they do not.

### Bile salt deconjugation by 15 bile salt hydrolases

The most surprising outcome here is how few of the fifteen BSHs are active at all:

- Only BSH1, BSH3 and BSH15 deconjugate both GUDCA and TUDCA.
- BSH5 and BSH6 are effectively GUDCA-specific.
- The majority of the panel shows no activity above the sfGFP control under either condition.

#### Conclusions

Substrate preference is clearly uneven, with GUDCA being deconjugated far more than TUDCA by every active gene, which is consistent with the glycine-conjugated bile salt being the more accessible substrate for these enzymes.

Furthermore, since no correlation was found between the binding affinities and the deconjugation activity, we expect that there are other factors that play a role in BSH activity, such as catalytic rate or differences in expression level.

#### Future prospects

All strains that were used in the assay have an intact catalytic cysteine, so the next step is to find out whether the differences come from how much enzyme is made or how well it works. Measuring BSH protein levels in each strain would show if the inactive genes were simply expressed at low levels, or whether their enzymes are genuinely poor at converting these two substrates.

Our approach involves isolating _P. vulgatus_ from a patient's fecal sample,

balanced immune and metabolic modulation.
