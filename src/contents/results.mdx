<ExpandableText title="Strain selection" tocIgnore>

For our experiments, we selected six _Phocaeicola vulgatus_ strains spanning different isolation sources. Most strains were from fecal origin, to assess variation within fecal isolates on a strain level. The type strain is used as a reference strain, as it is the only member with a complete genome, documented _bsh_ loci and established genetic tools. This way, _bsh_ gene content from the other strains can be compared against it to reveal whether conserved loci are suitable CRISPR replacement targets or whether strain-variable BSH paralogs exist.

Five strains are of fecal origin: the laboratory strain DSM1447, also known as ATCC8482, together with the gut isolates DSM108222 (H4_2), DSM108228 (H5_1), DSM108234 (H6_5) and RCC1806. RCC1806 is a fecal isolate obtained from the laboratory of <Link to="/attributions">Jeroen Raes</Link>. DSM3289 (WAL1887) was included as a clinical isolate recovered from a retroperitoneal abscess. All DSM strains were obtained from [DSMZ](https://www.dsmz.de/)[^strain-dsmz].

### Growth curves and medium comparison

<div className="results-intro-row">
<div className="results-intro-aside">

<div className="results-intro-text">

We asked which of four anaerobic media makes _P. vulgatus_ grow fastest and
most reproducibly, and which of mGAM's own ingredients can explain the gap.
Growth of each condition was characterized by fitting its optical density
(OD) data over time, which yields parameters describing how quickly cells
grow, how long they take to begin dividing, and how much biomass they
accumulate. These parameters allow comparison of the _P. vulgatus_ type
strain (DSM 1447) and the fecal isolate (RCC 1806) across four anaerobic
media: Brain–Heart Infusion (BHI), Wilkins–Chalgren (WC)[^growth-wc], mGAM[^growth-hirano],
and an LB-based minimal medium (MM). Four iterations were run in 96-well
plates, reading optical density every 30 minutes for 24 to 48 h and fitting
by Gaussian-process regression with the DGrowthR
package[^growth-dgrowthr][^growth-keitel2025].

</div>

#### Major takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>
  <Takeaway>
    mGAM gave the fastest, most reproducible growth, followed by
    WC with a more sigmoidal curve.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Unsupplemented BHI supported no growth at all, agreeing with the
    literature: hemin and vitamin K are required by many _Bacteroides_ and
    relatives[^growth-gibbons].
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    BHI growth was strain dependent: the isolate grew in unsupplemented BHI
    while the type strain did not.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Our MM requires pH adjustment, and the enrichment with
    vitamin K and starch only contributed noticeably once the pH had been
    corrected.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    A standardized cell preparation protocol is required to obtain the
    exponential-phase cells needed for growth comparisons.
  </Takeaway>
</KeyTakeaways>

The main differences in medium components create differences in growth
performance. The following components are the most prominent differences
between the media, discussed here to work out which are responsible for the
higher-performing media.

<ExpandableText title="Hemin" tocIgnore>

WC contains 5 mg L⁻¹ hemin, whereas mGAM and MM contain 10 mg L⁻¹; BHI
contains no added hemin. Hemin supplies haem for haem-containing proteins
involved in electron transport. Hemin supplementation has been shown to
improve _B. vulgatus_ growth[^growth-lee]. Its importance may relate to
haem-dependent electron transport: in the related species _B. fragilis_,
hemin was required for functional fumarate reduction and substantially
increased growth rate and biomass yield[^growth-macy].

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Vitamin K compounds" tocIgnore>

mGAM contains vitamin K₁ (5 mg L⁻¹), whereas WC contains menadione
(0.5 mg L⁻¹); neither BHI nor MM contains an explicit vitamin K supplement.
These compounds are relevant because vitamin K₂ helps bacteria transfer
electrons to produce energy. However, _B. vulgatus_ can produce its own
vitamin K₂, and adding certain vitamin K compounds did not improve its growth
under the conditions tested[^growth-fenn]. Vitamin K₁ itself was not tested
there, so whether it improves growth in our medium needed to be determined
experimentally.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Starch" tocIgnore>

Only mGAM contains soluble starch, at 5 g L⁻¹. Starch has been shown to
support rapid growth and high bacterial yields in tested _B. vulgatus_
strains, which also produced more starch-digesting enzymes when grown on
starch than on glucose[^growth-mccarthy]. Starch may therefore improve growth
in mGAM by providing an additional source of carbon and energy.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Glucose and complex nutrients" tocIgnore>

Glucose concentrations are 2.0 g L⁻¹ in BHI, 1.0 in WC, 0.5 in mGAM and 0.2 in
MM. Glucose provides carbon and energy for _B. vulgatus_, which has been
successfully cultivated in a defined medium using glucose as its main carbon
source[^growth-keitel2024]. These differences may therefore affect growth,
but more glucose does not mean better growth — other nutrients must also be
considered, particularly starch in mGAM, which _B. vulgatus_ can use as an
additional carbon source[^growth-mccarthy].

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Bicarbonate" tocIgnore>

Only MM contains added sodium bicarbonate, at 1.25 g L⁻¹. Bicarbonate
provides a source of dissolved CO₂ and helps resist changes in pH, which is
relevant because _P. vulgatus_ requires CO₂ for growth: changing CO₂
availability has been shown to affect bacterial growth and organic acid
production[^growth-keitel2023]. Bicarbonate may therefore influence growth,
although CO₂ supplied through the cultivation atmosphere must also be
considered.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Reducing agents" tocIgnore>

mGAM contains cysteine hydrochloride and sodium thioglycollate, WC was
supplemented with cysteine hydrochloride, and MM contains cysteine. These
reducing agents help remove dissolved oxygen and maintain conditions suitable
for anaerobic growth. Increased oxygen exposure has been shown to impair
_P. vulgatus_ growth, with cysteine suggested to reduce oxygen-related
damage[^growth-keitel2023]. Differences in reducing agents may therefore
affect growth by changing the bacteria's exposure to oxygen.

</ExpandableText>

Because these media differ in several components simultaneously, superior
growth in one medium cannot be attributed to a single ingredient. Subsequent
experiments therefore varied starch, vitamin K₁ and pH individually to assess
their contributions.

mGAM was the clear winner: it grew fastest and reached a final OD of about
0.41, against only 0.13 in WC. It is hard to attribute this to a single
component. However, starch (both amylose and amylopectin) is a likely factor,
since it is known to give rapid growth and high yields in _B. vulgatus_[^growth-mccarthy].
Hemin may also have helped[^growth-lee], and the reducing agents would have
kept conditions properly anaerobic, though this experiment cannot separate out
what each ingredient contributed. Even so, mGAM's advantage was big enough to

<ExpandableText title="Why Gaussian process (GP) regression?" tocIgnore>

Bacterial growth curves have a lag phase, an exponential phase, and a
plateau. Parametric models such as the logistic impose a fixed shape on all
three, which can bias the estimated rate when the real curve deviates from
that shape. A GP makes no assumption about the functional form: it instead
fits a smooth curve to the data and returns a posterior distribution at every
time point. Because we fit to ln(OD), the derivative of the fitted curve
gives the instantaneous specific growth rate μ(t), with confidence bounds.
This lets us identify the exponential phase from the data instead of
choosing a window by eye, and it propagates measurement uncertainty into μ.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What does each variable describe?" tocIgnore>

- **ΔOD** is the total increase in optical density, which reflects the final
  biomass the culture reaches.
- **t<sub>d</sub>** is the time needed for the population to double at that
  rate, giving an intuitive measure of generation time.
- **μ<sub>max</sub>** is the fastest rate at which the population increases
  during exponential growth, reflecting how quickly cells can replicate under
  the given conditions — which depends on nutrient availability, stress, and
  the metabolic state of the cells.
- **Lag time (λ)** is the period after inoculation during which cells adjust
  to the new environment, for example by inducing enzymes or repairing
  damage, before they begin dividing; a longer lag usually indicates greater
  stress or a less favorable condition.
- **AUC** integrates the growth curve over the whole measurement, so it
  captures overall growth across lag, exponential and plateau phases in one
  value, combining how fast cells grow with how long they keep growing.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="How are the calculations performed?" tocIgnore>

ΔOD is the difference between the maximum and initial OD over the
measurement: ΔOD = OD<sub>max</sub> − OD<sub>initial</sub>.

Maximum specific growth rate (μ<sub>max</sub>) is the peak of the first
derivative of the fitted ln(OD) curve. During exponential growth, the
population grows as OD<sub>t</sub> = OD<sub>0</sub>e<sup>μt</sup>. Taking the
natural log linearizes this to ln(OD<sub>t</sub>) = ln(OD<sub>0</sub>) + μt,
so the slope of ln(OD) against time is the specific growth rate μ, in units
of inverse time, with no conversion factor. The doubling time follows
directly: t<sub>d</sub> = ln(2) / μ<sub>max</sub>.

Lag time λ is the time at which the tangent to the fitted ln(OD) curve at
μ<sub>max</sub> intersects the baseline ln(OD). Area under the curve (AUC) is
the integral of the fitted ln(OD) curve over the whole measurement.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What do the † and ‡ quality flags mean in Table 2?" tocIgnore>

**† (marginal):** conditions with a ΔOD of 0.03 or less. At this level the
growth signal is close to the noise of the measurement, so the estimated
parameters are unreliable.

**‡ (not fitted):** conditions the package did not fit because they contained
three or fewer distinct optical densities.

A tight standard deviation describes the model's confidence in its fit. It
does not confirm that the fit reflects real growth, since the GP will return
a numeric fit for almost any input. For † rows in particular, the error bars
are best read alongside the ΔOD value rather than on their own.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Discussion" tocIgnore>

**Experiment 1: Pilot.** The first pilot established that medium choice
mattered more than any other variable we tested. mGAM supported growth in
every well, reaching 0.40 OD above background with a doubling time of
~46 min, the fastest condition we recorded. This fits its reputation as a
medium that supports a broad range of predominant gut
anaerobes[^growth-hirano]. WC also grew in every well, but its exponential
phase started eight hours later, it doubled half as fast, and it reached
only a third of mGAM's density. WC nonetheless resolved all three growth
phases cleanly, so we would favour it where kinetic readouts matter more
than final biomass. Unsupplemented BHI and MM did not grow within the 24 h
window (+0.010 OD, marginal), but as the second pilot day showed, that
result reflects the assay length rather than a permanent failure.

**Experiment 2: Pilot continuation.** The second day changed how we read MM.
Its lag of ≈ 7 h into Day 2 means growth began roughly 31 h after
inoculation, and once started it gained +0.048 OD. A standard 24 h assay
would have scored it as a failure, which is why every later plate was run
for 42 to 48 h. mGAM and WC were already at plateau by the end of Day 1 and
gained only 0.028 and 0.012 OD on Day 2, so they showed no further growth,
and BHI stayed flat, as in Experiment 1. The result also shows that the lag
values from the GP fit are measured from the start of each day's read, not
from inoculation, so lags must be compared with that in mind.

**Experiment 3: Strain dependency.** This was the first place where the
faecal isolate (RCC 1806) and the type strain (DSM 1447) behaved
differently. The isolate reached higher densities in every medium, in
places threefold, but it started consistently later: in mGAM the type
strain was already dividing at the first reading (mean lag 0.6 h), while the
isolate waited 24.2 h. Most unexpectedly, the isolate grew in unsupplemented
BHI, where the type strain did not, on the same plate read at the same time.
Since RCC 1806 is recorded as a faecal isolate, the explanation for this can
only be an assumption without further experiments. The LB-based minimal
medium gave a divided result: two of its three DSM 1447 wells grew and one
did not, so the pooled +0.024 OD describes neither. Its median OD falls just
below the 0.03 OD marking line, while its mean falls just above. That is the
whole distance between reading the condition as dead and reading it as alive,
so we treat it as unreliable. Its 41.9 ± 1.1 h lag is
the latest in the study and fits the slow, inconsistent behaviour this
medium showed throughout.

**Experiment 4: Reducing lag times.** On <Link to="/human-practices#laurie-comstock">Laurie Comstock's</Link> advice that most
of an overnight culture was dead by the time it is subcultured, we inoculated
both strains in the morning from colonies on a fresh plate. This cut the
isolate's mGAM lag from 24.2 h to 6.0 h, a four-fold reduction, while the
type strain went from essentially no lag to 2.5 ± 1.9 h, an interval wide
enough that the change is not resolvable. Both then doubled quickly, at
0.83 ± 0.01 h and 0.69 ± 0.01 h. The preculture change also reversed which
strain grew in Wilkins–Chalgren and in the LB-based minimal medium relative
to Experiment 2: here DSM 1447 grew in both and RCC 1806 in neither, where
previously it had been the other way round. Preculture state is therefore
the largest uncontrolled variable in this study, and growth parameters are
not comparable between plates without it. This plate was read at 630 nm
rather than 600 nm, so its absolute densities cannot be compared with other
experiments, although rates and lags are unaffected since each curve shares
its wavelength and blank. The run was interrupted at 20 h, and every well
jumped by up to 0.2 OD across three readings before returning to its
previous trend, so those readings were excluded. Roughly half the BHI wells
then showed a sustained rise starting at the gap, most likely a residual
artefact of the disturbance rather than growth. BHI run at increased cell
density supported no growth at all, which supports our conclusion that
supplemented BHI is needed to sustain _P. vulgatus_.

**Experiment 5: Full factorial study.** Soluble starch, vitamin K and a pH
adjustment to 7.2 were tested in all eight combinations of a 2³ factorial in
the LB-based minimal medium, with six wells per condition over 48 h. Vitamin
K was chosen because many _Bacteroides_ and related species cannot
synthesise it[^growth-gibbons], and the pH adjustment was needed because the
medium as made came out at pH 8.7–8.8. All three supplements together gave
the largest gain (+0.047 OD), vitamin K with pH gave +0.044, and starch with
pH gave +0.032, while vitamin K alone gained only +0.014. The pH adjustment
appeared in all three working combinations and in none of the failures, so
it appears necessary but not sufficient. Mean doubling times in the working
combinations were long (9.5–18.4 h) with lags spanning up to ±10 h, so no
ordering among them is meaningful.

We found that the base medium was contaminated. Filter sterilisation appears
to have worked, since uninoculated wells drifted by only 0.002 OD over 48 h,
but compounds the contaminant had already consumed cannot be filtered out.
This is the most likely reason the unsupplemented medium, which had
supported growth in every earlier experiment, grew in none of its wells
here. A permutation test put the largest likelihood ratios where the
largest ΔOD differences were, but the strongest comparison (full combination
against starch plus vitamin K) gave p = 0.043 before and 0.151 after
Benjamini–Hochberg correction across seven comparisons[^growth-bh]. With six
wells per condition the design is underpowered for this
question[^growth-tonner], so the result reflects a limitation of the
experiment rather than evidence that the supplements do not differ.

</ExpandableText>

#### Conclusions and future directions

These four cycles were as much a learning curve for EMPOWER as for
_P. vulgatus_. They showed that the assay must match the organism, since a
standard 24 h read would have wrongly excluded the minimal medium, and that
preculture is a controllable variable that shifted lags more than strain
choice did. The factorial work points toward a minimal medium that can
support _P. vulgatus_ growth with pH adjustment and vitamin K, but it does
not yet match mGAM and still needs dose–response fine-tuning, so mGAM
remains our reference medium. Open questions include a clean re-run of the
unsupplemented control on uncontaminated base medium, a larger experiment to
rank the supplements, and a direct test of supplemented BHI, which the
literature suggests is a credible alternative[^growth-gibbons].

</ResultSection>

### Antibiotic susceptibility of _P. vulgatus_

{/* Updated with Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), 2026-10-21, to match the team's document "Toolkit - section 4 - MIC"; kept the existing heading so the /results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus links keep working; methods and conclusion from the earlier write-up were kept as dropdowns. */}

<div className="results-standfirst">
  Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) for nine antibiotics across two{" "}
  <em>P. vulgatus</em> strains, to find reliable selection markers we could use
  downstream in our project.
</div>

The MIC assay is part of the toolkit development, which has the aim of facilitating research with _Phocaeicola vulgatus_. The goal of the MIC assay is to determine which antibiotics are most effective at inhibiting growth of _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447/ATCC8482 and fecal strain _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806. In the therapeutics pipeline, the most effective antibiotic would serve to inhibit _P. vulgatus_ carrying the CRISPR construct in the case of uncontrolled growth.

<ExpandableText title="Do you know what EUCAST is?" tocIgnore>

EUCAST stands for the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing. It is a scientific committee and jointly organized by the European Society for Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), and European national breakpoint committees.

What do they do? They set different rules for testing antibiotics. They decide the exact dose needed to kill different bacteria and fungi, helping doctors choose the right medicine to treat infections successfully.

</ExpandableText>

#### Major takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>
  <Takeaway>
    Tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and metronidazole were found to be the most
    effective antibiotics against <em>P. vulgatus</em> DSM1447 and RCC1806.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    4 mg/L: both strains sit at the EUCAST breakpoint of 4 mg/L, where a
    therapeutic is likely to succeed. The only compound here with usable
    clinical guidance.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Ampicillin and erythromycin: both vary widely between strains, and EUCAST
    advises against ampicillin for anaerobes above 0.5 mg/L.
  </Takeaway>
</KeyTakeaways>

<ExpandableText title="Materials and methods" tocIgnore>

_P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 was obtained from the research group Molecular Biotechnology of Plants and
Micro-organisms from KU Leuven by <Link to="/human-practices#matthew-herdman">Matt Herdman</Link>. The clinical isolate _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806 was obtained from
the Raes lab of Bioinformatics and (Eco-)Systems Biology by <Link to="/human-practices#ruben-vazquez-uribe">Ruben Vazquez-Uribe</Link>. Ampicillin, chloramphenicol,
erythromycin, metronidazole, kanamycin, spectinomycin, tetracycline and gentamicin were tested, together with
simvastatin — not an antibiotic, but reported to inhibit the growth of _P. vulgatus_
DSM1447[^mic-escalante]. Starting concentrations for the two-fold dilution series in mGAM were taken from
literature[^mic-Hayashi], and each assay was run anaerobically in triplicate.

OD630 was read after 48 h at 30°C and expressed as % inhibition = 1 − (OD<sub>well</sub> −
OD<sub>blank</sub>) / (OD<sub>positive control</sub> − OD<sub>blank</sub>), with the inoculated antibiotic
solvent as blank and inoculated mGAM as positive control. The MIC of a repeat is the lowest concentration
inhibiting visible growth; the IC<sub>90%</sub>, the concentration giving 90% growth inhibition, is reported
as the quantitative measure[^mic-veiga] and taken as the consensus across repeats.

Full plate layouts, dilution ranges and run-by-run records are in the <Link to="/notebook">Notebook</Link>
(entries MIC-01 to MIC-04).

</ExpandableText>

The antibiotic with the lowest IC<sub>90%</sub> is the most effective and can be used in the pipeline to inhibit growth if needed. Tetracycline was most effective, followed by chloramphenicol and metronidazole (Figure 1). Ampicillin and erythromycin showed more variable results between the two strains. Gentamicin and kanamycin did not inhibit _P. vulgatus_ within the tested range, except for RCC1806, which had a IC<sub>90%</sub> of 256 mg/L for gentamicin.

While tetracycline had the lowest IC<sub>90%</sub>, the range spanned several orders of two for _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447, and there was only one replicate for RCC1806. In some dilutions, wells with a lower antibiotic concentration reached above 90% inhibition, while wells with the highest concentration in a dilution sometimes had values of 88% or 89%. These replicates, including the replicates of tetracycline, were discarded at a cutoff of 90%.

Repeating the analysis at an 85% cutoff gives a more nuanced figure (Figure 2), which justifies the choice of tetracycline, metronidazole, and chloramphenicol as the most effective antibiotics for both strains.

<ExpandableText title="Ampicillin" tocIgnore>

As a β-lactam, and with β-lactamases reported in _P. vulgatus_, resistance was expected[^mic-mahmood][^mic-parker][^mic-sabunwala]. Instead, the MIC is 4 mg/L (1–32) for DSM1447 and 64 mg/L for RCC1806, in line with earlier reports (Table 1). Possibly, β-lactamases are absent or less actively expressed in these strains than in the resistant clinical isolates. EUCAST advises against ampicillin for anaerobic bacteria when the MIC is higher than 0.5 mg/L[^mic-eucast-nobp], which together with the variability between strains, rules out the use of ampicillin as an effective antibiotic against _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 and RCC1806.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Metronidazole" tocIgnore>

For both strains the IC<sub>90%</sub> sits at the breakpoint of 4 mg/L, indicating that it is most likely an effective therapeutical antibiotic[^mic-eucast-bp]. A recent meta-analysis confirms resistance against metronidazole remains low[^mic-markowska].

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and erythromycin" tocIgnore>

No breakpoints or other clinical guidance are available for _P. vulgatus_, so these rest on the measured values alone.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Gentamicin and kanamycin" tocIgnore>

Uptake depends on the proton motive force, and so indirectly on oxygen, so no adverse effect on growth was predicted[^mic-krause][^mic-webster]. This is indeed confirmed by our results.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Conclusion" tocIgnore>

Tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and metronidazole were found to be the most effective antibiotics against
_P. vulgatus_ DSM1447 and RCC1806. As the EUCAST specifically recommends the use of metronidazole when the MIC
is below 4 mg/L, this antibiotic is most recommended when working with _P. vulgatus_. Ampicillin and
erythromycin are less encouraged, given the variability between the strains and a negative advice against
ampicillin by EUCAST.

</ExpandableText>

### Conjugation into _P. vulgatus_

{/* Updated with Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), 2026-10-21, to match the team's document "Toolkit - part 5 - Conjugation analysis"; figures renumbered 1-5 in reading order, cycles moved into dropdowns. */}

<div className="results-standfirst">
  Testing conjugation efficiency into six <em>P. vulgatus</em> strains in three
  DBTL cycles.
</div>

Our goal is to target the most active bile salt hydrolase (BSH) via CRISPRi. This CRISPR construct of course needs to be present in _P. vulgatus_. One method to achieve this is by conjugation: _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir would conjugate the CRISPR plasmid to _P. vulgatus_, which would then integrate into the genome and express the CRISPR system. Conjugation efficiency was determined, and is part of the toolkit development.

<ExpandableText title="The three conjugations" tocIgnore>

To investigate this part of the therapeutics pipeline, multiple conjugations were performed:

- _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir to _P. vulgatus_ DSM1447: a preliminary conjugation to assess efficiency of a conjugation into _P. vulgatus_ and, if needed, to optimize it.
- _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir to _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806, DSM108222, 3289, 108234, and 108228 to assess efficiency of a conjugation into clinical (fecal) isolates.
- _E. coli_ S17-1 λpir to _P. vulgatus_ RCC1806, DSM1447, 108222, 3289, 108234, 108228 to assess efficiency of a conjugation into clinical isolates and to compare with DSM1447 conjugation efficiency.

As the CRISPR construct was not yet developed, pNBU2 was selected as the plasmid for conjugation. Three conjugation cycles were run: a pilot into the type strain, an extension to the clinical isolates, and a final conjugation for all six strains.

</ExpandableText>

<Callout title="Important: the selection plates may have let false positives through" kind="warning" raised>

Our MIC data, which became available after these cycles were run, exposed an error in the design. The plates carried 25 µg/mL erythromycin, while RCC1806 has an IC<sub>90%</sub> of 256 µg/mL and DSM1447 has an IC<sub>90%</sub> of 4 µg/mL. The RCC1806 strain can therefore grow on the selection plates without ever receiving the pNBU2 plasmid. Colonies counted as transconjugants may include false positives.

Antibiotic susceptibility was not tested for the other strains, but it is possible that they have a similar IC<sub>90%</sub>. To verify the presence of the plasmid, and as such the presence of false positive colonies, integration should have been checked. We did not do this, as it is a suicide plasmid. We assumed _P. vulgatus_ could not grow on mGAM + Gm + Ery without containing the plasmid, which the MIC data now show to be a wrong assumption for RCC1806, and possibly for the other clinical isolates too.

**Recommendation for future researchers:** check integration, or at least test erythromycin susceptibility for all _P. vulgatus_ strains and use a higher concentration than the MIC on the plates. The conjugation efficiencies should thus be interpreted extremely cautiously, and the experiment repeated once erythromycin susceptibility has been determined for all strains.

</Callout>

### CRISPR construct design

{/* Updated with Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), 2026-10-21, to match the team's document "Therapeutics - part 2 - section 6 - CRISPR"; sections moved into dropdowns; the four takeaways are summaries of the document's own statements. */}

{/* The team's own "Construct Planning FINAL" document, transferred as written; only typography was adjusted (species names in italics, R6K γ ori, en dashes) and the document's own headings were kept as the structure below. */}
{/* TODO(team): the document's unresolved comments are not carried onto the page — the open ones are flagged with TODO comments where they apply. */}
{/* Edited with Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), 2026-10-18: dropped the standfirst teaser and the "Executive summary" label (team feedback: duplicated the paragraph below without adding anything) and moved the plasmid map down to sit beside its own legend instead of beside this text. */}

Our approach involves isolating _P. vulgatus_ from a patient's fecal sample,
CRISPR engineering it *in vitro*, and administering it to the same patient. The
CRISPR system, introduced through conjugation, uses the guide RNA (gRNA) to
target the native BSH and replaces it with a BSH homolog of different substrate
preference, so that the bile salts with the strongest TGR5 affinity remain. The
homologous recombination system of _P. vulgatus_ can use the designed homology
arms flanking our BSH of interest to insert it into the genome after double
strand break induction. The intended effect is a restored bile salt pool for
balanced immune and metabolic modulation.

#### Major takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>
  <Takeaway>
    The gRNA guides Cas9 to the native BSH, which is replaced by a homolog of
    different substrate preference, so the bile salts with the strongest TGR5
    affinity remain.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    The plasmid backbone does not integrate, so it can be cured, leaving a
    scarless gene replacement.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Four validation steps are planned: junction PCR, Cas9 expression, BSH
    activity and stability. The system has not yet been tested *in vivo*.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>
    Still open: the BSH variant, 4–5 gRNA spacers, the homology arms and the
    strength of the native HDR system.
  </Takeaway>
</KeyTakeaways>
