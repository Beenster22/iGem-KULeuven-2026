### 1.1 Virulence analysis

*Phocaeicola vulgatus* is a non-model organism that is sparsely represented in curated databases.
Before starting our laboratory work we were determined to check this organism for resistances and
possible interference with our lab work. We performed all these analyses on publicly available
web-based tools, using *P. vulgatus* strain ATCC 8482 as the reference genome, retrieved from NCBI
(assembly GCF_000012825.1 / ASM1282v1; RefSeq NC_009614.1; taxid 435590). We chose this strain
because it is one of the few *P. vulgatus* isolates with a well-annotated genome (approximately 87%
annotated — above the completeness NCBI recommends for a reliable reference).

This choice was of great importance for the interpretation of our results. Homology-based searches
can only be as good as their reference databases. For an organism as understudied as *P. vulgatus*,
the absence of a hit may reflect the genuine absence of a gene, or simply a missing database entry.

**Virulence factors and pathogenic potential.** Our first analysis checked whether any virulence
factors are present in the *P. vulgatus* genome. We used VirulenceFinder[^vir-1], a web tool
maintained by the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE, TU Denmark), which searches a curated
database of experimentally characterised virulence genes. No virulence genes in the CGE database
were detected. We then cross-checked this with PathogenFinder2[^pf2-1], an online server and
standalone program that uses a protein language model to predict human pathogenic capacity from the
genome, rather than matching individual genes. Because it does not depend on a reference database, it
complements the homology-based approach and reports both the proteins driving the classification and
an embedding that locates the genome within the Pathogenic Bacteria Landscape. The results were
consistent with VirulenceFinder: both indicate that *P. vulgatus* ATCC 8482 is classified as
non-pathogenic to humans.

**Prophage content.** Lastly, we used PHASTEST[^phastest-1], which identified a single prophage
region of approximately 24.6 kb (positions 3,608,142–3,632,769) containing 19 coding sequences (CDS).
The region scored "questionable" on PHASTEST's three-tier scale (intact &gt;90, questionable 70–90,
incomplete &lt;70) — a score determined by the identity percentage of the matched phage proteins, the
number of phage features found in the query sequence, and the size of the prophage sequence. The
region contains multiple genes with strong similarity to bacteriophage genes: a head-and-packaging
module (major capsid protein, prohead protease, portal protein and both terminase subunits), an
integrase and recombinase for lysogeny, and a replication module. However, no recognizable tail or
lysis genes were detected. Their absence, combined with the relatively small number of CDS, suggests
that the region represents an incomplete or degraded prophage that is unlikely to be inducible, and we
therefore do not expect it to interfere with our engineering work.

### 1.2 Promoter expression analysis

Unlike the model organism *E. coli*, Bacteroidaceae initiate transcription from promoters with −7 and
−33 consensus elements[^prom-mastro], and *P. vulgatus* lacks validated native promoters. Our
approach was then to borrow from the closest well-characterized relatives: *B. thetaiotaomicron* and
*B. fragilis*. A short literature survey yielded twenty-four promoters validated within *Bacteroides*
spp. (Table 2), from which we selected 5, keeping time constraints in mind. Each was combined with 4
reporters to rigorously test expression strength, resulting in a library of twenty constructs (<Link to="/results#a-characterized-promoter-library-for-p-vulgatus">results</Link>; see the
<Link to="/protocols#promoter-reporter-screen">Promoter-Reporter Screen</Link> protocol for the assay itself).

Characterized inducible promoters do exist for *Bacteroides*, but validating an inducible system means
testing the promoter, the regulator and the inducer dose-response, which is a substantially larger
experiment than testing a constitutive promoter. Due to time constraints it was reasonable to
prioritize constitutive promoters. The five selected promoters — P<sub>BT4001</sub>,
P<sub>BT1311</sub>, P<sub>PAM3</sub>, P<sub>cfxA</sub> and P<sub>BfP1E6</sub> — span different classes,
namely native housekeeping, engineered, native resistance-gene and phage-derived origins. This
diversity was chosen to reduce the loss of promoter activity on transfer and increase the chance of
finding a functional one.

The reporters we chose were superfolder GFP (sfGFP), KOFP-7, mCherry and NanoLuc. sfGFP and mCherry
are well-established fluorescent proteins that give single-cell resolution with good spectral
separation, but both require molecular oxygen for chromophore maturation. Since *P. vulgatus* is an
obligate anaerobe, this restricts them to endpoint measurements after exposure to oxygen. We therefore
also included an oxygen-independent reporter, KOFP-7, a flavin-binding fluorescent protein that
fluoresces under anaerobic conditions at both population and single-cell level[^reporter-1]. NanoLuc,
an engineered luciferase[^reporter-2], requires oxygen for the light-producing reaction itself but
needs no maturation step, so enzyme accumulated during anaerobic growth is fully active the moment
substrate is added. Together these allow promoter strength to be cross-validated by two independent
detection principles, and permit measurement under anaerobic conditions as well as aerobic conditions.

On top of finding promoters we set out to find an array of ribosome binding sites of various
strengths that were validated in *Bacteroides* spp. For this promoter expression analysis we decided
to use one RBS sequence, namely rpiL\*, which was described as strong and reliable in the
literature[^prom-mimee][^prom-taketani]. Holding it constant across all twenty constructs means that
any difference in output can be attributed to the promoter or the reporter rather than translation
efficiency.

### 1.3 Codon optimization tool

While planning our constructs in the wet lab we ran into a gap. There existed no online tool to
perform codon optimization or harmonization for *Bacteroides* species. Existing tools target
*E. coli*, yeast or mammalian hosts, and their defaults are a poor fit for an AT-rich anaerobe.
Since we needed one ourselves, we built it and made it freely available.

<SoftwareDownload
  name="Bacteroides codon optimization tool"
  path="software/codon-optimization-tool"
  description="Python command-line tool for codon optimization and harmonization in Bacteroides. Runs on your own machine."
/>

#### Optimization versus harmonization

Codon optimization replaces each codon with the one most frequently used by the host, maximising
translation speed and therefore heterologous expression. What it does not account for is folding.
The genetic code is redundant, and evolution can make a rare codon functionally important by
introducing a pause which causes it to give the nascent chain a window in which to fold
co-translationally[^cod-1]. Remove those pauses and the protein may misfold, producing an enzyme
that is abundantly expressed but yields some inactive version. Codon harmonization preserves this
pattern instead of erasing it, mapping the relative codon usage of the native sequence onto the
host.

<ExpandableText title="1.4 Tool design">

#### Aim

Our goal was a tool that takes a coding sequence and returns a version that can realistically be
expressed in *Bacteroides*, without the user needing to know anything about the genus' quirks. We
designed a tool that can handle both optimization and harmonization, since the choice between them
depends on whether the user cares more about yield or about folding.

#### Species and codon usage data

Codon usage tables are taken from the Kazusa Codon Usage Database and vendored locally, so the tool
works offline and gives reproducible results independent of database availability. Two reference
genomes are supported: *B. thetaiotaomicron* VPI-5482, *B. fragilis* NCTC 9343.

#### Two modes

In optimize mode, every position is reassigned to the most frequent synonymous codon for the
*Bacteroides* species. In harmonize mode, the rank of each codon in the source organism is mapped
onto the codon of equivalent rank in *Bacteroides*, preserving the relative fast/slow pattern of the
native sequence. We chose rank-based mapping over frequency-percentile mapping because ranks stay
meaningful between organisms with very different overall codon bias, which matters here given the
AT-rich *Bacteroides* genome. Harmonization works with a source-organism table supplied by the user.
If none is given, the tool falls back to self-referential ranking within the input CDS itself and
warns when the source table is too small to rank reliably.

#### Constraints

A sequence that is optimal on paper is still useless if it cannot be cloned or translated. Four
constraint modules run over every candidate sequence:

- **Restriction sites:** a fixed panel of nine enzymes covering common Golden Gate and BioBrick
  cloning cutters (BsaI, PaqCI, SapI, BbsI, EcoRI, NotI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI), checked on both strands.
  The panel can be replaced by the user.
- **GC content:** an upper ceiling of 70%, applied to the whole sequence and to a sliding 30 bp
  window. There is no lower limit, because low GC is the normal state for this genus rather than a
  defect. The ceiling mainly catches GC-rich, hairpin-prone or hard-to-synthesise regions, including
  those that substitutions made for other constraints could introduce.
- **Repeats:** direct and inverted repeats of at least 11 bp, with fuzzy matching allowing up to two
  mismatches, since near-identical repeats can cause recombination during cloning and synthesis
  failures at many manufacturers (see Limitations below).
- **5′ folding:** strong secondary structure around the start codon blocks translation initiation.
  Instead of a fixed ΔG cutoff, we compute the minimum free energy of the first 15 codons (plus an
  optional 5′ UTR) with ViennaRNA and convert it into a z-score against 100 shuffled versions of the
  same sequence. A z-score below −2.0 is flagged.

#### Repair

Violations are not simply reported: the tool attempts to fix them using synonymous substitutions, in
strict priority order. A candidate substitution is accepted only if it resolves its target violation
without introducing a higher-priority one, which prevents the repair loop from trading one problem
for a worse one.

#### Output

The tool returns the resulting sequence as a FASTA file together with a diagnostics report. The
report summarises each constraint (violations found, resolved and remaining), lists the unresolved
violations with their positions, and gives an overall status. A JSON version of the report also
contains the full log of every substitution made. A sequence that cannot be made fully compliant is
still returned, marked as partially compliant, with the remaining problems made explicit, so the
user can decide whether to accept it or redesign it.

#### Usage

The tool is a Python command-line application. A typical run looks like this:

```
python cli.py --mode optimize --species thetaiotaomicron_VPI-5482 input.fasta -o output.fasta
```

The input is a FASTA file containing in-frame coding sequences. Harmonization additionally accepts
a Kazusa-format codon table for the source organism.

#### Limitations

- **Repeat threshold.** The repeat check flags direct and inverted repeats of at least 11 bp with up
  to two mismatches. This fuzzy rule is too strict for full-length genes: in a sequence of several
  hundred base pairs, near-identical 11 bp stretches arise by chance. In our test on a 717 bp GFP
  coding sequence, the tool removed all exact repeats and restriction sites and kept GC content and
  5′ folding within limits, but 139 fuzzy repeats remained. The tool therefore reports such sequences
  as partially compliant, with the remaining repeats listed, rather than claiming full compliance.
  These residual repeats are a consequence of the threshold, not necessarily a sign that the sequence
  is unusable — but we have not yet benchmarked whether they cause problems in practice. Users should
  treat the unresolved-repeat list as advisory.
- **No experimental validation.** The tool has been tested on synthetic sequences and a GFP coding
  sequence, checking that the protein is unchanged and that constraints are met. We have not yet
  tested whether its output expresses better in *Bacteroides* than the original sequence, and the
  default thresholds (GC ceiling, repeat length, folding cutoff, enzyme panel) have not been
  benchmarked against expression data.
- **Codon usage data.** The tables are averages over all annotated genes of a genome, so highly
  expressed genes may follow a different codon usage pattern. Only two *Bacteroides* strains are
  currently supported. The quality of harmonization depends on the source organism's table, and the
  fallback of ranking codons within the input sequence is noisy for short genes.
- **What is not modelled.** Codons are selected using usage frequency and the four constraints only.
  The tool does not consider mRNA stability, promoter or ribosome-binding-site context, internal
  Shine–Dalgarno-like sequences, or other *Bacteroides*-specific regulatory motifs. Folding is
  evaluated only at the 5′ end of the transcript, and the 5′ folding check is stochastic, so results
  can vary slightly between runs.
- **Greedy repair.** Substitutions are made one at a time in priority order, so the tool can stop with
  a constraint unresolved even when a different combination of substitutions might have worked.
  "Unresolved" therefore means "the tool could not fix it", not "it cannot be fixed".
- **Scope and speed.** The tool accepts in-frame coding sequences in plain DNA letters, not whole
  plasmids, introns or ambiguous bases. A 717 bp gene takes around half a minute; longer genes will
  take longer.

</ExpandableText>

### 2.1 Antibiotic selection

Establishing what *P. vulgatus* is susceptible to served two purposes: it gives us a containment
route, and it establishes a clinical fallback should an engineered strain ever need to be cleared
from a patient. Here too we encountered the database-sparsity problem, as the resources did not
agree.

ResFinder[^resf-1], another open-source bioinformatics tool and curated database maintained by CGE
(TU Denmark), is used to identify acquired antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and chromosomal
resistance mutations. It returned no determinants for tetracyclines or fluoroquinolones. CARD's RGI
(Resistance Gene Identifier), a bioinformatic tool used to predict antibiotic resistomes[^card-1],
returned two hits when run on the same genome:

The two tools do not contradict each other, as they work on different principles. ResFinder reports
only genes that were acquired from other bacteria and have been shown experimentally to cause
resistance, while RGI also reports chromosomal homologues of resistance genes, which are often
present in a genome without making the organism actually resistant. The Bacterial and Viral
Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC)[^bvbrc-1], a web-based information system and research
platform that aggregates curated genotype and phenotype data across published studies, reports no
resistance marker for ATCC 8482 under its AMR phenotypes. However, for a strain with little
published characterization, this may equally reflect an absence of submitted phenotype data rather
than confirmed susceptibility.

No further genotype prediction can resolve which reading is correct, and this is the gap our
<Link to="/protocols#minimum-inhibitory-concentration-testing">MIC assay</Link> can experimentally close. The
contested classes gave us a starting point for designing the panel: tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones
and glycopeptides each had to be measured to settle the disagreement between the tools. We then
extended the panel well beyond these, to agents that would realistically be used clinically against a
Gram-negative anaerobe. This was done so that the resulting profile could support a genuine clinical
fallback rather than only resolving the in silico conflict.

Together these analyses gave us a foundation to rely on before starting experiments: a <Link to="/dry-lab#1-1-virulence-analysis">safety profile</Link>
for a strain with little published characterization, a set of genetic parts with a realistic prospect
of functioning in *P. vulgatus*, and an empirically grounded susceptibility profile.

### 2.2 BSH–bile salt interaction using AutoDock Vina

Sequence-level evidence alone is not enough to call a gene a bile salt hydrolase (BSH). BSHs belong
to the cholylglycine hydrolase family, which also contains enzymes with different substrate
preferences, so homology searches can return proteins that share the fold and the catalytic
machinery without acting on bile salts. To address this before committing wet lab capacity, we asked
a structural question: does each candidate actually have a binding site capable of accommodating a
bile salt?

Bile salt hydrolases (BSHs) are N-terminal nucleophile hydrolases (Ntn) and their catalytic
nucleophile is a cysteine at position 2, exposed by autocatalytic cleavage[^bsh-1][^bsh-2]. A
candidate without a cysteine at this position does not match the canonical BSH catalytic
architecture. We therefore checked this residue in all 15 predicted structures. Eleven candidates
carry Cys2 and were treated as canonical. Four — *Bacteroides dorei*, *Parabacteroides merdae*,
*Phocaeicola plebeius* and *Phocaeicola vulgatus* 1 — have no cysteine at position 2. Instead they
carry an N-terminal extension with the profile of a signal peptide (a hydrophobic stretch followed by
a polar region), and the first cysteine downstream of it sits at position 20–26. For these four we
took that cysteine as the putative catalytic residue, assuming the extension is cleaved to expose it.
The assignment is inferred from sequence features and has not been confirmed experimentally, so we
flag these candidates as divergent from canonical BSHs regardless of their docking results.

We predicted the structure of each of the 15 candidates with AlphaFold3 and docked them against a
panel of bile salts using AutoDock Vina[^vina-1][^vina-2], run in Google Colab so that the entire
pipeline is reproducible by anyone without local installation or licensed software. The docking
search box was centered on the catalytic cysteine of each protein. For each protein-ligand pair we
recorded the binding affinity of the top-scoring pose and inspected the pose relative to the
catalytic residue, since a favorable score in the wrong pocket says nothing useful.

Docking scores are a ranking tool, not a measurement. Vina affinities do not translate into kinetic
parameters, and the scoring function was not developed with steroid-scaffold ligands in mind. We
therefore treated the results as a prioritization step rather than a prediction of activity:
candidates that dock poorly across the entire bile salt panel, that bind in a pose incompatible with
catalysis, or that lack the Cys2 nucleophile are deprioritized, while the ranking is used to
interpret the BSH assay and LC-MS results rather than to replace them.
