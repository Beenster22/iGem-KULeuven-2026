## <Medal tier="silver" /> Silver Medal

---

<div className="award-split">

<div className="award-split-text">

**Why we deserve it.**

- **Engineering success.** Over the course of this project we completed nine full Design–Build–Test–Learn cycles, several of which reached their intended engineering goal. What makes this record meaningful is the context in which it was achieved. We worked in *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, a non-model, strictly anaerobic gut commensal for which almost no standardised synthetic biology toolkit exists. Every cycle therefore had to be run inside an anaerobic workflow, where each Build–Test outcome is slower, more failure-prone, and far less efficient than in *E. coli*. Rather than treating this as a limitation, we made it part of the engineering problem. A substantial fraction of our cycles were spent developing the tools from the ground up, therefore multiple iterations were required to gain more data.

  Each iteration, successful or not, produced a documented Learn step that directly reshaped our next Design, and the failed cycles were as informative as the successful ones in telling us which assumptions borrowed from model organisms simply do not transfer. The result is not a single lucky construct, but a validated, reusable engineering pipeline for an organism that previously had none.
- **Human practices.** To ensure that EMPOWER was developed responsibly and remained responsive to the needs of both scientific and commercial communities, we drew on extensive background research, personal reflection, and meaningful engagement with a wide range of stakeholders. By actively seeking, critically evaluating, and implementing feedback throughout the project, we continuously refined our design, strategy, and future development pathway.

## Best Entrepreneurship <PrizeStar to="/entrepreneurship" label="Best Entrepreneurship" />

---

This award recognizes exceptional effort to build a business case and commercialize an iGEM project, whether new or carried over from a previous year.

**Why we deserve it.** We believe EMPOWER exemplifies outstanding entrepreneurship because we went beyond demonstrating scientific feasibility and systematically addressed the challenge of real-world implementation. By integrating <Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-1-problem-and-mission">patient needs</Link>, <Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-2-market-and-target-group">market realities</Link>, <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-6-regulatory-strategy">regulatory requirements</Link>, <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">intellectual property considerations</Link>, <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-5-value-chain-analysis">manufacturing constraints</Link>, and <Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-4-financial-strategy">funding strategies</Link> into our project design, we developed a clear and actionable <Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-2-roadmap">roadmap</Link> toward patient impact. Importantly, we mapped the development and translation pathway of a live biotherapeutic product, from <Link to="/engineering">strain engineering</Link> and <Link to="/safety-and-security">biocontainment</Link> through <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-5-value-chain-analysis">manufacturing</Link>, <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-6-regulatory-strategy">regulatory approval</Link>, <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-5-value-chain-analysis">clinical development</Link>, and <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">commercialization</Link>. Through extensive <Link to="/human-practices">stakeholder engagement</Link> and a thorough evaluation of the regulatory, manufacturing, clinical, and commercial requirements of live biotherapeutic products, we transformed EMPOWER from a promising synthetic biology concept into a credible venture with a realistic path to patient impact.

### Judging criteria

<ExpandableText title="Has the team discovered their first potential customers and identified any unmet needs not yet covered by other existing solutions?">

EMPOWER addresses an important unmet medical need: women with PMOS-related infertility who do not respond adequately to first-line treatment and who therefore face limited therapeutic alternatives (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-2-market-and-target-group">Market and Target Group</Link>). Throughout our patient interviews, women highlighted a common frustration: current treatments often address individual symptoms without addressing the underlying biology that may connect them (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-1-problem-and-mission">Problem and Mission</Link>).

Unlike existing therapies, which primarily manage hormonal imbalances, insulin resistance, or other individual manifestations of PMOS, EMPOWER explores a novel therapeutic approach targeting the gut microbiome–bile acid–IL-22 axis. By investigating this potential upstream mechanism, we aim to address the biological pathway that is associated with inflammation, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism through a single, microbiome-based strategy (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-1-unique-value-proposition">Unique Value Proposition</Link>).

Our competitive analysis identified a potentially differentiated position for EMPOWER within the current PMOS treatment landscape, as we found no existing or emerging competitors specifically targeting this mechanism (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#competition">Competition</Link>). Beyond its scientific rationale, our market assessment estimates a serviceable addressable market of 863,518–3,108,666 women across the EU-27, with an initial serviceable obtainable market of 265,970–957,492 women (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-2-market-and-target-group">Market and Target Group</Link>).

Crucially, our approach is grounded in patient and stakeholder insights. We translated these insights into a patient journey map and a concept test, which supported the potential fit of EMPOWER within the treatment pathway for women who have not benefited sufficiently from first-line therapy (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-4-minimal-viable-product">MVP</Link>). Together, these findings provide a foundation for further developing and evaluating EMPOWER as a differentiated, patient-informed approach to PMOS-related infertility.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Has the team shown that their solution is possible, scalable and inventive?">

EMPOWER has taken concrete steps towards demonstrating the feasibility of its solution by engineering *Phocaeicola vulgatus* as a potential oral live biotherapeutic designed to modulate bile-acid signaling in the gut (<Link to="/engineering">Engineering</Link>). The team developed a synthetic biology toolkit for *P. vulgatus* and performed early experimental work on the proposed therapeutic mechanism, creating the technical foundation needed for further validation (<Link to="/engineering">Engineering</Link>; <Link to="/contribution">Contribution</Link>).

What sets EMPOWER apart is its ambition to move beyond symptom management. By targeting the gut microbiome–bile acid–IL-22 axis, we are investigating a potential upstream mechanism linking inflammation, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism. This approach opens a new therapeutic avenue in women's health, with a platform that could potentially be adapted to other microbiome-related conditions in the future (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-1-unique-value-proposition">Unique Value Proposition</Link>; <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">IP and Commercialisation Strategy</Link>).

Importantly, we have considered not only how EMPOWER could work, but also how it could be manufactured, delivered, and brought to patients. We assessed the key challenges of anaerobic GMP manufacturing, formulation, storage, bacterial viability, and genetic stability, and identified potential mitigation strategies to guide further development (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-3-risks-and-mitigation">Risks and Mitigation</Link>). We also compared two distinct development pathways: an off-the-shelf allogeneic product and a personalized autologous approach. By estimating the development and manufacturing costs of both models, we evaluated their practical and financial implications rather than assuming a single route would be feasible (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-4-financial-strategy">Financial Strategy</Link>).

Finally, we developed an out-licensing strategy that connects EMPOWER's scientific potential with a realistic route towards clinical application. Under this model, an experienced industrial partner would take responsibility for large-scale manufacturing, later-stage clinical trials, regulatory approval, and commercialization, while building on the technical foundation and development strategy established by our team (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-5-value-chain-analysis">Value Chain</Link>).

EMPOWER brings together synthetic biology, a differentiated therapeutic concept, manufacturing feasibility, financial planning, and a defined commercialization pathway. We have worked to address the challenges that extend beyond the laboratory, giving our project a stronger foundation for future development and demonstrating how an iGEM project can connect scientific innovation with a potential real-world healthcare solution.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Has the team presented logical product development plans with realistic milestones, timelines, resources and risks?">

EMPOWER has developed a long-term roadmap connecting the project's current stage at the 2026 iGEM Grand Jamboree to a targeted licensing agreement around 2033 (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-2-roadmap">Roadmap</Link>). This roadmap outlines the scientific, technical, regulatory, and financial milestones required to advance our proposed live biotherapeutic towards clinical application.

The initial phase focuses on further strain engineering, validation of the proposed therapeutic mechanism, preclinical safety studies, and continued development of the *Phocaeicola vulgatus* genetic toolkit. Subsequent steps include GMP-compatible manufacturing, regulatory preparation, and a Phase I clinical trial to assess initial safety in humans (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-2-roadmap">Roadmap</Link>; <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-5-value-chain-analysis">Value Chain</Link>).

Following these early development milestones, EMPOWER plans to out-license the technology to an established pharmaceutical or live biotherapeutics company. This partner would lead later-stage clinical trials, manufacturing scale-up, regulatory approval, and commercialization (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">IP and Commercialisation Strategy</Link>).

We estimate that USD 12–23 million would be required to reach Phase I, with staged funding through grants, university-linked funding, angel investors, and venture capital (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-4-financial-strategy">Financial Strategy</Link>). Our roadmap also addresses key risks, including bacterial translocation, antimicrobial resistance, genetic instability, manufacturing challenges, regulatory uncertainty, and reimbursement barriers. Mitigation strategies include preclinical safety studies, genome screening, antibiotic susceptibility testing, early FDA and EMA engagement, and licensing agreements with development obligations and clawback provisions (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-3-risks-and-mitigation">Risks and Mitigation</Link>).

By integrating scientific milestones, risk mitigation, financing, and a defined commercialization strategy, EMPOWER provides a structured pathway from an iGEM project towards potential clinical development.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Has the team outlined the skills, capabilities, and stakeholders required to be credible in developing their solution further?">

Through a structured internal skills assessment, EMPOWER identified the team's existing strengths and the expertise needed to advance the project towards clinical application (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-2-team-and-skills">Team and Skills</Link>). While we have a strong scientific and technical foundation, we also recognised gaps in regulatory affairs, intellectual property, business development, and technology transfer. To address these, we actively sought external expertise, explored entrepreneurship and innovation programmes, and reached out to specialists who could help us strengthen our development strategy (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-2-team-and-skills">Team and Skills</Link>).

Beyond identifying these gaps, we proactively contacted patients, clinicians, and industry experts to gather feedback and better understand the challenges of translating EMPOWER into a potential therapy. These interactions helped us refine our approach and connect our scientific ambitions with real-world needs (<Link to="/human-practices">Human Practices</Link>).

We also contacted a patent attorney to explore potential intellectual property protection and approached KU Leuven's technology transfer office to discuss opportunities for future collaboration (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">IP and Commercialisation Strategy</Link>). Looking ahead, we identified the key partners needed to advance EMPOWER, including academic research organisations, GMP manufacturers, clinical trial partners, university hospitals, investors, and pharmaceutical or microbiome companies (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-1-business-model-canvas">Business Model Canvas</Link>).

By actively seeking expertise, incorporating external feedback, and identifying the partnerships needed for future development, we are building the foundations to translate EMPOWER from a research project into a potential therapeutic product.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Has the team considered the positive and negative long-term impacts of their fully developed solution?">

If successfully developed, EMPOWER could introduce a new therapeutic approach for women with PMOS by targeting a potential upstream mechanism rather than focusing solely on individual symptoms (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#phase-4-long-term-impact">Long-term Impact</Link>). By investigating the gut microbiome–bile acid–IL-22 axis, the project could open new possibilities for improving reproductive health and potentially supporting long-term metabolic well-being (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-1-positive-impacts">Positive Impacts</Link>).

Beyond its potential therapeutic value, EMPOWER aims to contribute to greater recognition of women's health as an area requiring further research and investment (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-1-positive-impacts">Positive Impacts</Link>). The *P. vulgatus* genetic toolkit developed by the team could also support future microbiome research and provide a foundation for engineering other live biotherapeutic products (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-1-positive-impacts">Positive Impacts</Link>; <Link to="/contribution">Contribution</Link>).

However, developing a genetically modified living therapeutic also raises important safety and accessibility concerns. These include unpredictable effects in patients, bacterial shedding and potential environmental exposure, the possible transfer of engineered genetic material to other bacteria, and manufacturing costs that could limit affordability (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-2-negative-impacts">Negative Impacts</Link>).

We have considered these risks throughout our development strategy. Proposed mitigation measures include stepwise clinical evaluation, monitoring bacterial shedding and clearance, independent safety oversight, predefined stopping criteria, environmental risk assessment, early engagement with regulatory authorities, and consideration of reimbursement and access (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-2-negative-impacts">Negative Impacts</Link>; <Link to="/safety-and-security">Safety</Link>; <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-6-regulatory-strategy">Regulatory Strategy</Link>).

By considering both the potential benefits and the risks of our approach, EMPOWER aims to lay the groundwork for a scientifically responsible, accessible, and potentially impactful innovation in women's health.

</ExpandableText>

## Best Integrated Human Practices <PrizeStar to="/human-practices" label="Best Integrated Human Practices" />

---

**Why we deserve it.** We believe EMPOWER deserves Best Integrated Human Practices because our decision trail is documented, not asserted. We engaged six stakeholder groups — healthcare professionals, scientific and technical experts, biotech entrepreneurs and industry, patients, advocates and legislators, and the general public — and logged every piece of feedback in a <Link to="/human-practices#2-2-stakeholder-mapping">structured integration table</Link>, classified as active implementation, passive implementation, or not relevant. Of 18 logged entries, 14 were actively implemented and 3 passively, which shows how deeply external voices shaped EMPOWER rather than how often we simply listened. That influence runs from the lab bench to the business plan: Prof. Dr. Michel De Vos's critique of PMOS heterogeneity narrowed our <Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-2-market-and-target-group">target group</Link> to women who have not responded to first-line treatment; <Link to="/human-practices#weston-whitaker">Prof. Dr. Weston Whitaker</Link>'s ex-vivo strategy and Prof. Dr. Joleen Masschelein's specificity warning led us to abandon in-situ conjugation for an <Link to="/description#our-approach">ex-vivo design with auxotrophy as biocontainment</Link>; and <Link to="/human-practices#pedro-correa-de-sampaio">Dr. Pedro Correa de Sampaio</Link> and <Link to="/human-practices#sara-holland">Dr. Sara Holland</Link> reshaped our <Link to="/entrepreneurship">entrepreneurship</Link> and <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">IP strategy</Link>. <Link to="/human-practices#ricardo-azziz">Prof. Dr. Ricardo Azziz</Link>'s suggestion led us to run our own <Link to="/human-practices#pmos-and-sle-comparing-research-volume-against-disease-prevalence">PubMed analysis</Link> comparing PMOS and SLE publication volume across 43 European countries — an original contribution, not a citation of someone else's work. Beyond implementation, we treated patient and public involvement as a responsibility in its own right: every patient interview ran under ethical approval with informed consent in English, Dutch and French, our <Link to="/human-practices#4-public-survey">176-person public survey</Link> was independently reviewed, and we <Link to="/human-practices#6-the-funding-research-gap">raised awareness of the gender-based research and funding gap</Link> throughout the page rather than treating it as a footnote.

### Judging criteria

<ExpandableText title="What values (environmental, social, moral, scientific, or other) did you have in mind when designing your project?">

EMPOWER was built on four core values that we set before approaching a single stakeholder: integrated people-centered care, inclusivity, scientific integrity, and sustainability & entrepreneurship. Integrated people-centered care meant designing around the woman rather than around a single symptom, which is why we pursued an <Link to="/description#link-to-pmos-through-bile-acid-pool">upstream mechanism in the gut</Link> rather than a therapy that manages one manifestation of PMOS at a time. Inclusivity shaped whom we considered a stakeholder: we deliberately looked beyond academia to <Link to="/human-practices#5-patients">patients</Link>, <Link to="/human-practices#6-the-funding-research-gap">advocates and legislators</Link>, and the <Link to="/human-practices#4-public-survey">general public</Link>, and made an effort to recruit women of different phenotypes, age groups and countries of origin so our understanding of PMOS would not be a strictly Western one. Scientific integrity meant accepting failure and correction and creating a judgment-free environment where team members could learn, grow and stay curious. Sustainability and entrepreneurship meant treating translation as part of the responsibility: a therapy that never reaches a patient, or that only reaches women who can pay out of pocket, does not fulfill the need we identified, which is why we built a <Link to="/entrepreneurship#phase-4-long-term-impact">long-term impact</Link> assessment into our entrepreneurship plan from the start.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Which resources or communities did you consult to ensure those values are appropriate in the context of your project?">

We consulted six stakeholder groups and mapped each on an <Link to="/human-practices#2-2-stakeholder-mapping">influence-interest matrix</Link> to track how external voices moved the project over time. We spoke with <Link to="/human-practices#3-1-healthcare-professionals">five healthcare professionals</Link>, including gynecologists, fertility specialists and a functional dietitian, to understand where current PMOS care falls short. We consulted <Link to="/human-practices#3-2-scientific-technical-experts">scientific and technical experts</Link> across microbiology, synthetic biology, biocontainment, probiotics and engineered microbial therapeutics, several of whom supported us directly with strains, protocols and laboratory access. We approached <Link to="/human-practices#3-3-biotech-entrepreneurs-industry">eight people</Link> from biotech industry and entrepreneurship, from chief executives to a European Patent Attorney, on regulation, manufacturing, intellectual property and funding. We interviewed <Link to="/human-practices#5-patients">thirteen women living with PMOS</Link>, recruited through social media and community group chats under an ethically approved protocol with informed consent forms in English, Dutch and French. We surveyed <Link to="/human-practices#4-public-survey">176 members of the general public</Link> on attitudes toward genetically modified organisms in therapies for conditions with limited treatment options. Finally, we spoke with <Link to="/human-practices#6-the-funding-research-gap">four advocates and legislators</Link>, including PCOS Challenge, which represents roughly 60,000 members, and representatives working on gender-equality policy at the European Union level.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What evidence do you have to show that your project is responsible and good for the world?">

Our evidence is procedural as much as scientific. Every patient interview ran under an approved ethical review with informed consent forms in three languages (English, Dutch and French), and our <Link to="/human-practices#4-public-survey">public survey</Link> was independently approved before distribution. We documented every stakeholder interview in a <Link to="/human-practices#2-2-stakeholder-mapping">structured table</Link> and classified each piece of feedback as active implementation, passive implementation, or not relevant, so our decisions can be traced rather than asserted. That process visibly changed the project: we dropped the in-situ conjugation approach entirely after experts raised concerns about specificity, low transfer efficiency and fitness cost, and moved to an <Link to="/description#our-approach">ex-vivo gene replacement design</Link> with <Link to="/safety-and-security#risk-3-uncontrolled-colonization-excessive-persistence">auxotrophy as the biocontainment strategy</Link>. We narrowed our target population to the most severely affected subgroup, women who have not responded to first-line treatment, after clinicians explained how heterogeneous PMOS is (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-2-market-and-target-group">Market and Target Group</Link>). We also made a deliberate effort to raise awareness of the <Link to="/human-practices#6-the-funding-research-gap">gender-based research and funding gap</Link> throughout our IHP page, and we published our own limitations openly, including the skewed demographic of our survey sample. Finally, we assessed risk systematically across scientific, regulatory, financial, commercial and operational domains, using the live biotherapeutic product safety categories of Rouanet and colleagues, with named mitigations for translocation, antimicrobial resistance, virulence factors and metabolic interactions (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-3-risks-and-mitigation">Risks and Mitigation</Link>).

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What impact will your project have?">

PMOS affects roughly one in eight women and accounts for up to 80% of anovulatory infertility, yet it remains underresearched, underfunded, underdiagnosed and undertreated — women's health research receives an estimated 5% of global research and development funding (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-1-positive-impacts">Positive Impacts</Link>). EMPOWER's direct clinical impact, if it proves safe and effective, would be a first-in-class live biotherapeutic product that addresses inflammation, insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism through one shared upstream mechanism rather than symptom by symptom, which could support both reproductive outcomes and long-term metabolic health (<Link to="/description#our-hypothesis">Our Hypothesis</Link>). Beyond the clinical, our contribution to science is the <Link to="/engineering">synthetic biology toolkit</Link> we built for *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, one of the most abundant gut commensals and still poorly characterized, which other researchers can reuse for work unrelated to PMOS (<Link to="/contribution">Contribution</Link>). Socially, our <Link to="/science-communication">outreach, patient engagement and science communication</Link> aim to raise recognition of PMOS so that women seek care earlier and advocate for themselves. We also name the negative impacts honestly: the long-term safety of a living therapy, the environmental question of an engineered organism shed in stool, the theoretical spread of engineered genes, and the risk that an unreimbursed living therapy reaches only women who can pay (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#4-2-negative-impacts">Negative Impacts</Link>).

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Who are your proposed end users?">

Our primary end users are women living with PMOS, and we deliberately defined that group narrowly for the first application rather than claiming the whole population. Based on clinical feedback about the heterogeneity of the condition, we focus initially on Phenotype A, the most severely affected group, and within that on women who have not achieved successful outcomes with first-line treatment and who face limited remaining options (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#1-2-market-and-target-group">Market and Target Group</Link>). Our <Link to="/human-practices#5-patients">patient interviews</Link> showed that this is precisely the group that has exhausted the available treatment pathway after years without success, and many expressed a clear willingness to consider a novel approach that addresses their condition more comprehensively. Following clinical validation, EMPOWER could move earlier in the treatment pathway and serve a broader PMOS population. Alongside patients, there are two further user groups: clinicians, in particular gynecologists and fertility specialists, who would prescribe and monitor the therapy and whose evidence requirements shaped our <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-6-regulatory-strategy">development plan</Link>, and researchers, who are end users of a different kind through the <Link to="/engineering">genetic toolkit for *P. vulgatus*</Link> that we release for reuse.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="How do you envision others using your project?">

We see two distinct routes. The first is the therapeutic route, in which EMPOWER is used as an orally administered live biotherapeutic product prescribed by a clinician after first-line therapy has failed. We position it as an infrequently administered treatment with durable effect rather than a daily symptom-directed medication, reimbursed through national healthcare systems and insurers so out-of-pocket cost stays low (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-5-value-chain-analysis">Value Chain</Link>). We deliberately rejected the faster supplement route after discussion with <Link to="/human-practices#pedro-correa-de-sampaio">Dr. Pedro Correa de Sampaio</Link> and other industry experts, because marketing a therapeutic claim as a supplement would have compromised both regulatory honesty and patient safety. The second route is the platform route, and the one most immediately available to others: we built and released a <Link to="/engineering">synthetic biology toolkit</Link> for *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, documenting promoters, reporters and growth characteristics for a non-model organism that was previously difficult to engineer (<Link to="/contribution">Contribution</Link>). Other teams and laboratories can use that toolkit to engineer this chassis for conditions entirely unrelated to ours. We intentionally withhold only the specific bile salt hydrolase sequences at the core of the invention, following <Link to="/human-practices#sara-holland">Dr. Sara Holland</Link>'s advice on <Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-7-ip-and-commercialisation-strategy">IP strategy</Link>, while sharing the broader tools — and our own long-term plan is to extend the same approach to other non-model commensals in women's health.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="How would you implement your project in the real world?">

EMPOWER is developed as a live biotherapeutic product, meaning it is regulated as a medicine rather than a food supplement. In the United States it would require an Investigational New Drug application to the Food and Drug Administration before clinical testing, and in the European Union it would be regulated as a biological medicinal product meeting European Pharmacopoeia quality requirements (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#2-6-regulatory-strategy">Regulatory Strategy</Link>). Our <Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-2-roadmap">roadmap</Link> runs from the 2026 Grand Jamboree to a targeted licensing agreement in 2033, covering toolkit completion and strain optimization, patent filing and a spin-out with the KU Leuven Technology Transfer Office, preclinical studies on translocation, antibiotic susceptibility, virulence screening and metabolomic profiling, and a Phase I clinical trial. We do not intend to commercialize independently: after Phase I, we plan to out-license to a partner with women's health or microbiome expertise and the infrastructure for late-stage trials, manufacturing and market access, funded until then through non-dilutive grants followed by pre-seed, seed and Series A rounds (<Link to="/entrepreneurship#3-4-financial-strategy">Financial Strategy</Link>).

</ExpandableText>
