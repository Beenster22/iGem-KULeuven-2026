## MIC assay

*See also: <Link to="/results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus">MIC results</Link>, <Link to="/protocols#minimum-inhibitory-concentration-testing">MIC protocol</Link> and <Link to="/dry-lab#2-2-antibiotic-selection">dry-lab antibiotic selection</Link>.*

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="The literature ranges were wrong">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The goal for this experiment was to understand the susceptibility of P.vulgatus strains to range of antibiotics for downstream selection markers and biocontainment. Since no MIC data for our strains was available, we took starting concentrations for seven antibiotics from the literature on related Bacteroides species and tested DSM1447. We predicted two failures in advance: ampicillin not having an effect, because β-lactamases are reported in P. vulgatus, and kanamycin, because aminoglycoside uptake depends on the proton motive force and thus on oxygen, which an obligate anaerobe does not respire.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Seven antibiotics were used across three 96-well plates: kanamycin, metronidazole and spectinomycin on plate 1, ampicillin, chloramphenicol and erythromycin on plate 2, and tetracycline on plate 3. Each antibiotic occupied three adjacent columns, giving triplicate series, with the highest concentration in row A and a two-fold dilution at every step down to row G, each well containing 180 µL of mGAM. Row H was filled with inoculated mGAM as a positive growth control. The fourth column of each block carried the matching solvent (ddH₂O, DMSO, or ethanol at 50, 95 or 100%) diluted in the same two-fold steps, so that solvent toxicity could be told apart from antibiotic effect. The inoculum was prepared from an overnight culture of DSM1447 in mGAM, diluted into a single master plate, and transferred into each dilution plate with a 96-pin replicator so that every well started from the same cell density. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C and read at OD630 after 24 h and 48 h.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

After 48h of incubation time, none of our strains grew. For most compounds the entire series inhibited, so the ranges sat above the useful window, and no IC90 could be calculated. Kanamycin and spectinomycin did the opposite: growth continued at every concentration tested, up to 1024 µg/mL for kanamycin.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

This experiment gave us no MIC values but we learned that the literature ranges are several two-fold steps too high for our strains. Our predictions held for kanamycin, which is what gave us the approval for continuation with our plate layout. Ampicillin, in contrast, inhibited at every concentration tested, so the β-lactamase not dropped. Kanamycin and spectinomycin were dropped routine testing as those showed no inhibition at the highest concentrations.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="Lower ranges, and advice, giving a second strain and two new compounds">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

For our next iteration, we lowered every starting concentration by up to three two-fold steps. Two additions came out of conversations rather than the data: our instructor Matt supplied gentamicin, the aminoglycoside used clinically to clear gut microbes. Additionally, during a meeting with our PI Ruben on ,a PhD student showed us her graph on statins used to clear the gut microbiome. This led us to include simvastatin, reported to inhibit DSM1447 (Escalante et al., 2024). We also included the fecal strain RCC1806, since a toolkit for P. vulgatus should not rest on the type strain alone.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We used the same plate layout, replication and incubation time as for Cycle 1, so the ranges were the only change for DSM1447. RCC1806 was run at the original literature ranges, as its own window was still unknown.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Across the two strains:

- **Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and metronidazole** inhibited at the low end
- **Ampicillin and erythromycin** inhibited at concentrations that differed between the two strains
- **Gentamicin and kanamycin** had no effect on DSM1447, but gentamicin inhibited RCC1806 at the top of its series
- **Tetracycline** inhibited across its whole series, range was still too high

Including the second strain paid off immediately: ampicillin, erythromycin and gentamicin cannot be treated as having one species-level value. Three loose ends remained: tetracycline still had no endpoint, the ampicillin replicates disagreed, and RCC1806’s panel was incomplete.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 3" title="Closing the gap in the fecal strain">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

In MIC-02, RCC1806 had been run at the original literature ranges rather than the lowered ones used for DSM1447. For erythromycin, spectinomycin and simvastatin the strain grew at every concentration tested, so no inhibitory endpoint was reached. These three were therefore repeated at higher ranges, since the faecal strain had already proved the less susceptible of the two.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Our building strategy was identical to Cycle 2, but we included RCC1806 strain only. Initially, our first overnight cultures failed because the plates we used had sat in the anaerobic chamber over two weeks and had dried and cracked, so fresh plates were struck from cryostock and the run restarted.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

When we ran the experiment, we noticed that erythromycin inhibited RCC1806, but not at the same concentration that was used for DSM1447. This confirmed difference between the was visible here and not an artefact of the ranges used.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

The main takeaway is the fecal isolate does not behave like the type strain. Erythromycin inhibited RCC1806 at a different concentration that DSM1447. This pattern is consistent: RCC1806 is less susceptible of the two across several compounds. Susceptibility values from DSM1447 thus cannot be assumed to transfer to fecal or clinical isolates, and any concentration used for selection or containment has to be checked per strain. The cycle also gave us a practical in-lab rule: plates older than about a week in the chamber are not a reliable inoculum source, so working from recent plates became standard practice for the rest of the project.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 4" title="Settling the values, and discovering the cutoff mattered">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

By this point each antibiotics’ dilution series showed its inhibition point for both strains, so the aim shifted from locating endpoints to settling working values across the full panel, with enough replication to compare compounds statistically.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We did three replicate series per antibiotic with matched solvent controls, plates for DSM1447 alone and for both strains together, read at OD630 after 48 h of incubation. Endpoints were scored at 90% inhibition and compared within each strain by Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post hoc test.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

All antibiotics gave results within an acceptable range, which could be used for statistical calculations. The 90% cutoff on the other hand turned out to be suboptimal: wells at lower concentrations sometimes passed 90% while the highest concentration of the same series read 88–89%, which threw away most of the tetracycline data. Re-scoring at 85% kept those replicates and supported the same three compounds.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

What we learned from these iterations is that Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and metronidazole are the most effective of the panel. We chose metronidazole as the containment compound rather than simply the most potent one, because it is the only compound here with usable clinical guidance. Ampicillin was ruled out: it varies more than ten-fold between strains. The aminoglycoside result fed straight back into the conjugation work, where selection plates use 200 µg/mL gentamicin to counter-select E. coli. As P. vulgatus DSM1447 was insensitive to gentamicin, this allowed P. vulgatus to grow on the plates and kill E. coli at the same time, leaving us with only DSM1447 on our plates during conjugation.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

## Conjugation

*See also: the <Link to="/protocols#conjugation-of-pnbu2-plasmids-into-p-vulgatus">conjugation protocol</Link> and the <Link to="/results#conjugation-efficiency-testing">conjugation results</Link>.*

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Pilot run with the type strain">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

We needed to ensure successful conjugation so we could further engineer our therapeutic chassis.

First, we did a preliminary conjugation test to assess, and potentially optimize, the efficiency of conjugation from *E. coli* S17-1 λpir (a bacterial strain optimized for conjugation) to *P. vulgatus* DSM1447. As the protocol had already been successful for other *Bacteroides* species, we decided to do a pilot run without any changes to the initial protocol.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

To start building this pilot run, *E. coli* S17-1 λpir and *P. vulgatus* DSM1447 were grown until they both reached an OD of 0.2. They were mixed and plated out on rich mGAM medium. After a lawn formed, we plated out dilutions (10<sup>−1</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup>) on mGAM with gentamicin (Gm) and erythromycin (Ery). The reason for using these antibiotics is that *P. vulgatus* is naturally resistant to Gm and, when successfully conjugated, the pNBU2 plasmid will carry a resistance cassette against Ery. This way we ensure that only successfully conjugated strains can grow. As an additional selection marker, the pNBU2 plasmid carries a rhamnose-inducible promoter upstream of the reporter gene *kofp-7*.

In case of successful conjugation, we expected to see only resistant colonies expressing our *kofp-7*.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

For our first test run, we counted colonies on the plates to quantitatively determine the conjugation efficiency. Our testing confirmed high efficiency (4.11 × 10<sup>9</sup>), and single colonies were clearly visible only on the dilutions 10<sup>−5</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup>.

Our goal was to test all strains we had available and compare efficiency, in order to see which one would be the best for us to work with, but also to generate more data about these microorganisms. However, based on the success rate of the previous run, we decided to plate only the 10<sup>−5</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup> dilutions.

Besides changing the dilutions, the protocol was executed the same as in the previous cycle, only this time we tested everything in duplicate to ensure consistency of our data.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Including more than just our type strain showed that the conjugation efficiencies of all other strains were lower. This was possibly because we diluted our cells too much, or because conjugation simply failed. As a result, colonies were not counted.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

Multiple iterations of the conjugation assay led us to conclude that *P. vulgatus* DSM1447 is more efficient in conjugation than the clinical isolates, and this was determined quantitatively. However, the efficiencies of the other strains are possibly overestimated. By now, MIC data was also available, and highlighted an error in the design of all previous cycles: the concentration of erythromycin on the plates was 25 µg/mL.

*P. vulgatus* DSM1447 has an IC90 = 4 µg/mL, so it will not grow on the plates unless it contains the pNBU2 plasmid. However, RCC1806 has an IC90 = 256 µg/mL, meaning that even without the pNBU2 plasmid, it could grow on the plates. This could have led to false positives. Antibiotic susceptibility was not tested for the other strains, but it is possible that they have a similar IC90. To verify the presence of the plasmid, and as such the presence of false-positive colonies, integration should have been checked. We did not do this, as it is a suicide plasmid. We assumed *P. vulgatus* could not grow on mGAM + Gm + Ery without containing the plasmid, which the MIC data now show to be a wrong assumption for RCC1806, and possibly for the other clinical isolates too. A recommendation to future researchers is to check integration, or at least to test erythromycin susceptibility for all *P. vulgatus* strains and use a concentration higher than the MIC on the plates.

## Fluorescence microscopy – conjugation

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Is KOFP-7 visible in conjugated cells?">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

To visualize the presence of the pNBU2 plasmid (containing KOFP-7 downstream of a rhamnose-inducible promoter), we conducted fluorescence microscopy experiments. Conjugated *P. vulgatus* DSM1447 cells were studied, and we expected to see blue fluorescence when rhamnose was added.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Wildtype *P. vulgatus*, conjugated *P. vulgatus*, and conjugated rhamnose-induced *P. vulgatus* were studied using epi-fluorescence microscopy. Wildtype *P. vulgatus* would allow us to identify possible autofluorescence, while conjugated *P. vulgatus* without supplementing rhamnose would allow us to identify the strength of the promoter (qualitatively) and possible leaky expression.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Autofluorescence of *P. vulgatus* is clearly visible, because the wildtype (which does not contain pNBU2) emits blue fluorescent light (Figure 1A). This makes it impossible to see the effect of inducing KOFP-7 expression with rhamnose. Interestingly, this autofluorescence is not visible in cells that contained pNBU2, but which were not incubated with rhamnose (Figure 1B).

## CRISPR design

*See also: the full <Link to="/results#crispr-construct-design">CRISPR construct design</Link> in Results.*

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Riboswitch (stopped on feasibility)">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The design goal was dynamic, self-tuning control of the bile-salt pathway. A riboswitch is an RNA element that changes gene expression when it binds a small molecule. An aptamer selective for a bile salt would therefore switch expression in response to the bile salt. We planned to select the aptamer by SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential enrichment). SELEX selects RNA ligands by iterative binding and amplification.[^tuerk]

Our hypothesis was: an aptamer selective for TUDCA, GDCA, or other bile salts can feasibly be selected by SELEX within the project timeline.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

No construct was built. The feasibility check was a literature search for existing bile-salt-sensing riboswitches and an estimate of the SELEX timeline.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

The question was whether a bile-salt-sensing riboswitch could be found or made within the iGEM timeline. A literature search of riboswitch reviews and databases found no riboswitch that senses bile salts such as TUDCA, GUDCA, or TCDCA.[^serganov][^sun] The team estimated that determining an aptamer for a bile salt by SELEX would take years. This estimate was given by our PI, who described it as "a PhD thesis".

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

A bile-salt riboswitch cannot be developed within the iGEM timeline, so the riboswitch was dropped. The goal of dynamic control was kept and carried into Cycle 2. This was a feasibility decision that changed our design direction.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="CRISPRi controlled by BreR (stopped on access)">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) cycle ended because the BreR system could not be obtained. The design was a catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) system whose expression would follow the bile-salt signal.

The goal of designing a tunable biosensor continued. We discovered BreR, a bile-responsive TetR-family repressor. In *Vibrio cholerae*, bile salts relieve BreR repression of the *breAB* efflux operon.[^cerda] We thought the design could use BreR to release a repressor when deconjugated bile acids are high. Only then would a CRISPRi construct be expressed.

CRISPRi uses dCas9, which binds DNA without cutting it, to block transcription at a target site.[^qi] The guide RNA (gRNA) would be designed to target the native BSH gene, so the cell's own enzyme would be repressed when the signal is high. The design also considered expressing a second BSH, so that the enzyme's deconjugating activity could be changed.

Hypothesis: a BreR-controlled CRISPRi construct that represses native BSH only when deconjugated bile acids are high could be a feasible and successful iGEM therapeutic design.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

No DNA was assembled. However, these were the prospective parts:

- BreR cassette for environmental sensing
- gRNA and scaffold targeting the native BSH locus
- Antibiotic selection marker, chosen from the MIC assay
- dCas9
- Promoter, from the promoter library (not yet decided, because the library had not yet been tested)
- Origin of replication

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

No test was run. The BreR sequence was not publicly available when the plasmid compilation began, causing a large roadblock in this design.

The open questions below are design gaps that an additional iteration intended to address:

- Does dCas9 bind permanently, or does the binding strength (time) need to be tested?
- Is one promoter enough to drive both the BreR cassette and the gRNA, or are two needed?
- Does the repressor interfere with dCas9 activity?
- Is a reporter gene needed for testing?
- Is the integration mechanism suicidal?
- What is the efficiency of BreR in *P. vulgatus*?

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

The BreR system is patented and hard to obtain, so the design was changed. The use of BreR in *Bacteroides* is documented only in patent literature.[^lu] Two ideas were noted:

- Codon harmonization of the BreR sequence to the host. This changes the DNA but may not remove any patent coverage, because a patent can cover the protein itself. An intellectual property check is needed before this is revisited. The team's dry-lab codon optimization and harmonization tool could be used for this.
- Using a second BSH to change deconjugating activity. This idea was carried into Cycle 3.

With BreR removed, the dynamic requirement was also dropped, and the design became constitutive. PI Ruben's advice to keep the change narrow strengthened the idea of alternative BSH expression. Because of this discussion, we decided against simply knocking out the native BSH. The team's rationale is that replacing the enzyme, rather than deleting it, keeps the change smaller. This is a new hypothesis to test.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 3" title="Cas9 gene replacement (current design, 2 October 2026)">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The current design uses Cas9 to make a double-strand break at the native BSH gene. The cell's repair machinery then copies a donor carrying a replacement BSH. The design rests on three choices.

- **Replace rather than knock out.** BSH is not an essential gene, and a BSH-deletion strain of *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* is viable.[^yao] The aim is a minimal change: a replacement BSH with a different substrate preference should keep deconjugation in the gut while leaving the bile salts the team considers most important for TGR5 signalling conjugated. TGR5 is a membrane receptor for bile acids.[^maruyama][^kawamata] TUDCA and GUDCA are weak TGR5 agonists,[^ibrahim] so the wiki should not describe them as potent TGR5 agonists.
- **Use CRISPR.** CRISPR offers target specificity and uses homology-directed repair to insert a gene. CRISPR/Cas tools, including inducible FnCas12a, have been built for *Bacteroides* species, including *B. vulgatus*.[^zheng] An integrative pNBU2 backbone, validated in the team's conjugation protocol, was ruled out because pNBU2 integrates only at a serine tRNA site,[^wang] not at the native BSH locus.
- **Use a constitutive system.** The dynamic control from Cycle 1 was dropped with BreR in Cycle 2. The Cas9 and gRNA are expressed from fixed promoters.

Hypothesis: Cas9-induced repair at the native BSH site replaces the enzyme with one of different substrate preference. Expected readout: a changed percentage of deconjugated TUDCA and GUDCA, measured by LC-MS at 0.1% bile salt.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

The build is a plasmid map only. Sequences are missing for the gRNAs, the homology arms, and the replacement BSH, so the plasmid cannot yet be verified base by base.

In the wet lab, the construct would be assembled by Gibson assembly[^gibson] and conjugated from *E. coli* S17-1 λpir into *P. vulgatus*.[^simon]

<DBTLStage stage="test">

No test has been run. The planned tests below are future perspectives that guide the direction of the work. In silico, before any construct is built:

- gRNA on-target and off-target scoring against the DSM1447 genome, using CRISPOR and Cas-OFFinder. The group sets the thresholds and approves the gRNA choice.
- Homology arm design, including length and identity to the target.
- Full plasmid sequence check, including confirmation that the Cfx and BfP1E6 promoters are assigned to different parts.
- Origin compatibility between pB8-51 and R6K γ.
- Literature review of inducible promoters for Cas9 expression in *Bacteroides*. Inducible CRISPR/Cas expression has been used in *Bacteroides*,[^zheng] and inducible dCas9 control has been established in bacteria.[^qi]

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

The Learn step is open. Four decisions are pending, and each has a trigger.

- Editing efficiency too low: add Lambda Red recombineering to improve homology-directed repair. Lambda Red recombineering plasmids have been validated in Gram-negative bacteria.[^datta] It has not been shown in *Bacteroides*, so this option needs its own test.
- Cas9 expression needs tuning: add an inducible Cas9 promoter, which would also restore a form of dynamic control.
- Plasmid loss not reliable: choose a loss mechanism before the construct is described as cured. Passage without selection depends on the replicon's stability in *P. vulgatus*, which is untested.
- The replacement BSH is not named, as its choice depends on LC-MS data for the remaining bile salts of interest. Only 2 of 10 have been tested so far.

</DBTLStage>
