<div className="hp-section-intro">

<p className="hp-section-intro-label">Introduction</p>

Working with *Phocaeicola vulgatus* meant that almost every engineering decision had no precedent to fall back on. As a non-model, obligate anaerobe, it comes with very few of the established protocols, characterized parts, or troubleshooting shortcuts that make engineering fast and successful. Every Design–Build–Test–Learn cycle below had to run through an anaerobic workflow, where a single build-and-test round takes time and every failed attempt leads to new discoveries.

Across this project we **ran through 24 Design–Build–Test–Learn cycles spanning 8 areas of work**, from growth conditions and antibiotic selection to conjugation, reporters, and our BSH assay. Many of those cycles exist precisely because a first, second or third attempt did not work as expected: assumptions that hold for model organisms had to be re-tested rather than borrowed. Every cycle, successful or not, produced a Learn step that directly shaped the next Design, so the cycles that fell short still moved the project forward.

What this adds up to is not one successful construct, but a documented, reusable engineering process and newly generated data for an organism that previously had none.

</div>

## Growth curves and medium comparison

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Pilot experiment">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

In order to work with a non-model organism and engineer it, our first priority was establishing reproducible, measurable growth as without that, no downstream genetic work would be possible. Based on advice from our instructor <Link to="/human-practices#matthew-herdman">Mattew Herdman</Link> and our PI <Link to="/human-practices#ruben-vazquez-uribe">Ruben Vazquez-Uribe</Link>, we designed a pilot experiment comparing four different media chosen to span the range from rich and undefined (mGAM, BHI) to lean and defined (MM), with WC as a middle ground. We used the type strain DSM 1447, since it is commercially available and well-characterized, so that our results could be cross-checked against the literature.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We built a 96-well plate orientation with six replicate wells per condition for precision and high statistical power, blanks in triplicate to rule out random contamination, and a 24h timeframe growing at 37 °C in the anaerobic chamber. The plate reader shook the samples before each OD600 read every 30 min. The 30-minute interval was chosen so that 48 time points would be automatically collected through the time frame to capture bacterial growth phases.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

After 24 h of incubation, clear differences had emerged between the four media (Figure 1). mGAM supported fast, dense growth in every well, reaching the highest plateau of any condition in the study at a mean doubling time of ~46min. This was consistent with its composition as it is the only one of the four media carrying peptones, tissue extracts, carbohydrates, free amino acids, reducing agents, and both growth factors (hemin and vitamin K) at once. WC grew consistently but far more slowly, entering exponential phase nearly eight hours later and reaching roughly a third of mGAM’s density, in line with its leaner, peptone-only composition. BHI, despite carrying slightly more tissue extract than mGAM, did not sustain any growth, giving us an early hint that growth here depends on specific cofactors rather than raw nutrient richness. MM showed almost no signal for the first 20 h, but a small upward inflection became visible right at the end of the window, too close to the 24 h cutoff to tell apart from noise.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

Following these results, we decided to look more closely at the differences in composition between the media. BHI’s failure to support any growth, despite a similarly rich base to mGAM, pointed toward missing growth factors specifically hemin and vitamin K, which the literature confirms are required for this group of organisms [5]. That gave us a clear hypothesis: BHI will support growth once supplemented. Separately, the small late uptick in MM raised the question of whether the 24h was simply too short of a timeframe for a lean, defined medium, or if the strain genuinely failed to grow. Rather than writing MM off or committing to a new plate before knowing the answer, the fastest way to resolve the ambiguity was to keep the same plate incubating and read it again after another 24h period.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="New strain and longer incubation">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The design of our follow up experiment was aimed at addressing the MM. To settle whether MM’s delayed growth increase was representative of an early exponential phase, we kept the pilot plate running for a second 24 h window rather than discarding it. Because the plate was not changed, we did not supplement the BHI during this iteration. Additionally, we set up a new plate to test a fecal isolate, RCC 1806, for its closer biological relevance to gut bacterium and took measurements for 48 hours. The *P. vulgatus* pan-genome is constantly adapting to changes in nutrient availability in the gut, so, a more recently isolated strain might be more robust to nutrient deprived environments. Hence, we hypothesized that the fecal isolate should grow in BHI, even in the absence of supplements and thought this was worth testing. Running both strains in parallel allowed us to save time and resources.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

The pilot plate was returned to the reader for a second 24 h window the following day, giving a 48 h picture of the pilot conditions in DSM 1447 alone. The second plate (RCC 1806) was set up with three replicate wells per condition, so that all conditions could fit on one plate. As both plate readers were running in parallel, we lacked additional instrument space for the original 6 well test. The conditions consisted of DSM 1447 and RCC 1806 across all four media for 48h. This allows us to compare the strains under the same conditions, on the same plate, reducing batch effects.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

The extended read (Figure 2) answered the first question as MM’s apparent flatness at 24 h was a window problem, not a growth problem. Growth became clearly visible roughly six hours into the second day and continued to a mean doubling time of 3.48h. mGAM and WC were both already at plateau from the first day and showed only a shallow residual drift. The strain-effect plate (Figure 3) told a more complicated story. The fecal isolate grew in every medium tested, including BHI, where the DSM 1447 again showed no growth. The isolate also showed something unexpected in mGAM: a full 24h lag before it began dividing, where the type strain on the same plate started almost immediately.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

We learned three takeaways from putting these two results together. First, the window-length matters. Every later plate in the study was run for over 24 h because of it. Second, growth in BHI clearly depends on more than medium composition alone. Since RCC 1806 grew we theorized that either it is more adapted to dealing with nutrient depleted environments (like the gut) or the isolate’s growth raised doubts about whether it had been cleanly recovered from the fecal background. Since RCC 1806 is recorded as a fecal isolate rather than a pure culture and any assumption about its growth would be a speculation without further experiments. We could not resolve this with the data in hand, so it remains an open caveat on every RCC 1806 result in this study. Third, the RCC 1806’s 24h lag in mGAM, a medium that supported strong growth in DSM 1447, was an area of interest. There was no obvious explanation tied to the medium batch, since DSM 1447 on the same plate showed no lag at all. Our leading hypothesis is that the two strains had been started from precultures in different physiological states, with gene expression changes associated with metabolism possibly contributing.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 3" title="Reducing lag">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

To test whether preculture state explained the isolate’s unexpected lag, we changed how both strains originated before plating. Based on the advice from <Link to="/human-practices#laurie-comstock">Laurie Comstock</Link>, leading *Bacteriodes* researcher, that most of an overnight liquid culture is already stationary or dead by the time of subculturing, we inoculated both strains from colonies picked directly off a fresh plate instead as actively dividing cells. Also, to test whether simply adding more cells, rather than changing their physiological state, would help BHI plate at a higher inoculum density.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

The next plate orientation was built with three replicate wells per condition as previously validated, testing both DSM 1447 and RCC 1806 across all four media for 42h. The cells were picked from a colony, suspended in liquid media, and normalized for starting OD. Timepoints were measured every 30 mins under OD630 and additionally 10x inoculum density was plated to test effect of adding a higher concentration of cells.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Fresh-plate inoculation cut the isolate’s mGAM lag from 24.2 h to 6.0h, a fourfold reduction, while the type strain, went from essentially no lag to 2.5 ± 1.9 h and doubled even faster than before (Figure 4). Dense BHI inoculation, tested in parallel, did not rescue growth. Those wells lost density over time consistent with cells settling and lysing in a medium that cannot sustain them regardless of how many are added at the start.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

We learned that preculture state is the single largest source of plate-to-plate variation in this study. Both strains clearly benefited from this change, which is consistent with our preculture state influence hypothesis. Growth parameters cannot be compared across plates without proper growth phase control. Which strain grew in WC and MM reversed between cycle two and three. DSM 1447 grew in both and RCC 1806 in neither. This emphasizes that strain differences cannot be treated as universally applicable if a standardized preculture protocol is not followed. With the lag problem largely solved, the remaining question was whether MM could be supplemented to close the rate gap with mGAM. It was evident now that unsupplemented BHI was attributed to the lack of DSM1447 growth.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 4" title="Full factorial study">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

Our final goal was to optimize the growth yield of DSM1447 in our minimal media. We used the literature[^tramontano] and advice of our PI Ruben to identify three candidate supplements: soluble starch (carbon source), vitamin K (growth factor) and a pH adjustment to 7.2 and tested all eight combinations in a full 2³-factorial design, to understand their influences and interactions.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Six wells per condition were set up to be measured at OD600 for 48 hours and included eight supplement combinations plus unsupplemented MM and mGAM as references. The base medium had been contaminated during preparation and was filter-sterilized before use in an effort to reduce external effects while continuing with the prepped media available.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

In our final test, the clearest pattern (Figure 5) was that pH adjustment appeared in every combination that grew and in none of the combinations that failed. All three supplements together gave the largest gain (+0.047 OD), but growth stayed slow (doubling time = 12.3 h), and the differences between the working combinations were not statistically significant after multiple-testing correction.

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

In conclusion, we learned that pH adjustment to 7.2 is necessary but not sufficient on its own, and that the MM with complete supplements supported the highest biomass. However, the plate was underpowered (six wells per condition) for resolving the smaller effect sizes, and the contamination of the base medium added a caveat: the unsupplemented control, which had shown at least some growth in the type strain in every earlier experiment, grew in none of its wells here. This was likely because the contaminant had consumed components of the media that were missing regardless of our filter sterilization attempt to restore the media. Dose-response optimization of the supplement and a clean re-run of the unsupplemented control are the recommended next steps.

</DBTLStage>

## MIC assay

*See also: <Link to="/results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus">MIC results</Link>, <Link to="/protocols#minimum-inhibitory-concentration-testing">MIC protocol</Link> and <Link to="/dry-lab#2-1-antibiotic-selection">dry-lab antibiotic selection</Link>.*

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Antibiotic concentration ranges from literature were different from the ranges we found">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The goal for this experiment was to understand the susceptibility of *P. vulgatus* strains to range of antibiotics for downstream selection markers and biocontainment. Since no MIC data for our strains was available, we took starting concentrations for seven antibiotics from the literature on related *Bacteroides* species and tested DSM1447. We predicted two failures in advance: ampicillin not having an effect, because β-lactamases are reported in *P. vulgatus*, and kanamycin, because aminoglycoside uptake depends on the proton motive force and thus on oxygen, which an obligate anaerobe does not respire.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Seven antibiotics were used across three 96-well plates: kanamycin, metronidazole and spectinomycin on plate 1, ampicillin, chloramphenicol and erythromycin on plate 2, and tetracycline on plate 3. Each antibiotic occupied three adjacent columns, giving triplicate series, with the highest concentration in row A and a two-fold dilution at every step down to row G, each well containing 180 µL of mGAM. Row H was filled with inoculated mGAM as a positive growth control. The fourth column of each block carried the matching solvent (ddH₂O, DMSO, or ethanol at 50, 95 or 100%) diluted in the same two-fold steps, so that solvent toxicity could be told apart from antibiotic effect. The inoculum was prepared from an overnight culture of DSM1447 in mGAM, diluted into a single master plate, and transferred into each dilution plate with a 96-pin replicator so that every well started from the same cell density. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C and read at OD630 after 24 h and 48 h.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

After 48h of incubation time, none of our strains grew. For most compounds the entire series inhibited, so the ranges sat above the useful window, and no IC90 could be calculated. Kanamycin and spectinomycin did the opposite: growth continued at every concentration tested, up to 1024 µg/mL for kanamycin.

This experiment gave us no MIC values but we learned that the literature ranges are several two-fold steps too high for our strains. Our predictions held for kanamycin, which is what gave us the approval for continuation with our plate layout. Ampicillin, in contrast, inhibited at every concentration tested, so the β-lactamase not dropped. Kanamycin and spectinomycin were dropped routine testing as those showed no inhibition at the highest concentrations.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="Lower ranges, and advice, giving a second strain and two new compounds">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

For our next iteration, we lowered every starting concentration by up to three two-fold steps. Two additions came out of conversations rather than the data: our instructor Matt supplied gentamicin, the aminoglycoside used clinically to clear gut microbes. Additionally, our PI, Ruben, advised us to include simvastatin (a statin) in the MIC assay, as this has been reported to inhibit *P. vulgatus* DSM1447[^escalante]. We also included the fecal strain RCC1806, since a toolkit for *P. vulgatus* should not rest on the type strain alone.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We used the same plate layout, replication and incubation time as for Cycle 1, so the ranges were the only change for DSM1447. RCC1806 was run at the original literature ranges, as its own window was still unknown.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Across the two strains:

- **Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and metronidazole** inhibited at the low end
- **Ampicillin and erythromycin** inhibited at concentrations that differed between the two strains
- **Gentamicin and kanamycin** had no effect on DSM1447, but gentamicin inhibited RCC1806 at the top of its series
- **Tetracycline** inhibited across its whole series, range was still too high

Including the second strain paid off immediately: ampicillin, erythromycin and gentamicin cannot be treated as having one species-level value. Three loose ends remained: tetracycline still had no endpoint, the ampicillin replicates disagreed, and RCC1806’s panel was incomplete.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 3" title="Closing the gap in the fecal strain">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

In MIC-02, RCC1806 had been run at the original literature ranges rather than the lowered ones used for DSM1447. For erythromycin, spectinomycin and simvastatin the strain grew at every concentration tested, so no inhibitory endpoint was reached. These three were therefore repeated at higher ranges, since the faecal strain had already proved the less susceptible of the two.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Our building strategy was identical to Cycle 2, but we included RCC1806 strain only. Initially, our first overnight cultures failed because the plates we used had sat in the anaerobic chamber over two weeks and had dried and cracked, so fresh plates were struck from cryostock and the run restarted.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

When we ran the experiment, we noticed that erythromycin inhibited RCC1806, but not at the same concentration that was used for DSM1447. This confirmed difference between the was visible here and not an artefact of the ranges used.

The main takeaway is the fecal isolate does not behave like the type strain. Erythromycin inhibited RCC1806 at a different concentration that DSM1447. This pattern is consistent: RCC1806 is less susceptible of the two across several compounds. Susceptibility values from DSM1447 thus cannot be assumed to transfer to fecal or clinical isolates, and any concentration used for selection or containment has to be checked per strain. The cycle also gave us a practical in-lab rule: plates older than about a week in the chamber are not a reliable inoculum source, so working from recent plates became standard practice for the rest of the project.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 4" title="Settling the values, and discovering the cutoff mattered">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

By this point each antibiotics’ dilution series showed its inhibition point for both strains, so the aim shifted from locating endpoints to settling working values across the full panel, with enough replication to compare compounds statistically.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We did three replicate series per antibiotic with matched solvent controls, plates for DSM1447 alone and for both strains together, read at OD630 after 48 h of incubation. Endpoints were scored at 90% inhibition and compared within each strain by Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post hoc test.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

All antibiotics gave results within an acceptable range, which could be used for statistical calculations. The 90% cutoff on the other hand turned out to be suboptimal: wells at lower concentrations sometimes passed 90% while the highest concentration of the same series read 88–89%, which threw away most of the tetracycline data. Re-scoring at 85% kept those replicates and supported the same three compounds.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

What we learned from these iterations is that Tetracycline, chloramphenicol and metronidazole are the most effective of the panel. We chose metronidazole as the containment compound rather than simply the most potent one, because it is the only compound here with usable clinical guidance. Ampicillin was ruled out: it varies more than ten-fold between strains. The aminoglycoside result fed straight back into the conjugation work, where selection plates use 200 µg/mL gentamicin to counter-select *E. coli*. As *P. vulgatus* DSM1447 was insensitive to gentamicin, this allowed *P. vulgatus* to grow on the plates and kill *E. coli* at the same time, leaving us with only DSM1447 on our plates during conjugation.

</DBTLStage>

## Conjugation

*See also: the <Link to="/protocols#conjugation-of-pnbu2-plasmids-into-p-vulgatus">conjugation protocol</Link> and the <Link to="/results#conjugation-efficiency-testing">conjugation results</Link>.*

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Pilot run with the type strain">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

We needed to ensure successful conjugation so we could further engineer our therapeutic chassis.

First, we did a preliminary conjugation test to assess, and potentially optimize, the efficiency of conjugation from *E. coli* S17-1 λpir (a bacterial strain optimized for conjugation) to *P. vulgatus* DSM1447. As the protocol had already been successful for other *Bacteroides* species, we decided to do a pilot run without any changes to the initial protocol.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

To start building this pilot run, *E. coli* S17-1 λpir and *P. vulgatus* DSM1447 were grown until they both reached an OD of 0.2. They were mixed and plated out on rich mGAM medium. After a lawn formed, we plated out dilutions (10<sup>−1</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup>) on mGAM with gentamicin (Gm) and erythromycin (Ery). The reason for using these antibiotics is that *P. vulgatus* is naturally resistant to Gm and, when successfully conjugated, the pNBU2 plasmid will carry a resistance cassette against Ery. This way we ensure that only successfully conjugated strains can grow. As an additional selection marker, the pNBU2 plasmid carries a rhamnose-inducible promoter upstream of the reporter gene *kofp-7*.

In case of successful conjugation, we expected to see only resistant colonies expressing our *kofp-7*.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

For our first test run, we counted colonies on the plates to quantitatively determine the conjugation efficiency. Our testing confirmed high efficiency (4.11 × 10<sup>9</sup>), and single colonies were clearly visible only on the dilutions 10<sup>−5</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup>.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="Adding the clinical isolates">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The *P. vulgatus* strains RCC1806, DSM108222, DSM108234, DSM108228 and DSM3289 were now included in the experiment to determine conjugation efficiency. Our goal was to test all strains we had available and compare efficiency, in order to see which one would be the best for us to work with, but also to generate more data about these microorganisms. However, based on the success rate of the previous run, we decided to plate only the 10<sup>−5</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup> dilutions.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Besides changing the dilutions, the protocol was executed the same as in the previous cycle, only this time we tested everything in duplicate to ensure consistency of our data.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Including more than just our type strain showed that the conjugation efficiencies of all other strains were lower. This was possibly because we diluted our cells too much, or because conjugation simply failed. As a result, colonies were not counted.

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 3" title="Plating every dilution">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

Based on our previous conclusions, we decided to plate all dilutions in this cycle and to always include DSM1447 as a positive control. We expected to see more success with the rest of the strains because of the wide range of dilutions we included this time.

## BSH assay

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Getting a plate that pours clear and supports growth">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

Initially, we planned to test and evaluate BSH activity with a halo assay, where deconjugated bile salts would precipitate on the medium and form a ‘halo’ ring around the colony. Our designed included transforming *E.coli* DH5α with a plasmid constitutively expressing a BSH variant and growing them on agar plates supplemented with bile salts. Not only are these bile salts difficult to obtain but with no protocol to follow, we were unsure of how to best dissolve them. To solve this, our goals were (1) to figure out in which order the medium components must be assembled for the agar to pour cleanly, (2) whether the bile salts should be dissolved in DMSO or dH₂O, and (3) understand optimal concentrations of bile salt substrates.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We first set up an experiment by plating *E. coli* expressing two BSH variants: C1 (BSH3) and C2 (BSH11). These two constructs had the highest DNA at synthesis from IDT, and we reasoned that it must have the lowest toxicity to yield such high amounts.

Plating conditions were the same as in our previous run.

A halo depends on a precipitate forming and being visible against the agar, and nothing in these images distinguishes ‘no activity’ from ‘activity this method cannot show’. That’s why we repeated the comparison in liquid culture. Incubating the BSH constructs with GUDCA and TUDCA and measuring the conjugated and deconjugated ratios directly allows the activity and substrate preference to be quantified rather than inferred from a halo.

*Please see our next steps in the <Link to="/engineering#lc-ms">LC-MS cycle</Link>.*

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

</DBTLCycles>

## LC-MS

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Bile salt mixture at 0.5%">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

We pivoted from BSH ‘Halo’ assay to LC-MS analysis (see <Link to="/engineering#bsh-assay">BSH assay DBTL</Link> for more information). With LC-MS, we used smaller quantities of expensive bile acids while still getting precise measurements. When designing this experiment, we were aware that both the conjugated and the deconjugated bile salts in pure form are necessary as a reference, however, we only had conjugated forms at our disposal. We used a commercial bile salt mixture to calibrate the column and set up the method before any samples were measured. *See <Link to="/protocols#lc-ms-quantification-of-bile-acid-deconjugation">LC-MS measurement protocol</Link> for more information.*

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

We incubated *E. coli* expressing our BSH variants in liquid LB with 0.5% bile salt mixture (containing TUDCA and GUDCA together). These samples were then dried with speedvac on alcohol setting and redissolved in 50% MeOH. Due to a high number of samples, we were forced to split them into 2 days of work. Hence we stored the samples at -20°C, following the advice of our Prof. Jef Rozenski. This sample conservation at –20°C allowed us to do the measurements across multiple days.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

As the instrument is expensive and sensitive, all measurements were conducted by our instructor, Prof. Jef Rozenski. For our first test, the ratio between conjugated and deconjugated bile salts was calculated and the log10 of the ratios for each of the 15 BSH variants and the sfGFP control were compared for interpretability.

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="Separating the bile salts at 0.1%">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

Based on our previous findings, we now designed an experiment where TUDCA and GUDCA are separated. This way, each BSH preference could be determined. Prof. Rozenski advised us to redissolve the final pellet in differing MeOH amounts proportioanl to the percentage of bile salts (0.25% or 0.1%). This means the samples would have the same final concentration, simplifying downstream calculations.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

Our design included 16 samples of TUDCA (×2), 16 of GUDCA (×2), both at concentrations of 0.1% and 0.25% bile salts (128 samples total). Four samples were misplaced in the rack, both C1 tubes sitting where C2 should have been and vice versa, and tubes labelled twice (A1 A1) were replicates rather than distinct samples. Prof. Rozenski assumed they were not mislabeled and acquired C1 as C1.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

Protein traces from the 0.5% samples were clogging the column, with a reported high backpressure. Because of this, the lower concentration samples was tested first to determine if they yielded output within a reasonable range. After confirmation, we decided that only lower concentration samples were necessary for testing.  Across all three conditions, he standardized the readout as a percentage deconjugated.

## CRISPR design

*See also: the full <Link to="/results#crispr-construct-design">CRISPR construct design</Link> in Results.*

<DBTLCycles>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 1" title="Riboswitch (stopped on feasibility)">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The design goal was dynamic, self-tuning control of the bile-salt pathway. A riboswitch is an RNA element that changes gene expression when it binds a small molecule. An aptamer selective for a bile salt would therefore switch expression in response to the bile salt. We planned to select the aptamer by SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential enrichment). SELEX selects RNA ligands by iterative binding and amplification.[^tuerk]

Our hypothesis was: an aptamer selective for TUDCA, GDCA, or other bile salts can feasibly be selected by SELEX within the project timeline.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

No construct was built. The feasibility check was a literature search for existing bile-salt-sensing riboswitches and an estimate of the SELEX timeline.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

The question was whether a bile-salt-sensing riboswitch could be found or made within the iGEM timeline. A literature search of riboswitch reviews and databases found no riboswitch that senses bile salts such as TUDCA, GUDCA, or TCDCA.[^serganov][^sun] The team estimated that determining an aptamer for a bile salt by SELEX would take years. This estimate was given by our PI, who described it as "a PhD thesis".

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

A bile-salt riboswitch cannot be developed within the iGEM timeline, so the riboswitch was dropped. The goal of dynamic control was kept and carried into Cycle 2. This was a feasibility decision that changed our design direction.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 2" title="CRISPRi controlled by BreR (stopped on access)">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) cycle ended because the BreR system could not be obtained. The design was a catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) system whose expression would follow the bile-salt signal.

The goal of designing a tunable biosensor continued. We discovered BreR, a bile-responsive TetR-family repressor. In *Vibrio cholerae*, bile salts relieve BreR repression of the *breAB* efflux operon.[^cerda] We thought the design could use BreR to release a repressor when deconjugated bile acids are high. Only then would a CRISPRi construct be expressed.

CRISPRi uses dCas9, which binds DNA without cutting it, to block transcription at a target site.[^qi] The guide RNA (gRNA) would be designed to target the native BSH gene, so the cell's own enzyme would be repressed when the signal is high. The design also considered expressing a second BSH, so that the enzyme's deconjugating activity could be changed.

Hypothesis: a BreR-controlled CRISPRi construct that represses native BSH only when deconjugated bile acids are high could be a feasible and successful iGEM therapeutic design.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

No DNA was assembled. However, these were the prospective parts:

- BreR cassette for environmental sensing
- gRNA and scaffold targeting the native BSH locus
- Antibiotic selection marker, <Link to="/results#antibiotic-susceptibility-of-p-vulgatus">chosen from the MIC assay</Link>
- dCas9
- Promoter, <Link to="/results#a-characterized-promoter-library-for-p-vulgatus">from the promoter library</Link> (not yet decided, because the library had not yet been tested)
- Origin of replication

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="test">

No test was run. The BreR sequence was not publicly available when the plasmid compilation began, causing a large roadblock in this design.

The open questions below are design gaps that an additional iteration intended to address:

- Does dCas9 bind permanently, or does the binding strength (time) need to be tested?
- Is one promoter enough to drive both the BreR cassette and the gRNA, or are two needed?
- Does the repressor interfere with dCas9 activity?
- Is a reporter gene needed for testing?
- Is the integration mechanism suicidal?
- What is the efficiency of BreR in *P. vulgatus*?

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

The BreR system is patented and hard to obtain, so the design was changed. The use of BreR in *Bacteroides* is documented only in patent literature.[^lu] Two ideas were noted:

- Codon harmonization of the BreR sequence to the host. This changes the DNA but may not remove any patent coverage, because a patent can cover the protein itself. An intellectual property check is needed before this is revisited. The team's <Link to="/dry-lab">dry-lab codon optimization and harmonization tool</Link> could be used for this.
- Using a second BSH to change deconjugating activity. This idea was carried into Cycle 3.

With BreR removed, the dynamic requirement was also dropped, and the design became constitutive. PI Ruben's advice to keep the change narrow strengthened the idea of alternative BSH expression. Because of this discussion, we decided against simply knocking out the native BSH. The team's rationale is that replacing the enzyme, rather than deleting it, keeps the change smaller. This is a new hypothesis to test.

</DBTLStage>

</DBTLCycle>

<DBTLCycle label="Cycle 3" title="Cas9 gene replacement (current design, 2 October 2026)">

<DBTLStage stage="design">

The current design uses Cas9 to make a double-strand break at the native BSH gene. The cell's repair machinery then copies a donor carrying a replacement BSH. The design rests on three choices.

- **Replace rather than knock out.** BSH is not an essential gene, and a BSH-deletion strain of *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* is viable.[^yao] The aim is a minimal change: a replacement BSH with a different substrate preference should keep deconjugation in the gut while leaving the bile salts the team considers most important for TGR5 signalling conjugated. TGR5 is a membrane receptor for bile acids.[^maruyama][^kawamata] TUDCA and GUDCA are weak TGR5 agonists,[^ibrahim] so the wiki should not describe them as potent TGR5 agonists.
- **Use CRISPR.** CRISPR offers target specificity and uses homology-directed repair to insert a gene. CRISPR/Cas tools, including inducible FnCas12a, have been built for *Bacteroides* species, including *B. vulgatus*.[^zheng] An integrative pNBU2 backbone, validated in the team's conjugation protocol, was ruled out because pNBU2 integrates only at a serine tRNA site,[^wang] not at the native BSH locus.
- **Use a constitutive system.** The dynamic control from Cycle 1 was dropped with BreR in Cycle 2. The Cas9 and gRNA are expressed from fixed promoters.

Hypothesis: Cas9-induced repair at the native BSH site replaces the enzyme with one of different substrate preference. Expected readout: a changed percentage of deconjugated TUDCA and GUDCA, measured by LC-MS at 0.1% bile salt.

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="build">

The build is a plasmid map only. Sequences are missing for the gRNAs, the homology arms, and the replacement BSH, so the plasmid cannot yet be verified base by base.

In the wet lab, the construct would be assembled by <Link to="/protocols#gibson-assembly">Gibson assembly</Link>[^gibson] and <Link to="/protocols#conjugation-of-pnbu2-plasmids-into-p-vulgatus">conjugated</Link> from *E. coli* S17-1 λpir into *P. vulgatus*.[^simon]

<DBTLStage stage="test">

No test has been run. The planned tests below are future perspectives that guide the direction of the work. In silico, before any construct is built:

- gRNA on-target and off-target scoring against the DSM1447 genome, using CRISPOR and Cas-OFFinder. The group sets the thresholds and approves the gRNA choice.
- Homology arm design, including length and identity to the target.
- Full plasmid sequence check, including confirmation that the Cfx and BfP1E6 promoters are assigned to different parts.
- Origin compatibility between pB8-51 and R6K γ.
- Literature review of inducible promoters for Cas9 expression in *Bacteroides*. Inducible CRISPR/Cas expression has been used in *Bacteroides*,[^zheng] and inducible dCas9 control has been established in bacteria.[^qi]

</DBTLStage>

<DBTLStage stage="learn">

The Learn step is open. Four decisions are pending, and each has a trigger.

- Editing efficiency too low: add Lambda Red recombineering to improve homology-directed repair. Lambda Red recombineering plasmids have been validated in Gram-negative bacteria.[^datta] It has not been shown in *Bacteroides*, so this option needs its own test.
- Cas9 expression needs tuning: add an inducible Cas9 promoter, which would also restore a form of dynamic control.
- Plasmid loss not reliable: choose a loss mechanism before the construct is described as cured. Passage without selection depends on the replicon's stability in *P. vulgatus*, which is untested.
- The replacement BSH is not named, as its choice depends on LC-MS data for the remaining bile salts of interest. Only 2 of 10 have been tested so far.
