## Stakeholder Framework

To really understand the societal impact and feasibility of EMPOWER, we deliberately strived to think outside the norm when deciding whom to include as stakeholders. We aimed to reach out to a diverse representation of each stakeholder group to gather different perspectives and opinions. Beyond the technicalities and the science, it was particularly important to us to include non-academic views, patients, representatives of the PMOS community, legislators and healthcare professionals with varying background. 

We grouped our stakeholders into five categories: healthcare professionals, scientific & technical experts, biotech entrepreneurs & industry, patients, and advocates & legislators. Within each category, we aimed to reach a diverse group of people to gather as many different perspectives and insights as possible.

## Feedback Cycle

<FeedbackCycle>

To ensure a sustainable and productive project development, we implemented an iterative Feedback Cycle with three steps: Listen, Analyse, Implement.

<FeedbackStep step="listen">

Before each interview, we researched the person we were speaking to in depth. We prepared personalized questions based on their experience, so we would get targeted feedback where we needed it most. Depending on the stakeholder, we focused on different aspects of the project. For example, with people without a scientific background, we simplified the theoretical part of the project as much as possible, and centered on information relevant to the conversation.

<Interviewee name="Dr. Biljana Popovic Todorovic " role="Gynecologist & Head of IVF Clinic, Avala General Hospital" date="15 September 2026" basedIn="Belgrade, Serbia" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/biljana.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — We contacted Dr. Biljana Popovic Todorovic, who is Head of the IVF (In Vitro Fertilization) clinic at the Avala General Hospital in Belgrade, Serbia with international research and treatment experience to better understand the realities of IVF and of fertility care for women with PMOS. Additionally, we hoped she would provide us with a multinational view on the PMOS clinics and how they differ in treatment plans and patient care, considering her broad international work experience.

**What did we learn?** — She named age as the main limit on IVF success, because patients are getting older on average. She also explained how standard IVF differs from a gentler alternative, in vitro maturation (IVM). In IVF, hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to produce several eggs, which are collected once they are mature. This can cause ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), a swelling of the ovaries in reaction to the hormones. Doctors can keep this risk very low, for example by freezing all embryos for a later transfer, but the enlarged ovaries still cause discomfort. In IVM, the ovaries are not stimulated, or only briefly primed with hormones, so there is no risk of OHSS. The eggs are collected before they are mature and ripen in the lab instead.
She sees little difference in how women with PMOS are treated across countries. The key difference is how long it takes to receive treatment. Women whose main issue is infertility usually see a reproductive specialist first, and those with amenorrhoea (asbent or irregular menstruation), weight gain, obesity, acne or hirsutism see a reproductive endocrinologist first. In her experience, women with PMOS account for about 5–10% of her patients who struggle with fertility. She also mentioned that PMOS is stigmatized and poorly understood: many patients do not realize they have the condition or what it means.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Dr. Popovic Todorovic provided us with valuable insights on how IVF, which is a common treatment for infertility, and PMOS are looked at from a multinational perspective. From this, we continued to research how phenotype manifest differently in different regions of the world.

<Interviewee name="Dr. Ruben Vazquez Uribe " role="Staff Scientist & Research Manager in Gut Microbiome, VIB-KU Leuven Center for Microbiology " date="02 April 2026" basedIn="Leuven, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/ruben.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — At the start of the project, we just concluded that we want to work on regulating the bile-salt metabolism through the gut bacterium P. vulgatus but lacked the experimental expertise to judge whether this was feasible. Therefore, we contacted Dr. Ruben Vazquez Uribe, who is a research scientist and Head of Fermentations at the VIB-KU Leuven Center for Microbiology, for advice on engineering gut bacteria and microbiome research.

**What did we learn?** — Initially, Dr. Uribe advised us not to build our project on P. vulgatus, since it is not a model organism and its limited genetic tools could make the project too ambitious for the iGEM timeline. He pointed out that different BSH enzymes differ in substrate activity and should not be treated as equals and suggested investigating more efficient alternatives. Bile acids are chemically very similar and hard to measure, so he recommended LC-MS, chromatography or bile-acid-sensitive biosensors. He also raised CRISPR as a method to selectively kill P. vulgatus, with bacterial conjugation as a more realistic delivery method than phages. However, he emphasized that the main challenge would be delivering the CRISPR system into the bacterium. While phage-based delivery would likely be difficult to establish, bacterial conjugation could provide a more realistic alternative. He encouraged us looking into modifying disease-relevant molecules that are already present in the gut, rather than making the entire project dependent P. vulgatus. 

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — We compared the activities of different BSH enzymes and explored how we can incorporate E. coli as a model host instead of working directly in P. vulgatus. As a result, we transformed different BSH variants into E. coli to characterize them instead of doing it in P. vulgatus directly. We have also investigated alternative enzymes that could also affect bile-acid metabolism as advised but decided to continue working with BSH as our target. We also focused more on bacterial conjugation as a possible way to deliver genetic material into P. vulgatus. This led us to discuss how CRISPR could potentially be included in our system. This was one of many meetings with Dr. Uribe, who agreed to be our  co-Principal Investigator for the project. He kindly provided us with facilities of Raes laboratory such as anaerobic chambers, to investigate conjugation mechanisms with P. vulgatus and to fully develop our toolkit. 
Besides incredibly valuable scientific advice provided by Ruben, another way he shaped our IHP was through his professional network. Considering his expertise is reflecting our project, his network was invaluable to shape our IHP progress.

<Interviewee name="Prof. Dr. Weston Whitaker" role="Assistant Professor, Penn State University" date="13 July 2026" basedIn="University Park, PA, USA" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/weston-whitaker.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Prof. Dr. Weston Whitaker is the founder of Novome Biotechnologies and a synthetic biologist who engineered P. vulgatus for a therapeutic that reached the clinical stage, one of the closest real-world precedents to our project. We contacted him for advice on promoter and reporter selection, delivery and engraftment, biocontainment, regulation and patient acceptance of engineered bacteria.

**What did we learn?** — Dr. Weston Whitaker advised us on several practical aspects of engineering P. vulgatus. He explained that strong constitutive expression can be difficult, especially when constructs are integrated into the genome. For expression systems in P. vulgatus, he recommended strong promoters that have been well-established and a common RBS. He advised Nanoluciferase as an effective reporter, since GFP does not work in anaerobic conditions, and recommended avoiding antibiotic resistance markers and using inducible or feedback-controlled promoters to regulate activity. He noted that conjugation works better than electroporation in Bacteroides. 
On delivery, he found that in-situ editing would add a considerable amount of complexity and advised engineering one well-characterized strain and introducing it with a defined metabolic niche to allow engraftment (Novome used porphyrin). He considered our approach safer and more predictable than delivering IL-22 directly and stressed involving patients throughout development.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Based on his approach from Novome, we reconsidered our delivery strategy by comparing in-situ conjugation with introducing an ex-vivo engineered strain with a defined metabolic niche. For the wet lab, we implemented several of the promoters he recommended, Nanoluciferase, and avoided antibiotic resistance markers. He also strengthened our safety rationale for targeting BSH.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Dr. Mark Charbonneau" role="Chief Scientific Officer, Seco Bio" date="04 August 2026" basedIn="Cambridge, MA, USA">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Dr. Mark Charbonneau has experience developing and modelling engineered microbial therapeutics at Synlogic, including pharmacokinetics, clinical translation and regulatory development. We expected that his expertise could be especially relevant for evaluating our delivery strategy and dry-lab possibilities.

**What did we learn?** — While he liked our approach, Dr. Charbonneau doubted how reliable and specific the P. vulgatus pathway is, since other gut bacteria may perform similar reactions. He stressed that we needed to clearly define where in the gastrointestinal tract our therapeutic acts: bile acids in the colon are already largely deconjugated, making changes difficult to measure there. He suggested considering alternative sampling strategies and biomarkers. He also recommended bioinformatically investigating other BSH-producing species. 
For delivery, he highlighted that the strain might need protection from stomach acid, and that colonization and selective pressure are key issues. Clinical translation of engineered microbes is possible, but manufacturing, scale-up and patient recruitment are major bottlenecks. 

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — We integrated his recommendations, by investigating the specificity of P. vulgatus and BSH more closely, as this was also critiqued previously. Therefore, other BSH-producing gut bacteria were analyzed in our dry-lab work. Acid-resistant delivery, pharmacokinetics, target location and biomarkers were beyond what we could test this season, so we recorded them as requirements for future development.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Prof. Dr. Joleen Masschelein" role="Principle Investigator, Laboratory for Biomolecular Discovery & Engineering, KU Leuven" date="12 August 2026" basedIn="Leuven, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/joleen-masschelein.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Prof. Joleen Masschelein is a researcher at KU Leuven's Center for Microbiology with a specialty in synthetic biology, genetic system design, and biocontainment. As we were continuously looking for comparisons of our two engineering approaches, we wanted her opinion on the feasibility and safety as well as on suitable reporters, genetic verification methods, and possible bioinformatic strategies.

**What did we learn?** — We presented two delivery strategies and our initial idea of a bile-acid-dependent kill switch. She called direct engineering of P. vulgatus technically challenging and warned that in-situ conjugation lacks specificity, because genetic material can also transfer to other Bacteroides species. She encouraged us to keep evaluating both.
For biocontainment, she suggested a bile-acid-dependent system in which the absence of bile acids triggers cell death, for example through CRISPR targeting of housekeeping genes or toxin-antitoxin systems. She stressed genetic stability and fitness cost. For verification, she recommended Nanoluciferase under anaerobic conditions and colony PCR, and suggested HMMER and remote-homology searches to find other BSH-carrying organisms.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Her suggestions, CRISPR targeting of housekeeping genes or toxin-antitoxin systems controlled by bile acids, became options in our biocontainment design. Her feedback also refined our wet-lab strategy. She connected us with her postdoc, Dr. Aaron Angles Pantoja, to explore circuit design and biocontainment in more detail.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Prof. Dr. Tae Seok Moon" role="Professor, J. Craig Venter Institute" date="19 August 2026" basedIn="Rockville, MD, USA" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/tae-seok-moon-jpg.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Prof. Tae Seok Moon is a professor at the J. Craig Venter Institute whose research focuses on synthetic biology, engineered probiotics, and genetic biocontainment. His experience developing CRISPR-based kill switches made him particularly relevant to the safety aspects of our expression system. We wanted some feedback on the two engineering approaches, kill-switch strategy, whether CRISPR works in P. vulgatus.

**What did we learn?** — He responded positively to the overall project but emphasized the importance of verifying whether CRISPR systems could function reliably in P. vulgatus before incorporating them into our design. Interestingly, he explained a kill switch is scientifically not strictly necessary, since engineered strains rarely out-compete natives and anaerobes technically die under aerobic conditions. 
Prof. Moon discussed several possible biocontainment strategies, including CRISPR-Cas9 systems targeting multiple essential genes and genes and a bile-acid-responsive circuit based on the BreR biosensor, which he found especially promising as a form of dynamic control. He also encouraged us to highlight the prevalence of the condition among menopausal women.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Led by his advice, biocontainment is framed as a must for the EU context in our design and safety narrative. We identified his published CRISPR-Cas9 kill-switch system and the BreR-based bile-acid sensor as possible starting points for our own design, which also led to us reaching out to the developer of the system, Mao Taketani. We also recognized the need to improve our toolkit for future studies by validating the CRISPR activity in P. vulgatus. Finally, we incorporated a stronger emphasis on how PMOS manifests throughout different age groups and menopause.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Dr. Aaron Angles Pantoja " role="Postdoctoral Researcher, Laboratory for Biomolecular Discovery & Engineering, KU Leuven" date="20 August 2026" basedIn="Leuven, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/aaron.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Dr. Aaron Angles Pantoja is a postdoctoral researcher working on genetic system design and microbial engineering in Joleen Masschelein's Laboratory for Biomolecular Discovery & Engineering. Followed by our discussion with Prof. Masschelein, we contacted him to explore how genetic circuits and biocontainment systems work in practice. 

**What did we learn?** — He stressed that the safety profile of P. vulgatus needs careful consideration, because it can behave as an opportunistic bacterium, and advised us to check our strain for toxin-producing capability with bioinformatic tools. He added that, depending on the therapeutic application, biocontainment may matter less than expected.
He suggested a CRISPR-Cas12-based kill switch that degrades the chromosome, as an alternative to systems targeting individual essential genes, and recommended an anaerobic-maturing GFP variant as a reporter for oxygen-sensitive bacteria. For two-plasmid systems, he noted that different origins of replication are needed.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Dr. Pantoja kindly provided the anaerobic-compatible GFP variant, which became an additional reporter in our toolkit, and his advice on different origins of replication informed how we plan circuits with several genetic components. His CRISPR-Cas12 kill switch widened the biocontainment options we investigated. Additionally, he also kindly contributed to constructing the Safety Plan.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Dr. Mao Taketani" role="Synthetic biologist" date="27 August 2026" basedIn="Cambridge, MA, USA" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/mao-taketani.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Mao Taketani developed the BreR bile-acid sensing system in Bacteroides, which Tae Seok Moon considered a promising basis for our biocontainment circuit. We contacted her to learn how the sensor was built and how well it could be adapted to our design.

**What did we learn?** — BreR worked in Bacteroides unexpectedly: it comes from a different bacterial species, and Bacteroides has an unusual promoter and RBS structure that normally breaks foreign genetic parts. Two other candidate sensors (CmeR and KstR) did not work at all in this host. Sensitivity differs between bile acids, and a good sensor needs a dynamic range of 100- to 1,000-fold. She recommended strong phage promoters, which worked very well in Bacteroides, and Weston Whitaker's tunable expression tools as a basis for an inducible system, for example a phage promoter combined with BreR.
On the business side, she advised us to research potential buyers or licensees before building a company, since a start-up needs an exit through licensing or acquisition. She also advised filing a patent before publishing anything, bringing the PIs on board, and using KU Leuven R&D, which handles much of the process.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — The meeting gave us concrete design parameters for a BreR-based sensor (strong phage promoters and a dynamic range of at least 100-fold) and papers to build on. Unfortunately, it has not been actively implemented for now due to time and resource limitations but will be re-investigated in future circuit designs. 

</Interviewee>
