## Stakeholder Framework

To really understand the societal impact and feasibility of EMPOWER, we deliberately strived to think outside the norm when deciding whom to include as stakeholders. We aimed to reach out to a diverse representation of each stakeholder group to gather different perspectives and opinions. Beyond the technicalities and the science, it was particularly important to us to include non-academic views, patients, representatives of the PMOS community, legislators and healthcare professionals with varying background. 

We grouped our stakeholders into five categories: healthcare professionals, scientific & technical experts, biotech entrepreneurs & industry, patients, and advocates & legislators. Within each category, we aimed to reach a diverse group of people to gather as many different perspectives and insights as possible.

<StakeholderGroups>

<StakeholderGroup title="Healthcare Professionals" icon="healthcare">

Before engaging with patients, we first wanted to gain a solid understanding of the clinical facts of PMOS: its symptoms, diagnostic process, prevalence and first-line treatment. Therefore, we contacted healthcare professionals, in particular gynecologists or PMOS specialists. This stakeholder group consists of [x] doctors and specialists from different backgrounds, who offered a clinical perspective on PMOS and its current treatment availabilities.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Scientific & Technical Experts" icon="science">

To learn from their experience, we reached out to knowledgeable scientists, PIs, professors from academia and the industry who have previously worked on projects similar to ours. We knew from the start that introducing an engineered bacterium into an environment as complex as the gut was going to be challenging. We therefore sought out scientists who could provide us with the know-how on the technical aspects, methodological skills and also the current scientific landscape. In total, we consulted [x] people from the synthetic biology field, who helped strengthen our project on a technical level.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Biotech Entrepreneurs & Industry" icon="industry">

This stakeholder group consists of people with industry experience, ranging from CEOs and advisors to R&D specialists. We reached out to them to gain a market perspective and to prepare our product for potential industrial implementation. In addition, they also gave us feedback on pitching, business development and securing funding to continue the project.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Patients" icon="patients">

From the very beginning of EMPOWER, we knew that involving the people affected by PMOS would be essential to our project's success. We therefore spoke to [x] women from different countries, recruited through online channels such as Instagram and community group chats, about how living with PMOS influences their daily life. To broaden our pool as much as possible, we included participants with different phenotypes, age groups and countries of origin.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Advocates & Legislators" icon="legislation">

To highlight the lack of funding that PMOS and other female-specific conditions receive, we contacted representatives, legislators and community advocates. Our goal was to understand why this issue persists and how it could be addressed on a legislative level, and what we, as a student team, could do about it. To this end, we spoke with [x] people from different sectors, including community activists, legislators at EU level and advocates.

</StakeholderGroup>

</StakeholderGroups>

## Stakeholder Mapping

To see how our stakeholders impacted EMPOWER, we mapped each group on an influence-interest matrix, inspired by the iGEM Munich 2025 team. We placed every group twice: first based on our initial assumptions before the interviews in around July, then again in the beginning of October after all the interviews had been done. This dynamic approach made us look back on our journey of feedback, iteration and reflection, also showing how much this project and our views changed over the last couple of months through the influence of external voices. 

<StakeholderMatrix />

The **interest** scale reflects how engaged each group was with our project: whether they asked questions, offered follow-up meetings, or referred us to other people and resources. 

**Influence** reflects how much of a stakeholder group’s feedback we implemented or researched further, and how much of it changed the project. 

Notably, the people we spoke represent a small fraction of each field, and the placements are our own interpretation of their role in this project, not a view of the whole group.

## Feedback Cycle

<FeedbackCycle>

To ensure a sustainable and productive project development, we implemented an iterative Feedback Cycle with three steps: Listen, Analyse, Implement.

<FeedbackStep step="listen">

Before each interview, we researched the person we were speaking to in depth. We prepared personalized questions based on their experience, so we would get targeted feedback where we needed it most. Depending on the stakeholder, we focused on different aspects of the project. For example, with people without a scientific background, we simplified the theoretical part of the project as much as possible, and centered on information relevant to the conversation.

</FeedbackStep>

<FeedbackStep step="analyse">

We received a great deal of information, feedback and suggestions on how to improve our project. To keep a clear overview, we kept a detailed table documenting each stakeholder interview: why we reached out to that person, their association and field of expertise and what our key takeaways were.

The challenging part was deciding what was relevant and feasible to integrate. Tangibility was mainly based on budget, time limit or laboratory resources. Interview summaries were presented at our general meetings, where feedback was allocated to one of three outcomes:

<FeedbackOutcomes>

<FeedbackOutcome kind="active" title="Active implementation">

Information that is detrimental and is integrated directly into the project design.

</FeedbackOutcome>

<FeedbackOutcome kind="passive" title="Passive implementation">

Relevant and potentially useful information, but not enough resources to test or research it further.

</FeedbackOutcome>

<FeedbackOutcome kind="none" title="Not relevant">

Not relevant to our project.

</FeedbackOutcome>

</FeedbackOutcomes>

</FeedbackStep>

<FeedbackStep step="implement">

Feasible suggestions were integrated or researched further, closing the feedback loop. Often, the received feedback raised new questions. When we got stuck, we contacted new stakeholders and the cycle began again.

</FeedbackStep>

</FeedbackCycle>
