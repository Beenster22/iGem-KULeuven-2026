## Stakeholder Framework and Analysis

### Stakeholder Framework

To really understand the societal impact and feasibility of EMPOWER, we deliberately strived to think outside the norm when deciding whom to include as stakeholders. We aimed to reach out to a diverse representation of each stakeholder group to gather different perspectives and opinions. Beyond the technicalities and the science, it was particularly important to us to include non-academic views, patients, representatives of the PMOS community, legislators and healthcare professionals with varying background. 

We grouped our stakeholders into seven categories: healthcare professionals, scientific & technical experts, biotech entrepreneurs & industry, the general public, patients and advocates & legislators. Within each category, we aimed to reach a diverse group of people to gather as many different perspectives and insights as possible.

<StakeholderGroups>

<StakeholderGroup title="Healthcare Professionals" icon="healthcare">

Before engaging with patients, we first wanted to gain a solid understanding of the clinical facts of PMOS: its symptoms, diagnostic process, prevalence and first-line treatment. Therefore, we contacted healthcare professionals, in particular gynecologists or PMOS specialists. This stakeholder group consists of 6 doctors and specialists from different backgrounds, who offered a clinical perspective on PMOS and its current treatment availabilities.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Scientific & Technical Experts" icon="science">

To learn from their experience, we reached out to knowledgeable scientists, PIs, professors from academia and the industry who have previously worked on projects similar to ours. We knew from the start that introducing an engineered bacterium into an environment as complex as the gut was going to be challenging. We therefore sought out scientists who could provide us with the know-how on the technical aspects, methodological skills and also the current scientific landscape. In total, we consulted 13 people from the synthetic biology field, who helped strengthen our project on a technical level.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Biotech Entrepreneurs & Industry" icon="industry">

This stakeholder group consists of industry experts in the biotechnological sector, ranging from CEOs and advisors to R&D specialists. We reached out to 9 people to gain a market perspective and to prepare our product for potential industrial implementation. In addition, they also gave us feedback on pitching, business development, intellectual property protection and securing funding to continue the project.  

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="General Public" icon="public">

Genetic modifications remain a controversial topic in society. We wanted to get an idea how the public perceives synthetic biology in therapeutic application for conditions that currently have limited treatment options, such as PMOS. Therefore, we conducted a survey targeting all age groups above 18 and thereby reached [x] people.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Patients" icon="patients">

From the very beginning of EMPOWER, we knew that involving the people affected by PMOS would be essential to our project's success. We therefore spoke to [x] women from different countries, recruited through online channels such as Instagram and community group chats, about how living with PMOS influences their daily life. To broaden our pool as much as possible, we included participants with different phenotypes, age groups and countries of origin.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Advocates & Legislators" icon="legislation">

To highlight the lack of funding that PMOS and other female-specific conditions receive, we contacted representatives, legislators that contribute to gender-equality directives and community advocates. Our goal was to understand why this issue persists and how it could be addressed on a legislative level, and what we, as a student team, could do about it. To this end, we spoke with 4 people from different sectors, including community activists, legislators at EU level and advocates. 

</StakeholderGroup>

</StakeholderGroups>

## Feedback Cycle and Interviews

### Feedback Cycle

<FeedbackCycle>

To ensure a sustainable and productive project development, we implemented an iterative Feedback Cycle with three steps: Listen, Analyse, Implement.

<FeedbackStep step="listen">

Before each interview, we researched the person we were speaking to in depth. We prepared personalized questions based on their experience, so we would get targeted feedback where we needed it most. Depending on the stakeholder, we focused on different aspects of the project. For example, with people without a scientific background, we simplified the theoretical part of the project as much as possible, and centered on information relevant to the conversation.

<Interviewee name="Dr. Biljana Popovic Todorovic " role="Gynecologist & Head of IVF Clinic, Avala General Hospital" date="15 September 2026" basedIn="Belgrade, Serbia" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/biljana.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — We contacted Dr. Biljana Popovic Todorovic, who is Head of the IVF (In Vitro Fertilization) clinic at the Avala General Hospital in Belgrade, Serbia with international research and treatment experience to better understand the realities of IVF and of fertility care for women with PMOS. Additionally, we hoped she would provide us with a multinational view on the PMOS clinics and how they differ in treatment plans and patient care, considering her broad international work experience.

**What did we learn?** — She named age as the main limit on IVF success, because patients are getting older on average. She also explained how standard IVF differs from a gentler alternative, in vitro maturation (IVM). In IVF, hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to produce several eggs, which are collected once they are mature. This can cause ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), a swelling of the ovaries in reaction to the hormones. Doctors can keep this risk very low, for example by freezing all embryos for a later transfer, but the enlarged ovaries still cause discomfort. In IVM, the ovaries are not stimulated, or only briefly primed with hormones, so there is no risk of OHSS. The eggs are collected before they are mature and ripen in the lab instead.
She sees little difference in how women with PMOS are treated across countries. The key difference is how long it takes to receive treatment. Women whose main issue is infertility usually see a reproductive specialist first, and those with amenorrhoea (asbent or irregular menstruation), weight gain, obesity, acne or hirsutism see a reproductive endocrinologist first. In her experience, women with PMOS account for about 5–10% of her patients who struggle with fertility. She also mentioned that PMOS is stigmatized and poorly understood: many patients do not realize they have the condition or what it means.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Dr. Popovic Todorovic provided us with valuable insights on how IVF, which is a common treatment for infertility, and PMOS are looked at from a multinational perspective. From this, we continued to research how phenotype manifest differently in different regions of the world. 

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Dr. Ruben Vazquez-Uribe " slug="ruben-vazquez-uribe" role="Staff Scientist & Research Manager in Gut Microbiome, VIB-KU Leuven Center for Microbiology " date="02 April 2026" basedIn="Leuven, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/ruben.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — At the start of the project, we just concluded that we want to work on regulating the bile-acid metabolism through the gut bacterium *P. vulgatus* but lacked the experimental expertise to judge whether this was feasible. Therefore, we contacted Dr. Ruben Vazquez-Uribe, who is a research scientist and Head of Fermentations at the VIB-KU Leuven Center for Microbiology, for advice on engineering gut bacteria and microbiome research.

**What did we learn?** — Initially, Dr. Uribe advised us not to build our project on *P. vulgatus*, since it is not a model organism and its limited genetic tools could make the project too ambitious for the iGEM timeline. He pointed out that different BSH enzymes differ in substrate activity and should not be treated as equals and suggested investigating more efficient alternatives. Bile acids are chemically very similar and hard to measure, so he recommended LC-MS, chromatography or bile-acid-sensitive biosensors. He also raised CRISPR as a method to selectively kill *P. vulgatus*, with bacterial conjugation as a more realistic delivery method than phages. However, he emphasized that the main challenge would be delivering the CRISPR system into the bacterium. While phage-based delivery would likely be difficult to establish, bacterial conjugation could provide a more realistic alternative. He encouraged us looking into modifying disease-relevant molecules that are already present in the gut, rather than making the entire project dependent *P. vulgatus*. 

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — We compared the activities of different BSH enzymes and explored how we can incorporate *E. coli* as a model host instead of working directly in *P. vulgatus*. As a result, we transformed different <Link to="/results#bile-salt-deconjugation-by-15-bile-salt-hydrolases">BSH variants</Link> into *E. coli* to characterize them instead of doing it in *P. vulgatus* directly. We have also investigated alternative enzymes that could also affect bile-acid metabolism as advised but decided to continue working with BSH as our target. We also focused more on bacterial conjugation as a possible way to deliver genetic material into *P. vulgatus*. This led us to discuss how CRISPR could potentially be included in our system. This was one of many meetings with Dr. Uribe, who agreed to be our  co-Principal Investigator for the project. He kindly provided us with facilities of Raes laboratory such as anaerobic chambers, to investigate conjugation mechanisms with *P. vulgatus* and to fully develop our toolkit. 
Besides incredibly valuable scientific advice provided by Ruben, another way he shaped our IHP was through his professional network. Considering his expertise is reflecting our project, his network was invaluable to shape our IHP progress.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Prof. Laurie Comstock" slug="laurie-comstock" role="Professor for Microbiology, University of Chicago" date="27 May 2026" basedIn="Chicago, USA">

<Interviewee name="Prof. Jef Rozenski" role="Professor, Faculty for Pharmaceutical Sciences, Ku Leuven" date="10 July 2026" basedIn="Chicago, USA" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/jef-rozenski.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Dr. Jef Rozenski is a professor at KU Leuven University and has specializations in biomedical analysis, LC/MS, and Mass Spectrometry of biomolecules. As our plate-based HALO assay could only report bile salt tolerance, we also wanted to be able to measure conjugation directly. Dr. Rozenski, who runs mass spectrometry at the Rega Institute, and we approached him to ask whether LC-MS could be used for quantifying conjugated bile salts.

**What did we learn?** — He confirmed that the measurements we were planning to do were feasible, and he told us what to send him, and in what state. In the week of 27 July, he ran pure a bile salt mixture to set the instrument up and find the gradient that resolves all three targeted standards (GUDCA, TUDCA, and UDCA). We also learned some limits of the output data, such as that it establishes identity rather than a calibration series, meaning that the output is peak areas and ratios, not concentrations.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — We built the liquid-culture assay with his aid and ran a second round at 0.1% (instead of 0.5%) with the two bile salts (GUDCA and TUDCA) dissolved separately, so each substrate could be tracked on its own. His measurements became the quantitative backbone of the BSH work. The method set-up can be found as it is documented in BSA-06 or in our <Link to="/protocols#lc-ms-quantification-of-bile-acid-deconjugation">protocols list</Link>.

</Interviewee>

**What did we learn?** — She explained what is and is not patentable in our project:
Testing known promoters in another bacterium and finding that they work is not patentable. Using public promoters for something interesting, or finding unexpected behavior, can be. For example, if four or five known promoters are all strong and we find one that is not, that could be patentable.
Most DNA sequences are public, but identifying and annotating a sequence with new information or a different application can be patentable. The hurdle is the inventive step, and combining pieces from the literature can also be patentable. Nucleic acids themselves cannot be protected.
Because we found our BSH sequences in NCBI, others could find them too, and similar sequences do not necessarily have the same function. Her advice was to protect the link between the bacteria and the disease, which in our case is the connection between bile acids, inflammation and the overabundance of *P. vulgatus*.
She also advised us on timing. We can file a first application now, before we disclose anything at iGEM, and then have 12 months to add data and build a broader international (PCT) application. In certain countries there is a so-called grace period, which would allow us to publicize the content and not be considered the first point of claiming a new invention. Lastly, she advised us to hold back on revealing how to engineer the strain and to protect the core inventive concept.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — We integrated her explanation of what is patentable and what is not in our IP and commercialization strategy. The biggest takeaway from this meeting was to not reveal any BSH sequences that we worked with, as this could be valuable for a patent application in the near future. Finally, we also learned to consider the cost of filing a patent application.

## Public

Over the months, the aim of EMPOWER has manifested into developing a genetically engineered live biotherapeutic product (LBP) to alleviate severely affected women with PMOS of their symptoms. However, we know that not everyone has the same opinion on synthetic biology as us.

Since genetically engineered organisms (GMOs) remain a controversial topic of conversation, we wanted to understand what shapes public attitudes toward genetically engineered microbial therapeutics, and whether these attitudes shift for diseases (such as PMOS) that currently have limited or no effective treatment options. We included topics on perceived risk and willingness to use such a treatment. The survey also collected demographic information and level of education to explore whether attitudes and trust vary across educational backgrounds. In addition, it measured general trust in science and asked respondents directly what information or reassurances would increase their trust or willingness to consider such a treatment.

<Interviewee name="Prof. Dr. Ricardo Azziz" role="Executive Director, Foundation for Research and Education Excellence; Professor, Obstetrics & Gynecology & Medicine, University of Alabama at Birmingham" date="26 August 2026" basedIn="Birmingham, AL, USA" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/ricardo-azziz.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Prof. Dr. Ricardo Azziz is an endocrinologist and PMOS researcher who has studied the research funding gap and economic burden of PMOS. We contacted him to better understand why PMOS remains underfunded despite its significant impact, and what we as young researchers can do to improve it.

**What did we learn?** — He explained that although PMOS research funding has increased, it remains limited compared with other chronic conditions. He highlighted that PMOS spans reproductive, metabolic, and mental health, but research has historically focused on reproductive aspects and the bias remains. Furthermore, limited funding from private investors often discourages researchers from entering the field, creating a cycle of underfunding and insufficient research, which leads to missing treatments and diagnoses. He stressed that this is a pipeline issue that needs to be addressed with awareness, education and funding opportunities. Additionally, he advised us to reach out to PCOS Challenge and other advocacy groups.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Prof. Azziz’s feedback greatly influenced our education and awareness efforts on the funding gap. Based on the meeting, we integrated a literature analysis on PMOS in Europe over the last twenty years compared to conditions with a similar prevalence to showcase the lack of research on PMOS. In addition, we strengthened our education and outreach efforts on the topic to make the public more aware of the problem and what needs to be done to close it. Finally, we identified and reached out to patient advocacy groups he recommended, such as PCOS Challenge.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Juliane Marie Neiiendam" role="Vice President of the Civil Society Organizations Group, European Economic and Social Committee" date="07 September 2026" basedIn="Brussels, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/juliane-marie-neiiendam.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — Juliane Marie Neiiendam is Vice President of the Civil Society Organizations Group of the European Economic and Social Committee (EESC) and head rapporteur for its Gender Equality Plan 2026–2030. We contacted her to understand how women's health is addressed in EU policy, how legislation impacts funding and how a student project can support it.

**What did we learn?** — She told us that the new plan includes health perspectives and focuses heavily on implementation compared to the previous plan of 2020–2025. Gender equality cannot be discussed without health, and investing in women's health is also an investment in the future labor market. Women's conditions are under-researched and wrongly treated as niche, although they represent more than half of the population.
For funding, she explained that society splits into idealists and finance-minded people, so a project must appeal to both. She advised telling three audiences three different stories: affected women need to know someone is thinking about them, healthcare professionals need to know what can and cannot be done, and policymakers need to understand the scale of the problem and what they can do. Our message should be concrete: what the problem is, why it matters and what difference we make, with the consequences, the gap and the number of people affected. **Why did we reach out to them?** — Dr. Danko Relic, who is a physician, Executive Director of APHEA (Agency for Public Health Education and Accreditation) and member of the EESC, was referred to us by Juliane Marie Neiiendam. We reached out to get a public health perspective on PMOS.

**What did we learn?** — Dr. Relic was familiar with the EU Gender Equality Plan but not involved in creating it. Coordination between countries and data exchange improved after COVID, but health has dropped from the top of the EU agenda since the war in Ukraine. Overall, he considers the EU system thorough because it takes into account all 27 countries and a wide range of perspectives, and he rated its social rights and insurance as the best in the world. He contrasted this with the US, where public health degrees are no longer recognized.
On regulation, he said getting medical devices onto the European market is slow and burdened with documentation. As rapporteur for IVF medical devices, he saw this first-hand. The Commission has proposed simplification, but patient safety is never compromised.
