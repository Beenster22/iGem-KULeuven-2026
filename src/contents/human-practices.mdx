## 1. Introduction


Like many other parts of this project, Integrated Human Practices (IHP) did not come easily to us. At first, we simply understood it as reaching out to people and talking about our project. We did not know what "Integrated" should look like, and we quickly realized that there is no blueprint.

We therefore began with a set of guiding values of EMPOWER: **inclusivity, patient-centered care, scientific integrity, and sustainability & entrepreneurship.** All of them are built on an open, curious, and inclusive environment in which science can flourish.

**EMPOWER's values:**

<ValueCards>

<ValueCard title="Integrated people-centered care">

A central piece in the project's design was the WHO's 2016 framework on **integrated people-centered care**[ref: World Health Organization. (n.d.). Integrated people-centered care. Retrieved October 21, 2026, from [https://www.who.int/health-topics/integrated-people-centered-care#tab=tab_2](https://www.who.int/health-topics/integrated-people-centered-care#tab=tab_2)]. This approach aims to globally organize research, medical treatment, and hospital facilities around patients and their needs. Its key recommendations include:

- Engaging and empowering people and communities
- Strengthening governance and accountability
- Reorienting the model of care
- Coordinating services within and across sectors
- Creating an enabling environment

Guided by this framework, we built our project around patient and public involvement (PPI). Since women with Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) have been and still continue to be **systemically overlooked** in research and funding we aimed to put them at the center and highlight this problem. We wanted to encourage them by developing this project and show that research will catch up eventually.

</ValueCard>

<ValueCard title="Inclusivity">

We built our team and our project on the belief that diversity in academic and professional backgrounds and in countries of origin creates an environment where **originality and creativity** can thrive. We are committed to respecting and appreciating everyone's religion, gender, sexual orientation, and origin. This applies not only to our own team, but also to external advisors, volunteers, and members of our stakeholder groups.

</ValueCard>

<ValueCard title="Scientific integrity">

Our scientific values are based on collaboration and communication. Science involves more failure than success, and on an iGEM team, not everyone has the same level of experience. Achieving great results therefore requires an open and accepting atmosphere where team members can learn, develop, and contribute meaningfully. Throughout the project we emphasized hard, dedicated work, but above all curiosity about science and the unknown.

</ValueCard>

<ValueCard title="Sustainability & entrepreneurship">

Sustainability was not a central driver of our research, but it shaped how we think about EMPOWER beyond the competition. Women with PMOS need solutions that last, so we considered how our work could stay accessible, affordable and viable in the long term, and how it could keep benefiting patients after our project ends. We discuss this in more detail in the <Link to="/entrepreneurship#phase-4-long-term-impact">Long-term impact</Link> section of our entrepreneurship page.

</ValueCard>

</ValueCards>

Based on these values, we asked ourselves which people we could reach out to, and how their feedback would help us live up to them. Integrating that feedback was not always straightforward, but we did our best and relied on creative team brainstorming.

Our IHP page has it all: science, entrepreneurship, frustration, injustice, and a lot of consent forms. We hope to inspire upcoming teams and young scientists to look beyond the scope of research. Science is much more than what happens at the lab bench.

## 2. Stakeholder Framework and Analysis

### 2.1 Stakeholder Framework

To really understand the societal impact and feasibility of EMPOWER, we deliberately strived to think outside the norm when deciding whom to include as stakeholders. We aimed to reach out to a diverse representation of each stakeholder group to gather different perspectives and opinions, creating an environment in which creativity and innovation can successfully thrive. Beyond the technicalities and science, it was particularly important to us to include non-academic views, patients, representatives of the PMOS community, legislators, and healthcare professionals with varying backgrounds. 
We grouped our stakeholders into six categories: healthcare professionals, scientific & technical experts, biotech entrepreneurs & industry, the general public, patients, and advocates & legislators. Within each category, we aimed to reach a diverse group of people to gather as many different perspectives and insights as possible. 

<StakeholderGroups>

<StakeholderGroup title="Healthcare Professionals" icon="healthcare">

Before engaging with patients, we first wanted to gain a solid understanding of the clinical facts of PMOS: its symptoms, diagnostic process, prevalence, and first-line treatment. Therefore, we contacted healthcare professionals, in particular gynecologists or PMOS specialists. This stakeholder group consists of 6 doctors and specialists from different backgrounds, who offered a clinical perspective on PMOS and its current treatment availabilities. 

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Scientific & Technical Experts" icon="science">

To learn from their experience, we reached out to knowledgeable scientists, PIs, professors from academia and the industry who have previously worked on projects similar to ours. We knew from the start that introducing an engineered bacterium - even if it’s a commensal one - into an environment as complex as the gut was going to be challenging. We therefore sought out scientists who could provide us with the know-how on the technical aspects, methodological skills and also the current scientific landscape. In total, we consulted 13 people from the synthetic biology field, who helped strengthen our project on a technical level. 

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Biotech Entrepreneurs & Industry" icon="industry">

This stakeholder group consists of industry experts in the biotechnological sector, ranging from CEOs and advisors to R&D specialists. We reached out to 9 people to gain a market perspective and to prepare our product for potential industrial implementation. In addition, they also gave us feedback on pitching, business development, intellectual property protection, and securing funding to continue the project.  

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="General Public" icon="public">

Genetic modifications remain a controversial topic in society. We wanted to get an idea of how the public perceives synthetic biology in therapeutic application for conditions that currently have limited treatment options, such as PMOS. Therefore, we conducted a survey targeting all age groups above 18 and thereby reached 176 people.

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Patients" icon="patients">

From the very beginning of EMPOWER, we knew that involving the people affected by PMOS would be essential to our project’s success. We therefore spoke to 13 women from different countries, recruited through online channels such as Instagram and community group chats, about how living with PMOS influences their daily life. To broaden our pool as much as possible, we aimed to include participants with different phenotypes, age groups, and countries of origin. 

</StakeholderGroup>

<StakeholderGroup title="Advocates & Legislators" icon="legislation">

To highlight the lack of funding that PMOS and other female-specific conditions receive, we contacted representatives, legislators that contribute to gender-equality directives and community advocates. Our goal was to understand why this issue persists and how it could be addressed on a legislative level, and what we, as a student team, could do about it. To this end, we spoke with 4 people from different sectors, including community activists, legislators at EU level and advocates. 

</StakeholderGroup>

</StakeholderGroups>

This dynamic approach made us look back on our journey of feedback, iteration and reflection, also showing how much this project and our views changed over the last couple of months through the influence of external voices.

## 3. Feedback Cycle and Interviews

<FeedbackCycle>

To ensure a sustainable and productive project development, we implemented an iterative Feedback Cycle with three steps: Listen, Analyze, Implement.

<FeedbackStep step="listen">

Before each interview, we researched the person we were speaking to in depth. We prepared personalized questions based on their experience, so we would get targeted feedback where we needed it most. Depending on the stakeholder, we focused on different aspects of the project. For example, we tried to simplify theoretical details for people without a scientific background and centered on information relevant to the conversation.

<ResourceDownload
  href="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/pdfs/example-questions-stakeholder-groups.pdf"
  title="Example stakeholder questions"
  type="PDF"
>
  Example questions we prepared for each stakeholder group.
</ResourceDownload>

</FeedbackStep>

<FeedbackStep step="analyse">

We received a great deal of information, feedback and suggestions on how to improve our project. To keep a clear overview, we kept a detailed table documenting each stakeholder interview: why we reached out to that person, their association and field of expertise and what our key takeaways were.

The challenging part was deciding what was relevant and feasible to integrate. Tangibility was mainly based on budget, time limit or laboratory resources. Interview summaries were presented at our general meetings, where feedback was allocated to one of three outcomes:

<FeedbackOutcomes>

<FeedbackOutcome kind="active" title="Active implementation">

Information that is detrimental and is integrated directly into the project design.

</FeedbackOutcome>

<FeedbackOutcome kind="passive" title="Passive implementation">

Relevant and potentially useful information, but not enough resources to test or research it further.

</FeedbackOutcome>

<FeedbackOutcome kind="none" title="Not relevant">

Not relevant to our project.

</FeedbackOutcome>

</FeedbackOutcomes>

</FeedbackStep>

<FeedbackStep step="implement">

Feasible suggestions were integrated or researched further, closing the feedback loop. Often, the received feedback raised new questions. When we got stuck, we contacted new stakeholders and the cycle began again.

</FeedbackStep>

</FeedbackCycle>

<Interviewee name="Dr. Biljana Popovic Todorovic " role="Gynecologist & Head of IVF Clinic, Avala General Hospital" date="15 September 2026" basedIn="Belgrade, Serbia" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/biljana.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — We contacted Dr. Biljana Popovic Todorovic, who is Head of the IVF (In Vitro Fertilization) clinic at the Avala General Hospital in Belgrade, Serbia with international research and treatment experience to better understand the realities of IVF and of fertility care for women with PMOS. Additionally, we hoped she would provide us with a multinational view on the PMOS clinics and how they differ in treatment plans and patient care, considering her broad international work experience.

**What did we learn?** — She named age as the main limit on IVF success, because patients are getting older on average. She also explained how standard IVF differs from a gentler alternative, *in vitro* maturation (IVM). In IVF, hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to produce several eggs, which are collected once they are mature. This can cause ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), a swelling of the ovaries in reaction to the hormones. Doctors can keep this risk very low, for example by freezing all embryos for a later transfer, but the enlarged ovaries still cause discomfort. In IVM, the ovaries are not stimulated, or only briefly primed with hormones, so there is no risk of OHSS. The eggs are collected before they are mature and ripen in the lab instead.
She sees little difference in how women with PMOS are treated across countries. The key difference is how long it takes to receive treatment. Women whose main issue is infertility usually see a reproductive specialist first, and those with amenorrhoea (asbent or irregular menstruation), weight gain, obesity, acne or hirsutism see a reproductive endocrinologist first. In her experience, women with PMOS account for about 5–10% of her patients who struggle with fertility. She also mentioned that PMOS is stigmatized and poorly understood: many patients do not realize they have the condition or what it means.

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — Dr. Popovic Todorovic provided us with valuable insights on how IVF, which is a common treatment for infertility, and PMOS are looked at from a multinational perspective. From this, we continued to research how phenotype manifest differently in different regions of the world.

<Interviewee name="Dr. Ruben Vazquez-Uribe " slug="ruben-vazquez-uribe" role="Staff Scientist & Research Manager in Gut Microbiome, VIB-KU Leuven Center for Microbiology " date="02 April 2026" basedIn="Leuven, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/ruben.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — At the start of the project, we just concluded that we want to work on regulating the bile-acid metabolism through the gut bacterium *P. vulgatus* but lacked the experimental expertise to judge whether this was feasible. Therefore, we contacted Dr. Ruben Vazquez-Uribe, who is a research scientist and Head of Fermentations at the VIB-KU Leuven Center for Microbiology, for advice on engineering gut bacteria and microbiome research.

**What did we learn?** — Initially, Dr. Uribe advised us not to build our project on *P. vulgatus*, since it is not a model organism and its limited genetic tools could make the project too ambitious for the iGEM timeline. He pointed out that different BSH enzymes differ in substrate activity and should not be treated as equals and suggested investigating more efficient alternatives. Bile acids are chemically very similar and hard to measure, so he recommended LC-MS, chromatography or bile-acid-sensitive biosensors. He also raised CRISPR as a method to selectively kill *P. vulgatus*, with bacterial conjugation as a more realistic delivery method than phages. However, he emphasized that the main challenge would be delivering the CRISPR system into the bacterium. While phage-based delivery would likely be difficult to establish, bacterial conjugation could provide a more realistic alternative. He encouraged us looking into modifying disease-relevant molecules that are already present in the gut, rather than making the entire project dependent *P. vulgatus*. 

**How did we integrate their feedback?** — We compared the activities of different BSH enzymes and explored how we can incorporate *E. coli* as a model host instead of working directly in *P. vulgatus*. As a result, we transformed different <Link to="/results#bile-salt-deconjugation-by-15-bile-salt-hydrolases">BSH variants</Link> into *E. coli* to characterize them instead of doing it in *P. vulgatus* directly. We have also investigated alternative enzymes that could also affect bile-acid metabolism as advised but decided to continue working with BSH as our target. We also focused more on bacterial conjugation as a possible way to deliver genetic material into *P. vulgatus*. This led us to discuss how CRISPR could potentially be included in our system. This was one of many meetings with Dr. Uribe, who agreed to be our  co-Principal Investigator for the project. He kindly provided us with facilities of Raes laboratory such as anaerobic chambers, to investigate conjugation mechanisms with *P. vulgatus* and to fully develop our toolkit. 
Besides incredibly valuable scientific advice provided by Ruben, another way he shaped our IHP was through his professional network. Considering his expertise is reflecting our project, his network was invaluable to shape our IHP progress.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Dr. Matthew Herdman" slug="matthew-herdman" role="Postdoctoral Researcher, Govers Lab, KU Leuven" date="28 April 2026" basedIn="Leuven, Belgium" image = "https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/ihp/stakeholder-pictures/matthew-herdman.avif">

**Why did we reach out to them?** — One of our members met Dr. Matthew Herdman per chance. After introducing EMPOWER, it turned out he actually works with *P. vulgatus* as part of postdoctoral research project in the Govers Lab in the Department of Biology at KU Leuven. 

**What did we learn?** — Dr. Herdman supplied the starting point for the whole project: P. vulgatus DSM 1447 and E. coli DH5α the E. coli S17-1 λpir donor strain carrying pRham_KOFP7 that we used in every conjugation, hemin and gentamicin. He also gave us access to the fluorescence microscope at the Bioscience Center, and he ran and returned the imaging himself. Our conjugation work is built on his Bacteroides protocol (day 0 to day 3), and we followed his sample preparation for fluorescence microscopy. Furthermore, he advised us on results, interpretation, and following experiment planning, thereby playing a key part in our Wet Lab’s success. He also pointed us to literature. Among the papers he showed us was work from Laurie Comstock’s lab, which is how we came to know her research.

The medium work was built on his hemin and vitamin K1 reasoning, which fed directly into the <Link to="/results#growth-curves-and-medium-comparison">factorial supplementation study</Link>. Based on his advice and our own results on <Link to="/results#a-characterized-promoter-library-for-p-vulgatus">promotor screening</Link>, we could decide that KOFP-7 wasn’t a good reporter gene to be used in P. vulgatus. The primer explanation sent us back to redesign for a gene shared across the strain panel. He also provided hemin and gentamicin and did <Link to="/engineering#fluorescence-microscopy-conjugation">fluorescence microscopy</Link> with us in the Bioscience Center.

</Interviewee>

<Interviewee name="Prof. Laurie Comstock" slug="laurie-comstock" role="Professor for Microbiology, University of Chicago" date="27 May 2026" basedIn="Chicago, USA">

## 4. Public Survey

### 4.1 Introduction

**Live Biotherapeutic Products (LBPs)**, defined as drugs containing live organisms that are used to prevent or treat a disease, are already being used for a variety of conditions and form the core of our therapeutic approach. EMPOWER aims to develop engineered LBPs, a type of genetically modified organism (GMO), to restore the bile salt pool of PMOS patients and relieve them of most severe symptoms. However, we know that not everyone has the same opinion on synthetic biology as us.

Societal perceptions of LBPs and GMOs are heavily influenced by unfamiliarity with the concept, and patients tend to be more willing to use such treatments when they are recommended by healthcare professionals. As more novel LBPs and gene-based treatments have reached the market over the last decade, understanding public views on the matter has become increasingly important. In the rapidly evolving field of microbiome engineering, public views on engineered LBPs remain largely unknown and are likely to shift as the field advances. To gain a better understanding of how LBPs and GMOs are currently perceived, and **how willing people are to use them**, EMPOWER designed a survey titled: "Genetically engineered organisms to the rescue: how the general public views genetic engineering for diseases with limited treatment options."

In our survey, we included topics on **route of administration, perceived risk, and willingness to use treatment**. The survey also collected demographic information and level of education to explore whether attitudes and trust vary across educational backgrounds. In addition, it measured general trust in science and asked respondents directly **what information or reassurances would increase their trust** or willingness to consider such treatment.

We submitted the survey to the Privacy and Ethical Review Committee (PRET) on the 31st of July 2026. After minor revisions, we received **formal approval** on the 13th of October under the reference number G-2026-11849-R2(MIN) to publish the questionnaire online on the platform Limesurvey.org.

<ExpandableText title="Discussion and statistical analysis">

Without statistical analysis and only observational graph analysis, respondents with a biology-related background were clearly more willing to accept treatment with a genetically modified bacterium. In contrast, respondents with a connection to a chronic condition did not show a clear difference from those without one, so further statistical analysis is needed to determine whether a relationship exists. Respondents concerned about long-term effects showed a lower tendency to accept the treatment and more often answered "Neutral". Respondents who trust health authorities showed a clear tendency to accept the treatment, while those who do not mostly answered "Neutral". Additionally, people who worried about long-term effects were less sure about the treatment. More of them answered "Neutral" and fewer "Agreed" or "Strongly Agreed".

However, these observations are based only on visual inspection of the graphs. To test them quantitatively, we performed a bivariate analysis using a two-way contingency table analysis. This test determines whether two categorical variables are statistically independent or associated with each other. In our case, it shows whether belonging to a particular group, such as having a biology-related background, is related to accepting treatment with a genetically modified bacterium. We applied this contingency table analysis to all four comparisons. The dependence test is widely used in medical survey research to assess whether two variables are related, with the p-value serving as the main parameter for deciding whether an observed association is statistically significant.

As we conducted two-way contingency table analysis to every associative question, we have tested the values with resultant P-values where we accepted the threshold of P = 0.05 as the statistical significance. Hence, p-values below the threshold value indicate a statistically significant association between the variables.

- **Biology-related degree:** The question "Is biological/medical training associated with accepting genetically engineered bacterial treatment?" gives P = 0.014 meaning respondents with relevant scientific backgrounds display greater acceptance toward usage of LBPs.
- **Personal experience with chronic illness:** The question "Do people with a chronic condition or who know someone with one express greater willingness to accept engineered LBPs?" gives P = 0.436 meaning there is no statistically significant association with treatment acceptance and experience of chronic illness.
- **Concerns about long-term effects:** The question "Are long-term safety concerns associated with lower expressed acceptance of genetically modified bacterial therapy?" gives P = 0.005 meaning safety concerns are significantly associated with lowered acceptance of LBP treatment.
- **Trust in health authorities:** The question "Is general trust in clinicians, scientists and health authorities associated with acceptance of a modified bacterial therapy?" gives P = 0.023 meaning trust in health authorities are significantly associated with acceptance of LBP treatment.

### 4.3 Conclusion

Overall, our survey suggests that public attitudes toward engineered LBPs are more positive than we assumed. Although the survey was shared mainly through student groups, peers, friends and family within a short time before the wiki deadline, our sample was more homogeneous than intended; we still had 176 responses where about half of it was not field related. While most respondents (76%) would accept treatment with a live genetically modified bacterium, 84% would consider an LBP for a hormonal condition such as PMOS with no effective treatment. Respondents were a little bit more divided on the issues of long-term safety and on whether LBPs should only be used if conventional methods fail. Our two-way contingency table analysis by using P-values showed that acceptance of genetically engineered bacterial treatment was significantly associated with a biology-related background, trust in health authorities, and a lack of concern about long-term effects. This survey taught us that acceptance of LBPs depends less on personal experience with disease and more on knowledge and trust. As we integrate our findings into our future Human Practices, we will use these insights to guide how we communicate about our therapy: openly, with evidence on its long-term safety, and together with the healthcare professionals people trust most. We also aim to extend this survey to a larger and more heterogeneous group, so that our findings better reflect the general public.

#### Misconceptions and stigma

Many patients said the hardest thing to explain about PMOS is that it is much more than “bad periods”. People tend to see it either as a fertility problem or as a cosmetic one, picturing the most extreme symptoms like facial hair or weight gain. Generally, it seems like PMOS has been perceived as black-and-white. In cases where a woman did not display the typical symptoms, PMOS diagnosis came as a surprise to people. But it doesn’t stop there: some symptoms seem to be neglected or misdiagnosed under the assumption that healthy weight equals lack of PMOS. Others have never heard of it at all.

Our interviewees described PMOS as something that affects the whole body and mind for life, including anxiety, mood swings and feeling let down by their own body. Several also remembered the myths they grew up with, such as PMOS being linked to poor hygiene or meaning you can never have children. A general awkwardness around periods meant symptoms were not taken seriously.

When asked about online resources, many found the available information to be chaotic and contradictory. Especially on social media applications like Instagram, PMOS has gained a lot of traction in the last years. While this is a positive aspect in the first place, it also causes a lot of misinformation to spread.

Several patients thought the recent name change from polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) to PMOS to be validating, because it finally shows that the condition is about far more than the ovaries. Many wished PMOS was addressed in schools as a real endocrine condition to prevent years of delayed diagnosis.

> “PMOS is a very invisible condition, a lot of the pain is not evident for outsiders.”

#### Willingness to try our genetically engineered LBP

When asked whether they would consider an engineered LBP like ours, no one rejected the idea, and most were open to trying it. In return, they needed to be able to trust it. That meant approval by regulators, a doctor who could explain how it works, what the side effects are, and how it would help. A capsule was the clear favorite and most were happy to take it daily, though some would prefer something less frequent. Those who talked about prices suggested around €10–30 a month, ideally covered by insurance.

### PMOS and SLE: comparing research volume against disease prevalence

Research money does not simply follow need. It follows visibility, structure and advocacy. Women’s health receives only 5% of global R&D funding: 4% goes to women’s cancers and 1% to all other conditions that affect women disproportionately or differently[^fund-nature]. Within that small share, PMOS receives several times less funding than diseases that are far less common[^fund-brakta].

#### 6.1 What the gap looks like

Between 2006 and 2015, PMOS research received $215.1 million, compared with $454.4 million for rheumatoid arthritis (RA), $609.5 million for tuberculosis (TB), and $773.8 million for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)[^fund-brakta]. Yet the global prevalence of these conditions is only 0.24% for RA, 0.13% for TB and 0.044% for SLE[^fund-gbd][^fund-who-tb][^fund-tian]. The gap persists in newer data: from 2016 to 2022, PMOS received $31.8 million a year, against $262.4 million for RA and $420.4 million for SLE[^fund-tilton]. In summary, PMOS receives significantly less funding than other conditions with similar degrees of morbidity and negative impact on quality of life (QOL), and similar or lower mortality and prevalence[^fund-brakta].

Following <Link to="/human-practices#ricardo-azziz">Prof. Azziz</Link>’s recommendation, we compared publications on PMOS and SLE in Europe and the US to get an idea of how the funding gap is reflected in the number of articles being published.

<ExpandableText title="How we did this analysis">

Figure 1 compares the number of PubMed papers on PMOS and SLE for each of the 43 European countries and the United States. Both conditions were searched with the same method: a Title/Abstract search combined (using OR) with a MeSH Major Topic search, adding "OR" with an affiliation for the country[^fund-pubmed]. The United States is so far ahead of every other country that it compresses the bars for Europe, which makes the country-level differences difficult to view the results. Figure 2 shows Europe on its own. The pattern holds even among Europe's largest research nations: Germany has 2,796 SLE papers against 497 on PMOS, France 2,699 against 522, and the United Kingdom 4,620 against 2,011.

To test whether this difference is statistically meaningful, we used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which is the standard non-parametric alternative to a paired t-test. It suits this data for two reasons. First, each country provides one PMOS count and one SLE count, so the values are naturally paired. Second, the counts are strongly skewed, ranging from zero to several thousand with the United Kingdom as a clear outlier, which rules out a test that assumes a normal distribution. We ran the test in RStudio on the paired counts of the 43 countries and found that PMOS and SLE differ significantly across the European countries (p = 2.472 × 10<sup>−6</sup>). Adding the United States as a 44th pair made the result stronger, lowering the p-value to 1.541 × 10<sup>−6</sup>.

</ExpandableText>

PMOS is therefore not only underfunded but also extremely underresearched. Across Europe and in the United States, SLE, which affects far fewer people than PMOS, has the larger published literature. The statistics confirm that this difference is significant.

In conclusion the gap exists not only in the funding, but also in the research.

#### 6.2 Why the gap exists

<GapReasons>

<GapReason title="Structure" icon="structure">

The first reason is structure. PMOS affects metabolism, reproduction and mental health, but it was first described as a reproductive condition, and most funders still see it that way[^fund-azziz]. The reproductive institute NICHD still supplied 44.8% of PMOS funding in 2016–2022 (down from 68.4%), while institutes focused on metabolism, cardiovascular disease and mental health have shown limited interest[^fund-brakta][^fund-tilton].

</GapReason>

<GapReason title="Pipeline" icon="pipeline">

Prof. Azziz explained that the second issue is the pipeline. Essentially, very few fundamental scientists work on PMOS. The field is mostly clinicians and translational researchers, and the number of young investigators entering stays consistently low[^fund-azziz]. Fewer researchers mean fewer grant applications, and less funding pushes researchers into other fields, a self-reinforcing cycle of underinvestment[^fund-brakta][^fund-tilton][^fund-azziz]. Finally, this leads to underdiagnosing and insufficient treatment options.

</GapReason>

<GapReason title="Stigma" icon="stigma">

Stigma compounds funding disparities for conditions that mainly affect women[^fund-nature]. For patients, this shows up as delay: late diagnosis, under-diagnosis and normalised symptoms all reduce the urgency felt by funders[^fund-tilton]. RA and SLE, by contrast, benefited from mature advocacy networks[^fund-tilton].

</GapReason>

</GapReasons>

#### The bigger picture: gender inequality

The PMOS funding gap is a symptom of a wider pattern. <Link to="/human-practices#danko-relic">Dr. Relic</Link> told us that medical training is still built around a default patient: a man of 70 kg and 175 cm. Women’s conditions are treated as niche, even though women make up more than half the population[^fund-nature][^fund-neiiendam].

> “You can’t talk about gender equality without talking about women’s health.” – <Link to="/human-practices#juliane-marie-neiiendam">Juliane Marie Neiiendam</Link>

#### 6.3 What is being done

Funding is moving in the right direction. Prof. Azziz and Mr. Patterson described how the efforts of advocacy groups such as PCOS Challenge (USA) and Verity (UK) to raise awareness, support patients and work with young investigators, push multinational actions like the name change. Collaboration and education also actively contribute to fighting the stigma around the condition. NIH funding for PMOS has risen by about 48% and now comes from 16 institutes instead of mostly one[^fund-tilton].

Overall, there has been a shift in private investigators as well. <Link to="/human-practices#johan-van-hylckama-vlieg">Dr. van Hylckama Vlieg</Link> noted that investors’ attitude towards women’s health has changed dramatically, and large companies such as Merck Healthcare are getting involved[^fund-vhv]. A McKinsey Health Institute report estimates that closing the women’s health gap could add at least $1 trillion a year to the global economy by 2040[^fund-neiiendam][^fund-mckinsey].

Finally, funding can be dramatically redirected through legislative regulations. The European Economic and Social Committee has adopted a new <a href="https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/policies/justice-and-fundamental-rights/gender-equality/gender-equality-strategy_en#gender-equality-strategy-2026-2030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Gender Equality plan for 2026–2030</a> in March of 2026, which not only targets funding, but also gender-based cyberviolence and gaps in employment. This framework has been adopted in <a href="https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/strategy/strategy-research-and-innovation/democracy-and-rights/gender-equality-research-and-innovation_en#gender-equality-in-horizon-europe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Horizon Europe</a>, which is the EU’s main funding program for innovation and research.

#### 6.4 What we can do

<Link to="/human-practices#juliane-marie-neiiendam">Ms. Neiiendam</Link>, who is the head rapporteur for the gender equality plan, explained that it matters how we frame our project in this complex network. Affected women need to know someone is thinking about them, healthcare professionals need to know what can and cannot be done, and policymakers need to understand the scale of the problem and how to act. Because society is split between idealists and those looking for a return, the message must appeal to both[^fund-neiiendam].

Another big component is <Link to="/science-communication">science communication and outreach</Link>, she said. By speaking about our project, which targets PMOS and actively highlights the inequality, to the public at events, to experts in the field, we are directly contributing to tightening the net in research, funding and advocacy.

#### 6.5Conclusion

Through our conversations with legislators, doctors and industry experts we have learned that the funding gap in PMOS and women’s health remains, driven by structure, a thin research pipeline and limited visibility. Funding has grown alongside advocacy, but the gap is far from closed[^fund-tilton]. It requires funding that attracts investigators, awareness among patients and young scientists, <Link to="/human-practices#juliane-marie-neiiendam">Ms. Neiiendam</Link> said: “People are much more aware of the gap than they were 5 years ago, but its going to take about 5 more to see a visible shift.”[^fund-ipsos]
