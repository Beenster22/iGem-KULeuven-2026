## 1. Risk Identification and Mitigation during Experimentation

EMPOWER treats safety with the highest priority from the start. The experimental pipeline involved
*Escherichia coli* DH5α and S17-1 λpir for cloning and conjugation, and *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, a
human gut commensal, all classified as Risk Group 1. Our work was divided between two labs: Prof.
Vitor B. Pinheiro's Lab and Prof. Jeroen Raes's lab under the guidance of our PI Dr. Ruben
Vazquez-Uribe, both in the Rega Institute in KU Leuven's Gasthuisberg. Each lab hosted separate
experimentation where a **risk analysis was written and reviewed by a representative** with the
**KU Leuven HSE Department before wet-lab work began and were valid from 1 July to 30 September
2026**. Risks are outlined and **addressed in-depth in every protocol** developed by our team and
represent our commitment to safety.

We followed the Code of Good
Microbiological Practice and KU Leuven containment guidelines. Frédérick Coosemans and Ami De Weerdt
act as our safety coordinators for the *E. coli* and anaerobic *P. vulgatus* work, respectively, and
are the first contacts for questions and incident reports. Both gave in-depth safety training, with
instrument handling, proper PPE, and emergency response information. Risks were identified by
working through each sub-experiment, including its organisms, chemicals, equipment, and waste
streams.

<ExpandableText title="Biological safety">

We worked in biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory when handling *P. vulgatus* in an anaerobic
chamber. *E. coli* focused work was carried out in BSL-1 conditions. Our synthetic inserts encode BSH variants or a GFP control and
contain no toxins or virulence factors. The only resistance marker is the kanamycin or ampicillin
resistance already present on the vector. Cultures are handled in biosafety cabinets or the
anaerobic workstation, and samples move between rooms in closed containers. Waste is separated at
the source. Liquid biological waste goes into yellow PE barrels, solid contaminated material into
yellow-lined cordi boxes, and needles into sharps containers. Chemical waste goes into a designated
basket in the stock room where it is handled in accordance with KU Leuven regulations.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Chemical safety">

Chemical hazards are limited but handled explicitly. The conjugated bile salts used as screening
substrates are treated as irritants. Kanamycin is used at 50 µg/mL or below, and 70% ethanol is the
disinfectant. The anaerobic workstation runs on a CO₂/N₂/H₂ gas mixture, with its oxygen sensor
checked before use.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Physical risks">

The main risks center around misuse or malfunction of instruments and machines. Vortexing,
centrifugation, breaking of glassware, electrical pulsing, and operational failures of the autoclave
and anaerobic workstation represent the main risks. Mitigation follows a clear order: choose
lower-hazard materials where possible, engineering controls such as routine checks of biosafety
cabinets and anaerobic workstations, personal protective equipment (PPE) and thorough training.

</ExpandableText>

## 2. Risk Identification and Mitigation during Project Design

A live biotherapeutic product is a living organism given to a patient, so the safety case reaches
beyond the laboratory. Before any clinical use, an engineered *P. vulgatus* strain would need to
show that it engrafts in the intended niche of the gut, that it does not spread beyond that niche,
and that it can be removed if needed. Our re-implantation approach depends on isolating the strain
from a patient's fecal sample, engineering it in the laboratory, and returning it to the same
patient. Each step in that loop, including the handling of patient material, would need its own risk
analysis and consent framework.

### Risk 1: Altered homeostasis due to *P. vulgatus*

<ExpandableText title="More about the risk">

Further dysregulation of the gut microbiomes of PMOS patients after reimplantation is a potential
risk. The risk of altered homeostasis accompanies the administration of any LBP.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Mitigation strategy">

    citations still need to be added as footnotes. */}

To decrease microbiome disruption, we are rewiring *P. vulgatus*, an abundant, beneficial gut
resident. By isolating *P. vulgatus* from the stool of each patient, the strain is adapted to the
metabolic niche already and therefore should have minimal disruptive effect. *P. vulgatus* has an
impressive range of available carbohydrate breakdown methods encoded in its genome, which helps
explain its adaptability (source). We hypothesize that this adaptability allows persistence in
hostile diseased environments such as PMOS patient's microbiome. Furthermore, *P. vulgatus* exhibits
clear beneficial effects within the gut ecosystem: it actively contributes to complex carbohydrate
degradation, produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like acetate, and promotes immune homeostasis
by suppressing gut inflammation and regulating host metabolic pathways (source).

</ExpandableText>

### Risk 2: Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT)

<ExpandableText title="More about the risk">

Horizontal gene transfer involves the exchange of genetic materials between related bacterial
species, a process of frequent occurrence in the intestinal environment. Because of this, it is
critical to consider that genetic material inside our LBP can spread into other organisms.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Mitigation strategy">

To avoid HGT of sensitive genetic material, we 1) cure the plasmids containing antibiotic resistance
genes and the CRISPR cassette before administration, 2) make minimal and stable genome edits,
3) screen the bacterial genomes for off-target effects and validation of intended edit. In addition,
the BSH gene of interest is from our pool of homologs that originate in species that are documented
components of the human microbiota. These origins are a testament to their capacity for benign
metabolic integration and argue that stringent standards of protection around the spread of that BSH
gene are not needed.

</ExpandableText>

### Risk 3: Uncontrolled colonization / excessive persistence

<ExpandableText title="More about the risk">

Genetically modified bacteria escaping or overgrowing its intended niche can be detrimental for the
patient and the environment. Biocontainment regulations must continue to be developed to fit the
need.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Mitigation strategy">

The first line of defense against unwanted growth of *P. vulgatus* lies in its strict anaerobic
nature as it is not motile and has limited oxygen tolerance[^saf-1]. As a second line of defense, it
is possible to include an auxotrophic marker that causes the bacterium to have a dependency on a
supplemented compound given to the patient. This has proven success in other emerging LBP companies,
see IHP. By pairing the intrinsic biological limitations of an obligate anaerobe with a robust
auxotrophy, we establish a multi-layered biocontainment framework that minimizes the risk of
environmental persistence and off-target transmission.

</ExpandableText>

With the US as our breakout market, we are bound to the requirements of the NIH regulation for
biocontainment enforced by the local institutional biosafety committee and the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration (FDA) that evaluates safety and chemistry controls through an Investigational New Drug
(IND) application. It must also undergo non-clinical safety testing which typically focuses on
evaluating translocation (the LBP crossing the mucosal barrier into systemic circulation) and general
pathogenicity in animal models. This is where our biocontainment plan integrates.

## 3. Ethical Engagement

To ensure appropriate patient data protection for patient interviews, we submitted a formal study
application to the Ethical Committee for Research (EC Research) of UZ Leuven. The study was approved
under compliance with GDPR guidelines (ref.-number: S72545), allowing us to recruit patients with
PMOS through online channels, but also through referral of Dr. Sharon Lie Fong in the PMOS clinic of
UZ Leuven.

In addition, our survey with the title: "Genetically engineered organisms to the rescue: how the
general public views genetic engineering for diseases with limited treatment options." was submitted
to the Social and Societal Ethics Committee (SMEC) of KU Leuven for formal review. It has been
approved for meeting the standard for academic research (ref.-number: G-2026-11849-R2(MIN))
