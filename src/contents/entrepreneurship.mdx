1 in 8 women are affected by PMOS, formerly known as PCOS, yet it remains underresearched,
underfunded, underdiagnosed, and undertreated[^pm-1][^pm-2][^pm-3].
<Link to="/human-practices#interviews">During our interviews with patients</Link>, particularly
those who had already explored all existing treatment options, the same frustration surfaced

condition. EMPOWER's mission is to investigate gut microbiome–bile acid
signaling as a proposed upstream mechanism linking inflammation, insulin resistance, and
hyperandrogenism in PMOS. These interconnected processes contribute not only to metabolic
dysfunction, but also to the reproductive complications associated with the disorder. As PMOS
accounts for approximately 80% of cases of anovulatory infertility, fertility-related complications
remain one of the most clinically significant manifestations of the disorder[^pm-4]. However,

often does not fully meet patients' needs, highlighting the need for therapeutic approaches that
can impact multiple aspects of the condition simultaneously.

efficacy, a key challenge will be building confidence in a GMO-based therapy and navigating the
regulatory requirements associated with its development. At the same time, EMPOWER must show that
its first-in-class, upstream mechanism provides sufficient value to justify the cost and complexity
of manufacturing and reimbursement.

</Milestone>

### 1.2 Market and target group

#### 1.2.1 Sizing EMPOWER's market

To estimate EMPOWER's market potential, we used a patient-based approach. Instead of starting from
market revenue, we first looked at how many women could potentially benefit from the therapy. This
approach is commonly used for innovative treatments that do not yet have a commercial track
record[^mkt-1][^mkt-2]. *Select a ring below to see how each estimate was built.*

<MarketRings>

<MarketRing label="TAM" name="Total Addressable Market" value="4.7 – 17.0 million women">

The Total Addressable Market (TAM) represents all women who could potentially benefit from EMPOWER.
For our project, this includes women aged 15 to 49 living in the EU-27[^mkt-3]. This
population consists of approximately 94.5 million women. Since PMOS affects an estimated 5–15% of
women of reproductive age, the potential patient population ranges from 4.7 to 17.0 million women
across the EU[^mkt-4]. At this stage, we intentionally include all women with PMOS in our TAM
estimate. Research shows that alterations in the gut microbiome and bile acid signaling play an
important role in PMOS, which forms the scientific basis of EMPOWER[^mkt-5][^mkt-6]. However,
further research is still needed to determine exactly which proportion of patients would be most
responsive to this specific therapeutic approach. Therefore, narrowing the market further would be
premature.

</MarketRing>

<MarketRing label="SAM" name="Serviceable Addressable Market" value="863,518 – 3,108,666 women">

The Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM) represents the subset of the total market that aligns with
EMPOWER's current target population. At this stage, EMPOWER is primarily focused on women
experiencing fertility-related complications associated with PMOS. Fertility was selected as the
initial clinical focus because PMOS is the leading cause of anovulatory infertility and is
estimated to account for up to 80% of anovulatory infertility cases worldwide[^mkt-7]. Ovulatory
dysfunction affects a substantial proportion of women with PMOS, with estimates ranging from
approximately 70% to 85% depending on the population studied[^mkt-8]. Using age-specific
infertility rates reported for women with PMOS, we estimated a SAM of 863,518 to 3,108,666 women in
the EU-27. Although diagnosis rates and patient-specific biological factors would likely reduce
this number further, robust EU-wide data are currently insufficient to accurately quantify their
impact.

</MarketRing>

<MarketRing label="SOM" name="Serviceable Obtainable Market" value="265,970 – 1,263,494 women">

The Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) represents the portion of the SAM that EMPOWER could
realistically reach during its initial commercialization phase. While TAM and SAM were calculated
using the EU-27 population to estimate the overall market opportunity, EMPOWER's initial
commercialization strategy focuses on the United States (US), where regulatory frameworks for LBPs
are relatively well established through Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance and clinical
development pathways, with expansion to Europe planned at a later stage[^mkt-9][^mkt-10]. The EU-27
SAM was converted into a cross-market ratio (0.91–3.29% of reproductive-age women) and applied to
the US female population as a scaling proxy. Our initial target within that group is later-line
patients: those who did not achieve a clinical pregnancy following first-line ovulation induction
with letrozole. Using outcomes from a randomized controlled trial reporting a 61.2% clinical
pregnancy rate and a 48.8% live birth rate following letrozole treatment[^mkt-11], we estimated
that 38.8–51.2% of women may remain insufficiently served after first-line therapy. This resulted
in an estimated SOM of 265,970 to 1,263,494 women.

</MarketRing>

</MarketRings>

#### 1.2.2 Target group

As described in the [SOM](#som), EMPOWER will initially target women with PMOS-related infertility
who have not responded successfully to first-line ovulation induction therapy. Through our
<Link to="/human-practices#interviews">patient interviews</Link>, we found that many women in this
group experience significant frustration after years of unsuccessful treatments and repeated
setbacks. Having exhausted the currently available options, many expressed a strong willingness to
explore novel therapeutic approaches that could address their condition more comprehensively. This
patient population therefore represents both a substantial unmet medical need and an ideal entry
point for EMPOWER's innovative treatment strategy. Following successful clinical validation,
EMPOWER could subsequently be positioned earlier in the treatment pathway to benefit a broader PMOS
population.

### 2.1 Unique value proposition

<h4 id="competition">2.1.1 Competition</h4>

EMPOWER's current direct competitors consist primarily of symptomatic treatments, including
hormonal contraception, anti-androgens, metformin, clomiphene citrate, and letrozole. These
therapies target the downstream effects of PMOS rather than addressing its underlying causes.
SPIOMET4HEALTH is an experimental approach aiming to target the disease more directly at its root,
specifically excess hepato-visceral fat[^comp-6]. Oxolife's OXO-001 also targets PMOS-related
infertility, but through a broader symptomatic effect on ovulation and embryo implantation, rather
than an underlying cause[^comp-7][^comp-8]. However, no existing or emerging competitor currently
targets the gut microbiome. EMPOWER therefore aims to establish itself in this largely unexplored
therapeutic space.

EMPOWER is investigating whether all three can be linked to, and addressed through, a single

### 2.5 Minimal viable product (MVP)

The minimum viable product (MVP) is the version of a new product that enables a team to obtain
the maximum amount of validated learning with the least
effort[ref: Ries, E. (2011). The lean startup: How today's entrepreneurs use continuous innovation to create radically successful businesses. Crown Business.].
In practice, it is the simplest version of a solution that can be presented to potential users to
test key assumptions and gather meaningful feedback. The goal of an MVP is not to deliver a
complete product, but to determine whether the identified problem is worth solving and whether
the proposed solution addresses user needs effectively. Surveys and interviews can provide
valuable insights, but observing how users interact with a tangible solution often reveals
information that cannot be obtained through conversation alone. An MVP therefore represents the
first practical step in translating user insights into a tested solution.

For medical products, it is not an unfinished drug. It is a testable version that checks one core
assumption, for example a storyboard of the patient journey or a concept slide with a realistic
business model, and it has to be shown to real users and decision-makers, not only
researchers[ref: Shen, H.-W. (n.d.). MVP (minimum viable product) in medical product development. BE Health Ventures. Retrieved October 5, 2026, from https://behealthventures.com/knowledge_base/minimum-viable-product/].
For therapeutics, it is also the smallest and fastest way to de-risk the product
candidate[ref: Clinical Supply Leader. (n.d.). What can the tech sector's "minimally viable" concept teach biopharma? Retrieved October 5, 2026, from https://www.clinicalsupplyleader.com/doc/what-can-the-tech-sector-s-minimally-viable-concept-teach-biopharma-0001].

#### Our hypothesis

Our interviews with women with PMOS and our stakeholder analysis confirmed that the problem is
real and insufficiently addressed by current care pathways. Women consistently described
frustration with delayed diagnosis, fragmented information, uncertainty about treatment outcomes,
and limited options when first-line fertility treatments were unsuccessful. These findings helped
us identify the key assumptions underlying our proposed solution.

An MVP is the next step because it allows us to test whether our proposed solution is wanted by
the people it is intended to help. Based on our human practices work, we formulated three
hypotheses:

1. Women with PMOS-related infertility whose fertility goals remain unmet after first-line
   treatment would be willing to consider an oral live biotherapeutic as a treatment option.
2. Clinicians would consider recommending such a therapy to appropriate patients, provided
   sufficient evidence of safety and efficacy is available.
3. Potential development partners would be interested in supporting further development once
   preliminary safety data have been generated (see Value Chain).

#### Our MVP

To test these hypotheses, we designed a patient journey map combined with a concept test. A
patient journey map visualizes the experiences, decisions, challenges, and emotions a patient
encounters throughout a healthcare pathway, helping identify unmet needs and opportunities for
intervention[ref: InsiderCX Editorial Team. (2024, January 2). How to create and use a patient journey map. InsiderCX. Retrieved October 5, 2026, from https://www.insidercx.com/blog/patient-journey-mapping].

Figure 2 presents the journey of a fictional woman with PMOS-related infertility, developed using
insights from both the literature and our interviews with women affected by the condition. The
map highlights key moments in the patient experience, identifies gaps in current care, and
illustrates where EMPOWER (see Problem and Mission) could realistically fit within the treatment
pathway, namely after first-line treatments have failed to achieve the desired outcome.

This MVP was designed primarily to validate our first two hypotheses. By presenting the patient
journey and treatment concept to patients, clinicians, and other stakeholders, we were able to
assess whether the proposed solution addresses a meaningful unmet need, whether the treatment
concept is acceptable, and which concerns, expectations, or evidence requirements must be
addressed before further development. Rather than testing the final product itself, this MVP
tests whether EMPOWER solves a problem that stakeholders consider important enough to warrant
further development.

## 3. Sustainability

*A strong idea deserves a sustainable future.*

### 3.1 Risks and mitigation

Developing EMPOWER as an LBP presents a unique opportunity to bring an innovative treatment approach
to patients with PMOS. To identify the challenges most critical to success, risks were assessed
across scientific, regulatory, financial, commercial, and operational domains.

<FanBranch label="Scientific" subtitle="challenges and opportunities" icon="flask">

Scientific challenges arise from the biological properties of our engineered strain itself,
specifically whether *Phocaeicola vulgatus* is safe, predictable, and well characterized enough to
eventually be tested in humans. These risks follow the categories defined by Rouanet et al. (2020),
the reference roadmap for LBP safety[^risk-1].

<td>The engineered *P. vulgatus* strain may translocate across the intestinal barrier and enter the bloodstream.</td>

<td>Undetected harmful genes could pose an unknown safety risk.</td>

</tr>
<tr>
<td>The metabolic activity of the engineered *P. vulgatus* may alter host metabolic pathways or interact with concomitant medications, resulting in unintended physiological effects.</td>
<td>Such interactions could reduce treatment safety, interfere with the efficacy of co-administered drugs, or lead to unforeseen adverse effects in patients.</td>
<td>Metabolic pathway analysis and metabolomic profiling will be performed to identify metabolites produced by the engineered *P. vulgatus* and predict potential host or drug interactions[^risk-8]. </tr>

<FanBranch label="Regulatory" subtitle="challenges and opportunities" icon="scales">

Regulatory considerations represent both a challenge and an opportunity for EMPOWER. As an LBP,
EMPOWER must comply with both established medicinal product regulations and emerging standards for
microbiome therapeutics. Within the EU, quality requirements are defined by the European
Pharmacopoeia, while non-clinical and clinical development is governed by Directive
2001/83/EC[^risk-9]. Early regulatory planning and proactive engagement with authorities will help
align development with regulatory expectations and support future market access.

<FanBranch label="Operational" subtitle="challenges and opportunities" icon="gear">

Operational considerations focus on the practical challenges of translating EMPOWER from a
laboratory concept into a manufacturable, scalable, and clinically deployable therapeutic product.
Beyond demonstrating safety and efficacy, successful development requires reliable strain
engineering, genetic stability throughout production, and preservation of bacterial viability
during manufacturing, storage, and administration. While these challenges are inherent to live
biotherapeutic products, addressing them early provides an opportunity to build a robust
development and manufacturing strategy.
