## Who is EMPOWER?

EMPOWER aims to address a major unmet need in women's health. One in eight women is affected by Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS), yet current treatments mainly focus on managing individual symptoms rather than targeting underlying mechanisms that may connect them. Our project explores an engineered gut bacterium, *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, as an oral live biotherapeutic designed to modulate bile acid signaling in the gut. By targeting this pathway, EMPOWER aims to address several aspects of PMOS through a single approach.

This business plan explores how EMPOWER could move from a research concept towards a potential therapy. Discover the unmet medical need on our <Link to="/description">Description</Link> page, our engineering strategy and experimental findings on <Link to="/engineering">Engineering</Link> and <Link to="/results">Results</Link>, our approach to risk assessment and biosafety on <Link to="/safety-and-security">Safety</Link>, the insights gathered from patients and experts on <Link to="/human-practices">Human Practices</Link>, and our strategy for future development, regulatory approval and market access on [Entrepreneurship](#phase-2-development).

<Milestone label="Mission" icon="flag" subtitle="A new therapeutic approach for Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome">

1 in 8 women are affected by PMOS, formerly known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), yet it remains underresearched,
underfunded, underdiagnosed, and undertreated[^pm-1][^pm-2][^pm-3].
<Link to="/human-practices#patients-1">During our interviews with patients</Link>, particularly
those who had already explored all existing treatment options, the same frustration surfaced
repeatedly: current therapies target individual symptoms, but never the full spectrum of their condition.

In response, EMPOWER aims to develop an oral treatment for women with PMOS, based on an engineered gut bacterium (a live biotherapeutic). Instead of treating symptoms one by one, it targets bile acid signaling in the gut, a pathway we propose is linked to inflammation, insulin resistance, and high male hormone levels. Our aim is a treatment that addresses several aspects of PMOS together, instead of one symptom at a time.

</Milestone>

<Milestone label="Business Model Challenge" icon="gate" subtitle="Turning a scientifically promising live biotherapeutic product into a clinically adopted and commercially sustainable therapy">

EMPOWER is based on a genetically modified live biotherapeutic product (LBP), a therapeutic approach that remains largely
unfamiliar to patients, physicians, regulators, and payers. Beyond demonstrating safety and
efficacy, a key challenge will be building confidence in a genetically modified organism (GMO)-based therapy and navigating the
regulatory requirements associated with its development. At the same time, EMPOWER must show that
its first-in-class, upstream mechanism provides sufficient value to justify the cost and complexity
of manufacturing and reimbursement.

</Milestone>

<Milestone label="Product" icon="pill" subtitle="A first-in-class LBP">

A first-in-class engineered *Phocaeicola vulgatus* LBP designed to modulate bile acid signaling and
address inflammation, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism in PMOS. Currently in early
development.

</Milestone>

</MilestoneArc>

<ExpandableText title="Why a Live Biotherapeutic Product and not a probiotic?">

Choosing the LBP pathway instead of the probiotic food supplement route is both a regulatory and strategic decision for a live microorganism intended to address PMOS.
Because PMOS is a recognized medical condition, regulatory frameworks such as those of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and
the European Union (EU) do not allow food supplements to make specific therapeutic claims regarding the treatment,
prevention, or cure of a disease[^lbp-1][^lbp-2].

A conventional food supplement is legally intended to support general health in healthy individuals
rather than to treat a medical condition[^lbp-2]. Consequently, pursuing the supplement pathway
would restrict EMPOWER to broad health-related claims instead of evidence-based therapeutic claims.
This limitation may reduce credibility among healthcare professionals and make differentiation more
challenging in a highly competitive supplement market.

From a societal and patient-care perspective, the LBP pathway offers important advantages. LBPs are
developed under pharmaceutical quality standards and are subject to rigorous safety and efficacy evaluation, which helps ensure product consistency, safety and quality[^mkt-9][^lbp-3].
Clinical validation also increases the likelihood that a successful therapy can ultimately be
integrated into clinical practice and evidence-based treatment strategies.

From an entrepreneurial perspective, the LBP route transforms EMPOWER from a consumer product into a
potential therapeutic asset. Clinical data, regulatory approval, and intellectual property (IP) protection can create
significant barriers to entry, facilitate reimbursement discussions, and support partnerships with
pharmaceutical companies[^lbp-1][^lbp-4]. While the development process is longer and more
resource-intensive than that of a traditional supplement, it also provides greater opportunities for
licensing, commercialization, and long-term value creation. This strategic choice was further reinforced through discussions with <Link to="/human-practices#pedro-correa-de-sampaio">Pedro Correa de Sampaio</Link>, Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of Neobe Therapeutics, who highlighted the importance of pursuing a therapeutic development pathway when aiming to create a clinically validated and commercially attractive live biotherapeutic product. For EMPOWER, this pathway aligns with
our goal of maximizing both patient impact and the project's future translational potential.

</ExpandableText>

### 1.2 Market and target group

#### 1.2.1 Sizing EMPOWER's market

To estimate EMPOWER's market potential, we used a patient-based approach. Instead of starting from
market revenue, we first looked at how many women could potentially benefit from the therapy. This
approach is commonly used for innovative treatments that do not yet have a commercial track
record[^mkt-1][^mkt-2]. *Select a ring below to see how each estimate was built.*

**Note:** The United States (US) is considered the initial target market, with potential expansion into the EU at a later stage. As key regulatory and development considerations differ between both regions, requirements relevant to each market are taken into account throughout the development process. Consequently, both markets are discussed in this business plan.

To see how these numbers were obtained, please refer to the PDF file in [Appendix 1](#appendix-1-tam-sam-and-som-estimates).

<MarketRings>

<MarketRing label="TAM" name="Total Addressable Market" value="4.7 – 17.0 million women">

The Total Addressable Market (TAM) represents all women who could potentially benefit from EMPOWER.
For our project, this includes women with PMOS aged 15 to 49 living in the EU-27. The EU-27 has approximately 94.5 million women in this age group[^mkt-3]. Since PMOS affects an estimated 5–18% of women of reproductive age, the potential patient population ranges from 4.7 to 17.0 million women
across the EU[^mkt-4]. At this stage, we intentionally include all women with PMOS in our TAM
estimate. Research shows that alterations in the gut microbiome and bile acid signaling play an
important role in PMOS, which forms the scientific basis of EMPOWER[^mkt-5][^mkt-6]. However,
further research is still needed to determine exactly which proportion of patients would be most
responsive to this specific therapeutic approach. Therefore, narrowing the market further would be
premature.

</MarketRing>

<MarketRing label="SAM" name="Serviceable Addressable Market" value="863,518 – 3,108,666 women">

The Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM) represents the subset of the total market that aligns with
EMPOWER's current target population. At this stage, EMPOWER is primarily focused on women
experiencing fertility-related complications associated with PMOS. Fertility was selected as the
initial clinical focus because PMOS is the leading cause of anovulatory infertility and is
estimated to account for up to 80% of anovulatory infertility cases worldwide[^mkt-7]. Using age-specific
infertility rates reported for women with PMOS, we estimated a SAM of 863,518 to 3,108,666 women in
the EU-27. Although diagnosis rates and patient-specific biological factors would likely reduce
this number further, robust EU-wide data are currently insufficient to accurately quantify their
impact.

</MarketRing>

<MarketRing label="SOM" name="Serviceable Obtainable Market" value="265,970 – 957,492 women">

The Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) represents the portion of the SAM that EMPOWER could
realistically reach during its initial commercialization phase. While TAM and SAM were calculated
using the EU-27 population to estimate the overall market opportunity, EMPOWER's initial commercialization strategy focuses on the US, where the FDA provides specific guidance for the clinical development of LBPs. Expansion to Europe is planned at a later stage[^mkt-9][^mkt-10]. The EU-27
SAM was converted into a cross-market ratio (0.91–3.29% of reproductive-age women)\* and applied to
the US female population as a scaling proxy. Our initial target population consists of women with PMOS whose needs are not adequately met by existing therapies. Since no comprehensive estimate is available for this group, we used first-line letrozole failure as a proxy. Clinical data show that 38.8–51.2% of patients do not achieve a successful outcome with letrozole[^mkt-11], corresponding to an estimated SOM of 265,970 to 957,492 women. Although some patients respond to later-line treatments, significant unmet needs remain across the treatment pathway.

\**To estimate the US market, we divided the lower and upper EU SAM estimates by the total number of women aged 15-49 in the EU and applied the resulting proportions to the US female population in the same age group. We then multiplied these estimates by the percentage of women who did not achieve a clinical pregnancy following letrozole treatment to estimate the potential US target population for EMPOWER.*

</MarketRing>

</MarketRings>

#### 1.2.2 Target group

As described in the [SOM](#som), EMPOWER will initially target women with PMOS-related infertility
who do not achieve successful outcomes with first-line therapy and continue to face limited treatment options. Through our
<Link to="/human-practices#patients-1">patient interviews</Link>, we found that many women in this
group experience significant frustration after years of unsuccessful treatments and repeated
setbacks. Having exhausted the currently available options, many expressed a strong willingness to
explore novel therapeutic approaches that could address their condition more comprehensively. This
patient population therefore represents both a substantial unmet medical need and an ideal entry
point for EMPOWER's innovative treatment strategy. Following successful clinical validation,
EMPOWER could subsequently be positioned earlier in the treatment pathway to benefit a broader PMOS
population.

### 2.1 Unique value proposition

<h4 id="competition">2.1.1 Competition</h4>

Discussions with industry experts, including <Link to="/human-practices#merck">Merck</Link>, reinforced the importance of building a clear, patient-centered value proposition and understanding how EMPOWER differentiates itself from existing treatments.

While EMPOWER intends to pursue an out-licensing strategy following Phase I clinical development rather than commercializing the product independently, understanding the therapeutic landscape remains essential. Healthcare providers, patients, and future licensing partners will ultimately compare EMPOWER against existing and emerging PMOS treatment options. Therefore, the following analysis focuses on competitors from a therapeutic perspective rather than a business-to-business perspective.

EMPOWER's current therapeutic competitors consist primarily of symptomatic treatments, including
hormonal contraception, anti-androgens, metformin, clomiphene citrate, and letrozole. These
therapies target the downstream effects of PMOS rather than addressing its underlying causes.
SPIOMET4HEALTH is an experimental approach aiming to target the disease more directly at its root,
specifically excess hepato-visceral fat[^comp-6]. Oxolife's OXO-001 also targets PMOS-related
infertility, but through a broader symptomatic effect on ovulation and embryo implantation, rather
than an underlying cause[^comp-7][^comp-8]. However, no existing or emerging competitor currently
targets the gut microbiome. EMPOWER therefore aims to establish itself in this largely unexplored
therapeutic space.

##### Pricing strategy and justification of EMPOWER

- **Estimated out-of-pocket cost (patient):** €0–150 (depending on the reimbursement system and insurance coverage).
- **Estimated total treatment cost (healthcare payer):** €150,000–300,000 per treatment course.

**Justification.** Although current PMOS treatments are relatively inexpensive, they primarily target individual symptoms and often require long-term use. In contrast, EMPOWER is designed as a personalized LBP that targets an upstream mechanism associated with PMOS pathology. Accordingly, its value proposition is based on the potential for durable therapeutic effects rather than symptom management alone.

EMPOWER's pricing strategy follows a value-based pricing framework, in which reimbursement is linked to the clinical benefit delivered to patients and healthcare systems. If successful, a one-time or infrequently administered treatment could reduce the need for repeated interventions and ongoing symptom-directed therapies. Furthermore, reimbursement would be expected to occur through national healthcare systems and insurance providers, helping to maintain patient accessibility despite the higher manufacturing costs associated with an autologous approach.

### 2.4 Minimal viable product

The minimum viable product (MVP) is the version of a new product that enables a team to obtain
the maximum amount of validated learning with the least
effort[ref: Ries, E. (2011). The lean startup: How today's entrepreneurs use continuous innovation to create radically successful businesses. Crown Business.].
In practice, it is the simplest version of a solution that can be presented to potential users to
test key assumptions and gather meaningful feedback. The goal of an MVP is not to deliver a
complete product, but to determine whether the identified problem is worth solving and whether
the proposed solution addresses user needs effectively. Surveys and interviews can provide
valuable insights, but observing how users interact with a tangible solution often reveals
information that cannot be obtained through conversation alone. An MVP therefore represents the
first practical step in translating user insights into a tested solution.

For medical products, it is not an unfinished drug. It is a testable version that checks one core
assumption, for example a storyboard of the patient journey or a concept slide with a realistic
business model, and it has to be shown to real users and decision-makers, not only
researchers[ref: Shen, H.-W. (n.d.). MVP (minimum viable product) in medical product development. BE Health Ventures. Retrieved October 5, 2026, from https://behealthventures.com/knowledge_base/minimum-viable-product/].
For therapeutics, it is also the smallest and fastest way to de-risk the product
candidate[ref: Clinical Supply Leader. (n.d.). What can the tech sector's "minimally viable" concept teach biopharma? Retrieved October 5, 2026, from https://www.clinicalsupplyleader.com/doc/what-can-the-tech-sector-s-minimally-viable-concept-teach-biopharma-0001].

#### 2.4.1 Our MVP

To test these hypotheses, we designed a patient journey map combined with a concept test. A
patient journey map visualizes the experiences, decisions, challenges, and emotions a patient
encounters throughout a healthcare pathway, helping identify unmet needs and opportunities for
intervention[ref: InsiderCX Editorial Team. (2024, January 2). How to create and use a patient journey map. InsiderCX. Retrieved October 5, 2026, from https://www.insidercx.com/blog/patient-journey-mapping].

[Figure 2](#figure-2) presents the journey of a fictional woman with PMOS-related infertility, developed using

map highlights key moments in the patient experience, identifies gaps in current care, and
illustrates where EMPOWER could realistically fit within the treatment
pathway, namely after first-line treatments have failed to achieve the desired outcome.

This MVP was designed primarily to validate our first two hypotheses. By presenting the patient
journey and treatment concept to patients, clinicians, and other stakeholders, we were able to
assess whether the proposed solution addresses a meaningful unmet need, whether the treatment
concept is acceptable, and which concerns, expectations, or evidence requirements must be
addressed before further development. Rather than testing the final product itself, this MVP
tests whether EMPOWER solves a problem that stakeholders consider important enough to warrant
further development.

### 2.5 Value chain analysis

To understand how EMPOWER would create and deliver value, we mapped its development onto the
biopharmaceutical value chain proposed by Kim et al. (2022)[^vc-1]. This framework follows a medicine
from discovery to post-market monitoring and highlights collaboration between different stakeholders
throughout the product life cycle (PLC). In a pharmaceutical context, the PLC describes the
commercial trajectory of a product, from its introduction to the market and growth in adoption to
eventual maturity and decline, helping companies plan development and investment decisions with
future market performance in mind[^vc-1]. Because EMPOWER is an LBP, we additionally incorporated key
considerations specific to LBPs described by Rouanet et al. (2020)[^risk-1].

The value chain begins with research and development, where our team designs and validates the
engineered bacterial strain and generates the preclinical data required for regulatory approval.
Following successful preclinical studies, clinical development would proceed through Phase I, with
the primary goal of demonstrating safety in humans. Throughout these stages, value is created
through scientific innovation, IP generation, and risk reduction.

After Phase I, we plan to out-license EMPOWER to an established pharmaceutical or live
biotherapeutic company. Such partners would be responsible for later-stage clinical trials,
large-scale manufacturing, regulatory submissions, commercialization, and post-market surveillance.
This strategy reflects common practice in biotechnology, where early-stage innovators transfer
promising therapeutics to organizations with the expertise and resources needed for late-stage
development and market access[^vc-3]. Our first market is the US, followed by the EU, so both regulatory pathways are reflected in the value chain below.

Several features distinguish the value chain of LBPs from that of conventional pharmaceuticals. First, safety assessments are strain-specific and require detailed characterization of antibiotic resistance, virulence potential, and translocation risk[^risk-1]. Second, the regulatory pathway for LBPs remains relatively novel and is not yet harmonised between regions, making early interaction with regulators such as the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) particularly important[^risk-1]. Third, manufacturing itself represents a critical source of risk, requiring strict control of product identity, purity, and potency throughout production[^risk-1].

### 2.7 IP and commercialisation strategy

#### What we plan to protect

To better understand what can and cannot be protected by a patent, we had a meeting with
<Link to="/human-practices#sara-holland">Sara Holland</Link>, a Chartered and European Patent
Attorney. She explained that patentable inventions are often built from existing technologies, with
innovation arising from a novel and non-obvious combination of known elements.

For this project, this means protecting the bile salt hydrolase (BSH) sequences with interesting features, as they sit at
the core of our therapeutic approach. This is the main reason why, at this stage of the project, we
have decided not to disclose specific genetic sequences of BSH variants we're working with. We
intentionally withhold these details because EMPOWER is not intended to end with iGEM. We plan to
further develop and validate the technology after the competition, with the long-term goal of
translating it into a real therapeutic platform. That's why we first reached out to the Tech
Transfer office here at KU Leuven. We have already discussed potential partnerships we can explore
together to reduce our start-up costs and increase our chance of success.

At this stage, for the sake of the competition and our contribution to the scientific community,
we've developed a synthetic biology toolkit for *P. vulgatus* which has value beyond our specific
application, and we aim to share these broader tools while withholding the details that are central
to the core invention.

However, this synthetic biology toolkit will serve as basis for broader application within our
company. Without disclosing preliminary data like we are now, we plan to build and further extend
this toolkit to other non-model bacteria associated with various health conditions.

What we've already identified as a critical point that should be protected within our toolkit, are
different promoters that would be mutated, optimized, and tested. Within our company, one of the
goals would be to create a library of promoters with variable strength that could be used as a base
for any other LBP we were to generate. To maximize our impact in an area we deeply care about, we
will focus on the field of women's health which has plenty of gaps.

#### Commercialisation

EMPOWER is being developed with an out-licensing strategy in mind (see [value chain analysis](#2-5-value-chain-analysis)). Our goal is to advance the technology through preclinical development and Phase I clinical testing before partnering with a pharmaceutical company or live biotherapeutics developer. Such a partner would provide the expertise, infrastructure, and financial resources required for large-scale manufacturing, late-stage clinical trials, regulatory approval, and commercialization.

We believe EMPOWER offers an attractive value proposition due to the large patient population affected by PMOS, the lack of therapies targeting the underlying disease biology, and the potential to provide a non-hormonal treatment option (see [Market and target group](#1-2-market-and-target-group)). By advancing EMPOWER through Phase I, we aim to generate human safety data and initial evidence of clinical activity, substantially de-risking the technology and strengthening its value as a licensable asset. Combined with a strong IP position, these data would form the basis of future licensing discussions.

In line with our [financial strategy](#3-4-financial-strategy), potential revenues would be generated through upfront payments, development and commercial milestones, and royalties on future sales. Rather than commercializing the product ourselves, our long-term objective is to accelerate EMPOWER's translation to patients through a licensing partnership. As outlined in our [roadmap](#3-2-roadmap), securing a licensing agreement around 2033 represents an aspirational yet realistic long-term goal.

Our ideal partner would combine expertise in women's health or microbiome therapeutics with the resources required for late-stage development. Examples include pharmaceutical companies with established women's health portfolios, such as Bayer, Organon, and Merck, as well as companies active in the microbiome field, such as Freya Biosciences, MaaT Pharma, and Ferring/Rebiotix. For EMPOWER, such a partnership would provide access to capital, regulatory expertise, manufacturing capabilities that meet good manufacturing practice (GMP) standards, and commercialization infrastructure. In return, the partner would gain access to a differentiated therapeutic asset addressing a large and underserved PMOS population. We will therefore seek partners with strong strategic alignment and a genuine commitment to advancing EMPOWER while minimizing the risks associated with misaligned incentives (see [risks and mitigation](#3-3-risks-and-mitigation)).

#### 3.4.1 Major cost drivers

The development of a novel LBP requires substantial investment. For EMPOWER, the principal cost drivers span preclinical development, GMP manufacturing, clinical testing, regulatory activities, and IP protection.

While bringing a new drug to market requires a median investment of 1.1 billion US dollars (USD)[^fin-1], EMPOWER utilizes an out-licensing strategy following Phase I clinical trials. Consequently, the heavy costs of Phase II/III trials and commercialization will be assumed by a future licensing partner. The largest immediate expenditures for EMPOWER are driven by the unique genetic and physiological properties of engineered *P. vulgatus*[^fin-7]:

- **GMO Safety & Biocontainment:** Preclinical costs are driven by mandatory assays for genetic stability, horizontal gene transfer (HGT), and the validation of synthetic biological safety mechanisms (kill-switches).
- **Anaerobic GMP Manufacturing:** *P. vulgatus* is a strict anaerobe, requiring specialized oxygen-free fermentation, lyophilization, and formulation. Manufacturing must occur in Biosafety Level 1/2 certified GMO facilities, which limits vendor availability and increases premiums.
- **Clinical Shedding & Monitoring:** Phase I trials require extensive quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) or metagenomic sequencing of participant stool samples to strictly monitor the shedding kinetics and environmental clearance of the GMO strain.
- **Therapeutic Modality:** The choice between an allogeneic ("off-the-shelf") and an autologous ("personalized") approach is a key determinant of clinical development and manufacturing costs, as personalized therapies do not benefit from the economies of scale achievable with standardized production.

##### Strategic modality choice for EMPOWER: the personalized model

Despite the higher development and manufacturing costs, EMPOWER pursues a <Link to="/description#how-can-we-make-sure-our-engineered-strain-colonizes-the-gut">personalized (autologous) approach</Link> in which a patient's native *P. vulgatus* strain is isolated, engineered *ex vivo*, and re-administered. This strategy aims to maximize host-microbe compatibility and reduce potential safety concerns associated with introducing a non-native engineered strain. While risks such as horizontal gene transfer remain important considerations for any engineered LBP, a personalized approach may help reduce biological variability between patients. As discussed on our <Link to="/safety-and-security">Safety page</Link>, these considerations played an important role in the selection of our therapeutic design. We therefore believe that the additional manufacturing complexity is justified by the potential safety and translational advantages of an autologous approach.

#### 3.4.2 Funding strategy

To advance EMPOWER through Phase I clinical development, we estimate a total funding requirement of **USD 12–23M+**, based on the development costs outlined in our [financial strategy](#3-4-financial-strategy). To meet these funding needs, we propose a staged financing strategy that combines non-dilutive grants, university-linked funding opportunities, <GlossaryTerm definition="Private investors providing early capital and expertise.">angel investors</GlossaryTerm>, and venture capital. This approach is consistent with the advice of <Link to="/human-practices#johan-van-hylckama-vlieg">Dr. Johan E.T. van Hylckama Vlieg (Freya Biosciences)</Link>, who emphasized the importance of first leveraging non-dilutive grant funding to generate robust preclinical data before engaging venture capital investors. Early development phases would therefore primarily rely on public and academic funding sources, while later stages would increasingly involve private investment as key scientific and regulatory risks are reduced. In this way, early grants support scientific de-risking and value creation prior to equity fundraising. Given the specialized manufacturing requirements of an autologous LBP, international investors, particularly those active in advanced therapeutics, may play an increasingly important role during clinical development.

#### 3.4.3 Licensing revenue model

The development of a novel therapeutic requires expertise and resources that often exceed the
capabilities of early-stage biotechnology ventures. As a result, partnerships between small
biotechnology companies and established pharmaceutical companies are common. Early-stage ventures
focus on innovation, proof-of-concept (POC) studies, and early clinical de-risking, while larger partners provide the
capital, infrastructure, regulatory expertise, and commercial capabilities needed to bring a therapy
to market[^fin-3][^fin-4].

EMPOWER will follow this model. We aim to advance the therapy through Phase I clinical development
before out-licensing it to an established pharmaceutical or live biotherapeutic company. At this
stage, the partner would assume responsibility for Phase II/III clinical trials, regulatory
approval, manufacturing scale-up, and commercialization.

In return, EMPOWER would generate revenue through a licensing agreement. Such agreements typically
consist of three components[^fin-3][^fin-4][^fin-5]:

- **Upfront payment**: a one-time payment made when the licensing agreement is signed, compensating
  the developer for the value created during discovery and early development.
- **Milestone payments**: payments linked to predefined development, regulatory, or commercial
  achievements, such as the initiation of Phase II or III trials, obtaining market approval, or
  reaching specific sales targets. These payments often constitute a significant portion of the
  total deal value and provide an important source of capital to support continued product
  development without requiring further equity financing.
- **Royalties**: a percentage of future product sales paid to the licensor after market launch.
  Shah et al. demonstrated that biotechnology licenses originating from academic institutions had a
  median effective royalty rate of approximately 3%, although negotiated rates vary depending on the
  technology and its stage of development[^fin-6]. As EMPOWER would originate from academic
  research, royalty revenues may also need to be shared with the originating university according
  to IP and technology transfer agreements.

An analysis of 2,942 biopharmaceutical licensing agreements found that upfront payments, milestone
payments, and royalties are included in the majority of deals, demonstrating that these mechanisms
form the foundation of biotechnology licensing partnerships[^fin-3]. Most of the value associated
with such agreements is generated through development and regulatory milestones, reflecting the
progressive reduction of risk as a product advances through clinical development[^fin-3].

The value of a licensing agreement depends on multiple factors, including the strength of the IP
portfolio, the quality of the preclinical and Phase I data, the regulatory pathway, and the
commercial potential of the therapy. Because these factors remain uncertain at this stage, we do not
attempt to predict specific deal values. Instead, we focus on the revenue structure that would be
expected for a biotechnology venture following an out-licensing strategy.

As an LBP, EMPOWER represents an emerging therapeutic modality with limited precedent for licensing
and commercialization. Because relatively few LBPs have reached advanced clinical stages or market
approval, there is insufficient historical data to reliably predict future deal structures or
values. Therefore, direct extrapolation from traditional biopharmaceutical licensing agreements
should be interpreted with caution.

For EMPOWER, a successful Phase I trial would represent a major value-inflection point, as it would
provide the first clinical safety data and significantly strengthen the basis for a future licensing
agreement. The uncertainties associated with clinical success, regulatory approval, and partner
negotiations are discussed further in the [Risks and Mitigation](#3-3-risks-and-mitigation) section.

#### 3.4.4 Economic viability

From an economic perspective, EMPOWER's value proposition must be evaluated relative to its development costs. Although development through Phase I is estimated to require USD 12–23M+, successful completion of this stage would generate three major value drivers: a patented therapeutic platform, preclinical proof-of-concept data, and human safety data. Together, these assets substantially reduce development risk and increase the likelihood of securing a licensing agreement. As EMPOWER targets a large and underserved patient population using a differentiated microbiome-based approach, we believe the value created during development can justify the required investment, supporting the overall economic feasibility of the project.

## Phase 4: Long term impact

The impact of a healthcare innovation extends beyond clinical outcomes alone. It includes its
effects on patients, society, healthcare systems, and the environment. To better understand
EMPOWER's future role, we explored both the opportunities it offers and the considerations needed
for its responsible implementation.

### 4.1 Positive impacts

#### Addressing an unmet medical need

PMOS affects millions of women in the EU alone (see
[Market and target group](#1-2-market-and-target-group)) and is responsible for around 80% of cases
of anovulatory infertility[^pm-4]. Many women nevertheless remain undiagnosed and untreated for years
after symptom onset[^pm-3]. Existing therapies primarily target individual symptoms, underscoring the
need for treatments that address the condition more holistically[^lti-3]. EMPOWER targets a proposed
mechanism that may connect inflammation, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism, offering a more
integrated approach to PMOS management. Through its potential effects on insulin sensitivity and
inflammation, EMPOWER may support reproductive health while also contributing to long-term metabolic
well-being. If it proves safe and effective, EMPOWER could become a first-of-its-kind therapy for
PMOS, and open possibilities for developing more targeted microbiome-based therapeutics.

#### Women's health

Women's health research receives an estimated 5% of global research and development
funding[^lti-4]. Through patient engagement, science communication, and educational outreach, EMPOWER
seeks to bring greater attention to PMOS and the broader importance of women's health. By increasing
awareness of PMOS symptoms and their impact, we hope to empower women to seek care earlier and
advocate for their own health.

#### Science and future therapies

*P. vulgatus* is one of the most abundant bacteria of the human gut and is linked to both health and
disease. It colonizes the gut soon after birth[^lti-5], and it is elevated in the gut microbiota of
women with PMOS[^mkt-5]. Yet its role in health and disease is still not well understood[^lti-5]. Our work
generates new data on this chassis, and the genetic toolkit we built for it can be reused by other
researchers (see <Link to="/contribution#parts">contribution</Link>). Better tools for its genetic
engineering help advance this understanding, well beyond our own project.

### 4.2 Negative impacts

#### Long term safety

Because EMPOWER is a living therapy, long-term safety is a key consideration. Potential risks and
the measures we propose to address them are discussed in [Risks and mitigation](#3-3-risks-and-mitigation). In addition, we envision a stepwise
clinical development pathway with careful monitoring, independent safety oversight, and clear
stopping criteria throughout clinical testing (see [Value chain](#2-5-value-chain-analysis)).

#### Environment

EMPOWER is a genetically modified living organism. Modifying it and releasing it, through patients,
into the body and the environment, could affect human health and the environment in ways we cannot
yet fully predict. For example, live bacteria can leave the body in stool, and engineered genes
could in principle spread to other bacteria. These risks would need to be assessed and determined
before any application can be considered. We plan to build this assessment into our regulatory
strategy from the earliest stage.

#### Access and affordability

Reimbursement routes for living therapies are still less established, and without reimbursement
EMPOWER could reach only women who can pay for it. We plan to engage with reimbursement bodies early.
