## Abstract

Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) is the most common metabolic and endocrine disorder affecting approximately 1 in 8 women of reproductive age. However, high prevalence and seriousness of the condition does not reflect the funding, research, and understanding it deserves. To this day, no approved disease-modifying treatment exists.

Recent studies show that the intestinal microbiome can play a crucial role in the development and progression of PMOS. We hypothesize that reduced microbial diversity together with bile acid pool modulation could stand upstream of various symptoms such as insulin resistance, inflammation, hyperandrogenism, and fertility complications.

One bacterium that's overabundant in PMOS is involved in this change through its enzymatic system, thereby disrupting bile acid metabolism and key anti-inflammatory signaling pathways in the gut, which can contribute to hormonal dysregulation and systemic inflammation.

This is why we created EMPOWER. Our approach selectively targets BSH activity of *P. vulgatus* to restore healthy bile acid signaling and hormonal balance.

## PMOS

For most of its history, Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS), previously known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), has been misunderstood and underresearched. This is striking information considering that it's one of the most common endocrine disorders affecting roughly 5 to 15% of women worldwide[ref: https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/23/8/1085]. It is also the biggest cause of anovulatory infertility in women of reproductive age, accounting for up to 80% of women with anovulation (Liu et al., 2024).

**Not-so-fun fact:** the global burden of infertility due to this condition rose from 6 million cases in 1990 to 12.13 million in 2019 (Liu et al., 2024)[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12178856/]. These numbers don't necessarily mean that PMOS incidence increased; they reflect the fact that PMOS goes undiagnosed for a very long period of time due to its complex and multifaced nature.

Why has a condition this common, and this consequential received so little scientific attention? Why are there still so many challenges even after 150 years of studying PMOS? We've asked plenty of questions and received no answer, which is why we knew we needed to do something...

<ExpandableText title="Want to know more about the history of PMOS?">

PMOS has been first identified in 1844, when Chereau described enlarged ovaries studded with small peripheral cysts. After this, multiple names followed (sclerocystic ovaries, follicular degeneration, chronic cystic oophoritis). Additionally, it was originally described as inflammation of the female genital organs (Chereau, 1844; Polk, 1895; reviewed in Adashi et al., 2022).

In 1935, Stein and Leventhal linked the ovarian pathology to menstrual dysfunction, hirsutism and infertility. They argued that the cause was hormonal, which led to a new name, Stein-Leventhal Syndrome (Stein & Leventhal, 1935). Afterwards, Polycystic Ovary Disease became Polycystic Ovary Syndrome once it was clear the disorder was not a single specific disease[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29995717/], but the term "ovary" stayed at the center of the name.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="The most recent name change">

After years of advocacy on this matter, in May 2026, following a global consensus process involving more than 22,000 patients and health professionals, endorsed by 56 professional and patient organizations, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome was formally renamed Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome[^lancet2026]. The old name was completely misleading as it pointed at ovarian cysts as the main symptom, but this was just the tip of the iceberg. Polycystic ovarian morphology is not universal nor the source of the condition's main burden. Previous name obscures the endocrine and metabolic dysfunction that drives long-term complications. The new name is a great step forward, however, it's being phased in over three years, with full implementation in the 2028 International Guideline update[^lancet2026]. This means that most of the literature we cite still uses PCOS. We are very eager to help implement this change and will be using Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) throughout this wiki!

</ExpandableText>

## Phenotype

According to the Rotterdam criteria, PMOS is traditionally characterized by a combination of androgen excess, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology[ref: https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/2/604/2972079]. However, in practice, the syndrome manifests very differently from patient to patient.

<ExpandableText title="What are the Rotterdam criteria for diagnosing PMOS?">

The Rotterdam criteria are the diagnostic standard for PMOS. They were agreed at a 2003 ESHRE/ASRM consensus workshop in Rotterdam and require that two of three features are present, considering that other causes with same symptoms are excluded. These features are irregular or absent ovulation; hyperandrogenism, either clinical (hirsutism, acne) or biochemical; and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound[ref: https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article-abstract/19/1/41/690226?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false]. Based on this PMOS is also divided into different subgroups (A-D), with A being the most difficult one.

The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline retained this two-of-three structure and refined it, notably by allowing elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) as an alternative to ultrasound in adults, and by not applying the ovarian morphology criterion to adolescents at all[ref: https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/108/10/2447/7242360]. Because any two of the three features suffice, eight different combinations can lead to the same diagnosis, which is why two patients with PMOS may share almost no symptoms, and a large part of why the condition is so difficult to study.

</ExpandableText>

<SymptomGroups />

Because PMOS presents differently between individuals and across regions, diagnosis is often delayed. This prolonged uncertainty adds a psychological burden for women who are already at higher risk of depression and anxiety due to the hormonal imbalances associated with PMOS[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9319705/].

Furthermore, PMOS causes considerable financial burden, to individuals and the overall medical system. It is estimated that in the US alone annual cost of diagnosis and treatment of symptoms and associated disorders is $15 billion[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10471160/].

Since there's no known etiology for PMOS, current therapeutic approaches come from off-label use of other medications to treat each symptom, individually. This shows to be effective in some cases, but not all. No current treatment is effective when it comes to tackling all of the symptoms at once. Symptoms seem to be addressed based on the patient's need at the moment of examination, whether that's a contraceptive pill to prevent conception, fertility treatments for IVF, insulin-sensitizing drugs or treating acne and hirsutism … This treatment and knowledge gap is a critical issue that continues to sustain a vicious cycle of PMOS, while prevalence is high, funding and treatment gaps remain persistent.

<ExpandableText title="Learn more about the different hypotheses of PMOS etiology">

As the actual cause of this condition remains unknown, over the years there have been many hypotheses created around the root cause of PMOS. Nisa et al. group them into five[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10784448/]:

- **Environmental hypothesis** — proposes how under ancestral conditions, such as heavy physical labor, periods of famine and epidemic disease, favoring efficient energy storage would have been advantageous[^thrifty]. Considering a drastic life change of continuous food availability and sedentary living, the same genotype is hypothesized to contribute to the rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes, and possibly to PMOS[^thrifty].
- **Genetic hypothesis** — addresses that PMOS tends to run in families, which strongly suggests that genes play a part in who develops it. Because the condition affects so many systems at once, researchers have looked at genes involved in hormone production, insulin signaling, inflammation, blood sugar control, and normal ovarian function[^pmos-genes]. So far, the clearest links involve genes that affect how the body handles insulin and fat, and genes that control androgen and reproductive hormone levels. Together, these genes may explain common symptoms of PMOS.
- **Insulin hypothesis** — emphasizes that insulin does more than just control blood sugar: it also helps regulate how the ovaries grow and how much androgen they produce[ref: Dapas M, Lin FT, Nadkarni GN, Sisk R, Legro RS, Urbanek M, et al. Distinct subtypes of polycystic ovary syndrome with novel genetic associations: an unsupervised, phenotypic clustering analysis. PLoS Med. 2020;17(6):e1003132. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003132]. Many women with PMOS, roughly 50–70%, have higher-than-normal insulin levels[ref: Poretsky L, Cataldo NA, Rosenwaks Z, Giudice LC. The insulin-related ovarian regulatory system in health and disease. Endocr Rev. 1999;20(4):535–582. https://doi.org/10.1210/edrv.20.4.0374]. Muscle and fat respond less to insulin, so the body compensates by making more of it[ref: Diamanti-Kandarakis E, Papavassiliou AG. Outstanding questions. Trends Mol Med. 2006;7(12):324–332. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmed.2006.05.006][^petersen2018]. This extra insulin pushes androgen levels up in several ways. It works alongside Luteinizing Hormone (LH) to boost androgen production in the ovaries[^petersen2018], stimulates the adrenal glands, shifts the hormone balance toward LH over Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)[ref: Tosi F, Negri C, Brun E, Castello R, Faccini G, Bonora E, et al. Insulin enhances ACTH-stimulated androgen and glucocorticoid metabolism in hyperandrogenic women. Eur J Endocrinol. 2011;164(2):197. https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-10-0782], and lowers Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG) so that more testosterone circulates freely[ref: Feng C, Jin Z, Chi X, Zhang B, Wang X, Sun L, et al. SHBG expression is correlated with PI3K/AKT pathway activity in a cellular model of human insulin resistance. Gynecol Endocrinol. 2018;34(7):567–573. https://doi.org/10.1080/09513590.2017.1411474]. Insulin resistance and excess androgens therefore feed into each other, and this cycle is thought to be one of the main drivers of PMOS.
- **Environmental hypothesis** — places importance on emerging evidence that suggests how environmental toxins may play a role in PMOS, with endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) being the most studied. Because their structure resembles natural hormones, these chemicals can bind hormone receptors, disrupting metabolic and reproductive processes. Everyday exposure comes largely from plastics, including food packaging, cosmetics, baby bottles and receipts, which release compounds like BPA and phthalates.
- **Neuroendocrine hypothesis** — places the primary defect upstream of the ovary, in the hypothalamic circuits controlling reproduction. Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) is released in pulses whose frequency determines which gonadotropin dominates. Fast pulses favor LH, slow pulses FSH[ref: Chang RJ. The reproductive phenotype in polycystic ovary syndrome. Nat Clin Pract Endocrinol Metab. 2007;3(10):688–695.] and in PMOS this balance shifts toward LH, driving theca-cell androgen production while reduced FSH arrests follicular development[ref: Chang RJ. The reproductive phenotype in polycystic ovary syndrome. Nat Clin Pract Endocrinol Metab. 2007;3(10):688–695. Jonard S, Dewailly D. The follicular excess in polycystic ovaries, due to intra-ovarian hyperandrogenism, may be the main culprit for the follicular arrest. Hum Reprod Update. 2004;10(2):107–117.]. Furthermore, it has been tested in mice that prenatal exposure to androgens can reproduce the phenotype[ref: Sullivan SD, Moenter SM. Prenatal androgens alter GABAergic drive to gonadotropin-releasing hormone neurons: implications for a common fertility disorder. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2004;101(18):7129–7134.], as does elevated prenatal anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)[ref: Tata B, Mimouni NEH, Barbotin AL, Malone SA, Loyens A, Pigny P, et al. Elevated prenatal anti-Müllerian hormone reprograms the fetus and induces polycystic ovary syndrome in adulthood. Nat Med. 2018;24(6):834–846.]. Whether the neuroendocrine change is cause or consequence remains unresolved[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31265059/], but it definitely has a big impact on PMOS.

</ExpandableText>

That gap is precisely what motivates our approach. By targeting the biological root that drives multiple symptoms at once, rather than the downstream symptoms individually, our EMPOWER team hopes to contribute a first step toward treatments that could meaningfully change the lives of this large and underserved patient population.

From what we've seen, the two sides of PMOS, metabolic and reproductive, have largely been studied separately, and there are multiple links between endocrine and metabolic symptoms, but the overall pathways are very complex and differ significantly between patients.

This gave us the idea to look into the influence of something that could be the link between multiple symptoms but also manifest differently between individuals … THE GUT MICROBIOME.

The human intestinal microbiome and its many metabolites have been widely recognized to influence human physiology[ref: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/links-between-diet-gut-microbiota-composition-and-gut-metabolism/3EC628D5D5F59D6E9F800E18AC1485D6]. The field focusing on microbiome research has gained significant traction over the years.

Intestinal microbiome composition of healthy women compared to women with PMOS is very different[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33669557/]. And the more we searched through the literature, the more consistently one unique difference emerged; the overabundance of *Phocaeicola vulgatus*[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33669557/].

<ExpandableText title="How different is the gut microbiome composition?">

Women with PMOS show a consistently altered gut microbiome compared with healthy controls, characterized by reduced α diversity (species richness) and a shifted β diversity, alongside an imbalance in the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio[ref: Torres PJ, Siakowska M, Banaszewska B, et al. Gut microbial diversity in women with polycystic ovary syndrome correlates with hyperandrogenism. J Clin Endocrinol Metab. 2018;103(4):1502–1511.][ref: Liu R, Zhang C, Shi Y, et al. Dysbiosis of gut microbiota associated with clinical parameters in polycystic ovary syndrome. Front Microbiol. 2017;8:324.][ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33669557/]. Beneficial, anti-inflammatory genera such as *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* are depleted[ref: Guo Y, Qi Y, Yang X, et al. Association between polycystic ovary syndrome and gut microbiota. PLoS ONE. 2016;11(4):e0153196.], together with faecal short-chain fatty acids, weakening gut barrier function and increasing endotoxin translocation[ref: Ratajczak W, Rył A, Mizerski A, et al. Immunomodulatory potential of gut microbiome-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Acta Biochim Pol. 2019;66(1):1–12.]. Conversely, *Escherichia*/*Shigella*, *Bacteroides vulgatus*, and *Catenibacterium* and *Kandleria* are enriched. The best-characterized difference regards *P. vulgatus*, which carries a higher abundance of bile salt hydrolase (bsh) genes in PMOS. This has been linked to insulin resistance, disrupted oestrous cycling and ovarian dysfunction in recipient mice[ref: Qi X, Yun C, Sun L, et al. Gut microbiota–bile acid–interleukin-22 axis orchestrates polycystic ovary syndrome. Nat Med. 2019;25(8):1225–1233.]. Changes are more pronounced in insulin-resistant PMOS, which shows the highest Bacteroidaceae, a greater loss of Prevotellaceae, and altered Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae[ref: Zeng B, Lai Z, Sun L, et al. Structural and functional profiles of the gut microbial community in polycystic ovary syndrome with insulin resistance (IR-PCOS): a pilot study. Res Microbiol. 2019;170(1):43–52.].

Disclaimer: These findings, however, are not uniform, because microbiome research is complex. Some studies have been found to contradict one another, so we looked into multiple papers to come to the best conclusion.

</ExpandableText>

*P. vulgatus* is a Gram-negative, anaerobic bacterium and one of the most abundant commensal members of the human gut microbiota, known for being able to use a wide range of substrates[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35080666/] (Lück & Deppenmeier, 2022).

Its overabundance in gut microbiome of women affected by PMOS does not necessarily mean that this bacterium is causing the condition. There's a big misconception within the scientific community when it comes to gut microbiome association and causation.

<ExpandableText title="Read more about the misconception within the scientific community">

One of biggest misconceptions in microbiome discussions is observing a bacterium that goes up in a disease and concluding it causes the disease. Although *P. vulgatus* levels are enriched in PMOS, the same condition of dysbiosis appears in other diseases too, but not always. In the context of inflammatory bowel disease, overabundance of *P. vulgatus* proteases may worsen the disease and inhibiting them was reported as a possible therapeutic route[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8852248/]. However, its role in obesity is reversed, and it was reported as protective since its metabolites provide improvement in lipid profiles for high-fat-diet-induced obesity[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39569932/]. But these are just 2 examples out of many...

We don't have enough data to classify *P. vulgatus* as a bad bacterium. It is a commensal and one of the most abundant species in the healthy human gut. Its role depends on the context and cannot be given as a causal element for PMOS.

In case of PMOS, it is also worth remembering that it's not a single-cause condition. Whether the gut microbiome contributes or adapts to this remains an unresolved question, but it is only one of numerous reasons that are a part of the whole self-sustaining cycle.

</ExpandableText>

What causes a whole debate around *P. vulgatus* is its nature. This bacterium is a primary degrader, meaning that it can use a wide range of substrates in our gut. This is very helpful when it comes to adapting to new conditions, whether they're good or bad. Its success at surviving stress caused by Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), better than other closely related bacteria[^ros-survival], and presence of certain enzymes is important to consider in our context.

<ExpandableText title={<>History of the name change from <em>Bacteroides</em> to <em>Phocaeicola</em></>}>

If you look up the bacteria with the name *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, you will encounter only a small number of papers written in the past years. However, if you search for *Bacteroides vulgatus*, you will find plenty. Initially, the name was given in 1933[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC533498/] by Eggerth and Gagnon after isolating it from human faeces and naming it vulgatus (common). Bacterial genera were defined based on what could be observed physically and measured like cell shape, oxygen tolerance... However, when bioinformatic methods arrived the classification of bacteria shifted toward analyzing the 16s rRNA gene which consists of around 1500nt. In 2019, García-López et al.[^garcialopez2019] claimed Bacteroidetes classification is limited to interpretation of poorly resolved 16S rRNA gene trees and a limited number of phenotypic features. They have used draft genome sequences from over Bacteroidetes and outgroup type strains to phylogenetic trees from genome-scale data.

On those trees, *Bacteroides vulgatus* and 13 other *Bacteroides* species turned out to be more closely related to *Phocaeicola* than to *B. fragilis*, the type species that defines the genus *Bacteroides*. Before this study between the two genera, the mainly accepted phenotypic difference was their motility. The authors claim *Phocaeicola* was wrongfully interpreted as having a flagellum because the method used to stain the bacteria was not appropriate to identify it[^garcialopez2019]. Hence, they have rejected the motility distinction and rebuilt the boundary between both genera with no consistent phenotypic difference but rather based on where the organisms sit on the genome tree. They measured relatedness by comparing the complete protein content of every genome available. As a result, *B. vulgatus* name was changed to *P. vulgatus* by correcting its place on the phylogenetic tree. The name later became formally valid in 2020.

</ExpandableText>

In women with PMOS, *P. vulgatus* abundance came alongside more genes encoding Bile Salt Hydrolases (BSH) which actively modify bile acids in the human gut. This is our point of focus because bile acids are crucial molecules, involved in the degradation of dietary fats, reuptake of cholesterol, and many other metabolic processes. In our gut, they're perceived as signaling molecules by our body.

<ExpandableText title="What are bile acids and how are they synthesized?">

Bile acids are the body's own detergents, but they are also important signaling molecules. Beyond emulsifying dietary lipids in the gut lumen, they activate specific receptors with distinct physiological effects across organ systems, such as the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) and the membrane receptor Takeda G-protein-coupled Receptor 5 (TGR5).[^fmicb2025][^apsb2024]

The two primary bile acids made in the human liver are cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), produced through a series of cytochrome P450 enzymes with CYP7A1 as the rate-limiting step. They are then conjugated to taurine or glycine by bile acid–CoA ligase and bile acid–CoA amino acid N-acyltransferase. This step is crucial for turning bile acid into a bile salt, keeping it ionised and trapped in the lumen.[^fimmu2022]

The healthy bile salt human pool is roughly three to five grams, of which about 95% is actively reclaimed in the distal ileum and only ~5% (~0.5 g) through the faeces. That small escaping fraction is where the microbiome takes over.[^fimmu2022]

Gut-derived BSH catalyzes the reaction of the wider bile acid modification pathway, shaping the bile acid landscape and mediating crosstalk between the microbiome and host. Only after deconjugation can the rest follow. First, 7α-dehydroxylation generates the secondary bile acids deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), while hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases oxidize and epimerize the ring hydroxyls. Bacteria are also able to re-conjugate a class of microbially conjugated bile acids, linked to amino acids such as phenylalanine, leucine and tyrosine, which has recently been described and found elevated in Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) and cystic fibrosis.[^fimmu2022]

Why we care. The pool arriving at any receptor is not a fixed liver output but a shape, set largely by which microbial enzymes are present. BSH sits at the top of that cascade and is an enzyme *P. vulgatus* Bv46 encodes. It shows deconjugating activity on plate assay, with supplementation shifting the faecal pool toward unconjugated and secondary bile acids in hyperlipidaemic rats[ref: https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1758707]. Downstream, bile acid signaling reaches well beyond the gut: bile acid metabolism and gut dysbiosis have been proposed as components of a gut–liver axis linked to ovarian function, and TGR5 is an established mediator of metabolic effects.[^apsb2024]

</ExpandableText>

Based on the emerging evidence in the literature, we hypothesize that communication between the gut microbiome and immune system occurs through bile acid metabolism. Bile acids exert different effects through dedicated host receptors, which translate shifts in pool composition into intracellular signaling in both epithelial and immune cells of the gut.

Among various receptors, Takeda G-protein coupled receptor (TGR5) is one of the most potent bile acid receptors[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12294878/] present on ILC3 (innate lymphoid cells type 3), but also on certain enterocytes[^chiang2017].

<ExpandableText title="More about TGR5">

This receptor can respond to both conjugated and unconjugated bile acids, with lithocholic acid and taurolithocholic acid being the most potent endogenous agonists[ref: Sato H., Macchiarulo A., Thomas C., Gioiello A., Une M., Hofmann A.F., Saladin R., Schoonjans K., Pellicciari R., Auwerx J. Novel potent and selective bile acid derivatives as TGR5 agonists. J Med Chem. 2008;51:1831–1841.], and signals through Gαs-mediated adenylate cyclase activation, raising intracellular cAMP and engaging Protein Kinase A (PKA) and Exchange Protein Directly Activated by cAMP (EPAC)[ref: Pols T.W., Noriega L.G., Nomura M., Auwerx J., Schoonjans K. The bile acid membrane receptor TGR5 as an emerging target in metabolism and inflammation. J Hepatol. 2011;54:1263–1272.]. In the intestine, this cascade drives secretion of GLP-1 and Peptide Tyrosine Tyrosine (PYY) from enteroendocrine L-cells, while TGR5 activation in immune cells dampens macrophage activation and reinforces epithelial barrier integrity[ref: Thomas C., Gioiello A., Noriega L., Strehle A., Oury J., Rizzo G., Macchiarulo A., Yamamoto H., Mataki C., Pruzanski M., et al. TGR5-mediated bile acid sensing controls glucose homeostasis. Cell Metab. 2009;10:167–177.].

</ExpandableText>

Specifically, binding of glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) and tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) to TGR5 stimulates a downstream pathway which activates the transcription factor GATA3 and thus Interleukin 22 (IL-22) expression[^s41591]. This anti-inflammatory cytokine can bind its receptors in the ovaries, specifically the granulosa cells, leading to the expression of ovulation-related genes via JAK-STAT signaling[ref: https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaae037] and a happy state of the ovaries.

As TGR5 binds conjugated bile acids with a higher affinity than deconjugated, the entire pathway is downregulated when this bile acid pool changes[ref: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26146547].

<Tgr5Ilc3Figure />

To understand how this influences the body in case of PMOS, we need to consider both metabolic and reproductive consequences. In inflamed conditions, there's an excess of ROS. Most *Bacteroides* are sensitive to this and die. However, *P. vulgatus* manages to survive even though it's strictly anaerobic[^ros-survival]. Following this, more BSH activity is present and consequently there are fewer conjugated bile acids. Less activity of TGR5 means that IL-22 levels drop, contributing to overall inflammation of the ovaries.

The more we looked into various pathways driving PMOS, the more pathways we found to feed into one another. Besides inflammation, interactions with TGR5 also include insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism.

<ExpandableText title="How is TGR5 involved in insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism?">

Insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism are symptoms of PMOS that interplay with each other. By binding the TGR5 receptor, conjugated bile acids lead to the production of Glucagon-like Peptide-1 (GLP1)[^apsb2024][^chiang2017][^fmicb2025]. This leads to hyperinsulinemia, which in turn causes less Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG) synthesis in the liver and more androgen production in ovarian theca cells[ref: Plymate, Stephen R., Louis A. Matej, Robert E. Jones, and Karl E. Friedl. "Inhibition of Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin Production in the Human Hepatoma (Hep G2) Cell Line by Insulin and Prolactin." The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism 67, no. 3 (1988): 460-64. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-67-3-460]. A dysregulated Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal axis (HPA) also contributes to hyperandrogenism[^hpa-axis].

</ExpandableText>

Focusing on one target upstream of multiple symptoms is the core of our EMPOWER approach! We are on a mission to engineer *P. vulgatus* by replacing the native BSH gene with one of its homologs with a higher affinity for different bile acids.

The choice of *P. vulgatus* as our chassis relies on the fact that it's a commensal bacteria powerful enough to resist inflamed and disbalanced conditions in order to deliver the desired therapeutic effect. But in order to work with this non-model organism we need the <Link to="/experimental-overview">right genetic tools...</Link>

With the right synthetic biology tools and identified BSH of interest, we want to ensure successful engineering and therapy which will ensure complete restoration of bile acid pool and proper activation of TGR5. This approach will then mitigate multiple symptoms at once: inflammation, insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism, all challenging fertility, which is one of the most consequential symptoms of PMOS.

In parallel, we characterized 15 BSH homologs from a multitude of related species to better understand the enzymes at the center of it all. As a uniform expression vector, we worked with model organism, *E. coli*, to express BSHs and test their deconjugative ability by growing them in media with individual bile salts at specific concentrations. Our endgame is utilizing *P. vulgatus* to restore bile acid balance through a BSH gene replacement, leaving TGR5 in presence of conjugated bile acids to mediate symptoms of PMOS. By introducing a rewired bacterium already fit to live in a complex environment as gut microbiome of PMOS patients, we aim to rebuild the pool of bile acids and restore health of women with PMOS.

### How many people live with PMOS?

In order to understand the scale of the problem we're addressing, finding the most accurate estimate of how many women are affected by this condition and how these cases are distributed across the world is crucial[ref: Lin T, Xie B, Yang J, et al. Changes in the global burden of polycystic ovary syndrome from 1990 to 2021. Reproductive Health 22, 86 (2025).]. Most published studies report results for large statistical regions rather than specific continents, so we calculated the continent totals ourselves from openly available data.

We used the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Results, made available by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)[ref: GBD 2023, Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME); GBD Results Tool. https://ghdx.healthdata.org/]. GBD is the most comprehensive dataset on disease burden worldwide. It provides standardized estimates for 204 countries and territories, which makes numbers comparable between countries.

Every search we used the same settings:

| Cause | Polycystic ovarian syndrome |

What we changed between each search was the location. GBD groups countries into 21 regions that do not follow continental borders. We therefore ran one search per continent and selected regions or individual countries so that every country was counted in the right continent.

For each continent, we summarized the case numbers of the selected locations. As a reference, we searched for global total data separately. The six continent totals add up to 67.93 million, compared to the global GBD value of 67.91 million, a difference of 0.03%. This confirms that no region was missed or counted twice.

<Callout title="Disclaimer: how to interpret the data">

- What we present here are **case counts**. Asia has the most cases largely because most of the world's population lives there. The numbers show where the most affected women live, not where PMOS is most common per person.
- The real number is much higher. GBD mostly counts diagnosed cases, however PMOS is known to be heavily underdiagnosed. The international consensus that renamed the condition estimates it affects about 1 in 8 women worldwide, which is well over 170 million people[^lancet2026]. The gap between ~68 million recorded cases and this estimate represent women who have never been diagnosed.
- Furthermore, underdiagnosis is not even. Regions with limited healthcare access record very low numbers. This most likely reflects missed diagnoses rather than fewer cases.
- Lastly, all GBD values are model-based estimates and come with a 95% uncertainty interval. For the global total, this means the true number of recorded cases lies between roughly 48 and 93 million women.

</Callout>
