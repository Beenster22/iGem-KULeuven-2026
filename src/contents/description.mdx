## Abstract

Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) is the most common metabolic and endocrine disorder affecting approximately one in eight women of reproductive age. However, high prevalence and severity of the condition does not reflect the funding, research, and understanding it deserves. To this day, no approved disease-modifying treatment exists.

Recent studies show that the intestinal microbiome can play a crucial role in the development and progression of PMOS. We hypothesize that a shift in microbial composition, and the following altered bile acid pool, sits upstream of symptoms such as insulin resistance, inflammation, hyperandrogenism, and fertility complications. Rather than eliminating this bacterium, we aim to engineer *P. vulgatus* to modulate bile acid metabolism and harness its potential as a therapeutic microorganism. However, the genetic engineering of this non-model anaerobe remains challenging, with limited tools available. To address this gap, we developed a synthetic biology toolkit for *P. vulgatus*, including characterized promoters evaluated using four fluorescent reporters, optimized conjugation, and a phenotypic characterization of potential chassis strains. Building on this foundation, we developed EMPOWER (Engineering Microbes to Promote Ovarian WEllbeing and Recovery), a strategy designed to selectively modulate BSH activity in *P. vulgatus*, with the goal of restoring beneficial bile acid signaling and improving hormonal and metabolic function in PMOS.

## Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome

For most of its history, Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS), previously known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), has been misunderstood and underresearched. This is striking information considering that it's one of the most common endocrine disorders affecting roughly 5 to 15% of women worldwide[ref: Noland, M. J. O., & Olfert, M. D. (2026). Updated overview of the geographical prevalence of polycystic ovary syndrome by diagnostic criteria, phenotypes, race, and ethnicity. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, *23*(8), Article 1085. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph23081085]. It is also the biggest cause of anovulatory infertility in women of reproductive age, accounting for up to 80% of women[ref: Zheng, L., Xu, X., Zhou, J. Z., Hong, L., He, Y. F., Fang, Y. X., Wang, B. B., Chen, H., Chen, K. J., Yang, S. S., Yang, X. L., Pan, H. F., & Zhou, S. G. (2025). The burden of polycystic ovary syndrome-related infertility in 204 countries and territories, 1990–2021: An analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. *Frontiers in Endocrinology*, *16*, Article 1559246. https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2025.1559246].

**Not-so-fun fact:** the global burden of infertility due to this condition rose from 6 million cases in 1990 to 12.13 million in 2019[ref: Zheng, L., Xu, X., Zhou, J. Z., Hong, L., He, Y. F., Fang, Y. X., Wang, B. B., Chen, H., Chen, K. J., Yang, S. S., Yang, X. L., Pan, H. F., & Zhou, S. G. (2025). The burden of polycystic ovary syndrome-related infertility in 204 countries and territories, 1990–2021: An analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. *Frontiers in Endocrinology*, *16*, Article 1559246. https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2025.1559246]. These numbers don't necessarily mean that PMOS incidence increased; they reflect the fact that PMOS goes undiagnosed for a very long period of time due to its complex and multifaceted nature.

Why has a condition this common and consequential received so little scientific attention? Why are there still so many challenges even after 150 years of studying PMOS? We've asked plenty of questions and received no answer, which is why we knew we needed to do something...

<ExpandableText title="Want to know more about the history of PMOS?">

PMOS has been first identified in 1844, when Chereau described enlarged ovaries studded with small peripheral cysts. After this, multiple names followed (sclerocystic ovaries, follicular degeneration, chronic cystic oophoritis). Additionally, it was originally described as inflammation of the female genital organs[ref: Chereau, A. (1844), and Polk, W. (1895), as cited in Adashi, E. Y., Cibula, D., Peterson, M., & Azziz, R. (2023). The polycystic ovary syndrome: The first 150 years of study. *F&S Reports*, *4*(1), 2–18. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xfre.2022.12.002].

In 1935, Stein and Leventhal linked the ovarian pathology to menstrual dysfunction, hirsutism and infertility. They argued that the cause was hormonal, which led to a new name, Stein-Leventhal Syndrome[ref: Stein, I. F., & Leventhal, M. L. (1935). Amenorrhea associated with bilateral polycystic ovaries. *American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology*, *29*(2), 181–191. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-9378(15)30642-6]. Afterwards, Polycystic Ovary Disease became Polycystic Ovary Syndrome once it was clear the disorder was not a single specific disease[ref: Azziz, R. (2018). Polycystic ovary syndrome. *Obstetrics & Gynecology*, *132*(2), 321–336. https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000002698], but the term "ovary" stayed at the center of the name.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="The most recent name change">

After years of advocacy on this matter, in May 2026, following a global consensus process involving more than 22,000 patients and health professionals, endorsed by 56 professional and patient organizations, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome was formally renamed Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome[^lancet2026]. The old name was completely misleading as it pointed at ovarian cysts as the main symptom, but this was just the tip of the iceberg. Polycystic ovarian morphology is neither universal nor the source of the condition's main burden. The previous name obscures the endocrine and metabolic dysfunction that drives long-term complications. Meanwhile, the new name is a great step forward for funding opportunities and more accurate description, and is being phased in with full implementation in the 2028 International Guideline update[^lancet2026]. This means that most of the literature we cite still uses PCOS. We are very eager to help implement this change and will be using Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) throughout this wiki!

</ExpandableText>

## What does PMOS look like?

According to the Rotterdam criteria, PMOS is traditionally characterized by a combination of androgen excess, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology[ref: Gibson-Helm, M., Teede, H., Dunaif, A., & Dokras, A. (2017). Delayed diagnosis and a lack of information associated with dissatisfaction in women with polycystic ovary syndrome. *The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism*, *102*(2), 604–612. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2016-2963]. However, in practice, the syndrome manifests very differently from patient to patient.

<ExpandableText title="What are the Rotterdam criteria for diagnosing PMOS?">

The Rotterdam criteria are the diagnostic standard for PMOS. They were agreed at a 2003 workshop in Rotterdam convened by the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ESHRE/ASRM) and require that two of three features are present, considering that other causes with same symptoms are excluded. These features are irregular or absent ovulation; hyperandrogenism, either clinical (hirsutism, acne) or biochemical; and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound[ref: Rotterdam ESHRE/ASRM-Sponsored PCOS Consensus Workshop Group. (2004). Revised 2003 consensus on diagnostic criteria and long-term health risks related to polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). *Human Reproduction*, *19*(1), 41–47. https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deh098]. Based on this, PMOS is also divided into different subgroups (A-D), with A being the most difficult one.

The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline retained this two-of-three structure and refined it, notably by allowing elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) as an alternative to ultrasound in adults, and by not applying the ovarian morphology criterion to adolescents at all[ref: Teede, H. J., Tay, C. T., Laven, J. J. E., Dokras, A., Moran, L. J., Piltonen, T. T., Costello, M. F., Boivin, J., Redman, L. M., Boyle, J. A., Norman, R. J., Mousa, A., & Joham, A. E. (2023). Recommendations from the 2023 international evidence-based guideline for the assessment and management of polycystic ovary syndrome. *The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism*, *108*(10), 2447–2469. https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgad463]. Because any two of the three features suffice, eight different combinations can lead to the same diagnosis.

</ExpandableText>

PMOS presents differently between individuals and across regions, often delaying diagnosis. This prolonged uncertainty adds a psychological burden for women who are already at higher risk of depression and anxiety due to the hormonal imbalances associated with PMOS[ref: Dybciak, P., Humeniuk, E., Raczkiewicz, D., Krakowiak, J., Wdowiak, A., & Bojar, I. (2022). Anxiety and depression in women with polycystic ovary syndrome. *Medicina*, *58*(7), Article 942. https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina58070942].

## Additional hurdles of PMOS

Furthermore, PMOS causes considerable financial burden to both individuals and the overall medical system. It is estimated that in the US alone annual cost of diagnosis and treatment of symptoms and associated disorders is $15 billion[ref: Yadav, S., Delau, O., Bonner, A. J., Markovic, D., Patterson, W., Ottey, S., Buyalos, R. P., & Azziz, R. (2023). Direct economic burden of mental health disorders associated with polycystic ovary syndrome: Systematic review and meta-analysis. *eLife*, *12*, Article e85338. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.85338].

Since there's no known etiology for PMOS, current therapeutic approaches come from off-label use of other medications to treat each symptom individually. This shows to be effective in some cases, but not all. No current treatment is effective when it comes to tackling all of the symptoms at once. This matches what women with PMOS told us in our patient interviews: current treatments often do not work as expected and address only one symptom at a time (see <Link to="/human-practices">Integrated Human Practices</Link>). Symptoms seem to be addressed based on the patient's need at the moment of examination, whether that's a contraceptive pill to regulate the cycle, fertility treatments, insulin-sensitizing drugs or treating acne and hirsutism. This treatment and knowledge gap is a critical issue that continues to sustain a vicious cycle of PMOS, while prevalence is high, <Link to="/human-practices#legislators-advocates">funding and treatment gaps</Link> remain persistent.

That gap is precisely what motivates our approach. By targeting the biological root that drives multiple symptoms at once, rather than the downstream symptoms individually, our EMPOWER team hopes to contribute a first step toward treatments that could meaningfully change the lives of this large and underserved patient population.

<SectionItem label="Evolutionary hypothesis">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Evolutionary hypothesis</h4>

Proposes how under ancestral conditions, such as heavy physical labor, periods of famine and epidemic disease, favoring efficient energy storage would have been advantageous[^thrifty]. Considering a drastic life change of continuous food availability and sedentary living, the same genotype is hypothesized to contribute to the rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes, and possibly to PMOS[^thrifty].

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Genetic hypothesis">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Genetic hypothesis</h4>

Addresses that PMOS tends to run in families, which strongly suggests that genes play a part in who develops it. Because the condition affects so many systems at once, researchers have looked at genes involved in hormone production, insulin signaling, inflammation, blood sugar control, and normal ovarian function[^pmos-genes-1][^pmos-genes-2][^pmos-genes-3][^pmos-genes-4]. So far, the clearest links involve genes that affect how the body handles insulin and fat, and genes that control androgen and reproductive hormone levels. Together, these genes may explain common symptoms of PMOS.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Insulin hypothesis">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Insulin hypothesis</h4>

Emphasizes that insulin does more than just control blood sugar: it also helps regulate how the ovaries grow and how much androgen they produce[ref: Dapas, M., Lin, F. T., Nadkarni, G. N., Sisk, R., Legro, R. S., Urbanek, M., et al. (2020). Distinct subtypes of polycystic ovary syndrome with novel genetic associations: An unsupervised, phenotypic clustering analysis. *PLOS Medicine*, *17*(6), Article e1003132. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003132]. Many women with PMOS, roughly 50–70%, have higher-than-normal insulin levels[ref: Poretsky, L., Cataldo, N. A., Rosenwaks, Z., & Giudice, L. C. (1999). The insulin-related ovarian regulatory system in health and disease. *Endocrine Reviews*, *20*(4), 535–582. https://doi.org/10.1210/edrv.20.4.0374]. Muscle and fat respond less to insulin, so the body compensates by making more of it[ref: Diamanti-Kandarakis, E., & Papavassiliou, A. G. (2006). Outstanding questions. *Trends in Molecular Medicine*, *7*(12), 324–332. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmed.2006.05.006][^petersen2018]. This extra insulin pushes androgen levels up in several ways. It works alongside Luteinizing Hormone (LH) to boost androgen production in the ovaries[^petersen2018], stimulates the adrenal glands, shifts the hormone balance toward LH over Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)[ref: Tosi, F., Negri, C., Brun, E., Castello, R., Faccini, G., Bonora, E., et al. (2011). Insulin enhances ACTH-stimulated androgen and glucocorticoid metabolism in hyperandrogenic women. *European Journal of Endocrinology*, *164*(2), 197. https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-10-0782], and lowers Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHBG) so that more testosterone circulates freely[ref: Feng, C., Jin, Z., Chi, X., Zhang, B., Wang, X., Sun, L., et al. (2018). SHBG expression is correlated with PI3K/AKT pathway activity in a cellular model of human insulin resistance. *Gynecological Endocrinology*, *34*(7), 567–573. https://doi.org/10.1080/09513590.2017.1411474]. Insulin resistance and excess androgens therefore feed into each other, and this cycle is thought to be one of the main drivers of PMOS.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Environmental hypothesis">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Environmental hypothesis</h4>

Places importance on emerging evidence that suggests how environmental toxins may play a role in PMOS, with endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) being the most studied. Because their structure resembles natural hormones, these chemicals can bind hormone receptors, disrupting metabolic and reproductive processes. Everyday exposure comes largely from plastics, including food packaging, cosmetics, baby bottles and receipts, which release compounds like Bisphenol A (BPA) and phthalates.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Neuroendocrine hypothesis">

<h4 data-toc-ignore="true">Neuroendocrine hypothesis</h4>

Places the primary defect upstream of the ovary, in the hypothalamic circuits controlling reproduction. Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) is released in pulses whose frequency determines which gonadotropin dominates. Fast pulses favor LH, slow pulses FSH[ref: Chang, R. J. (2007). The reproductive phenotype in polycystic ovary syndrome. *Nature Clinical Practice Endocrinology & Metabolism*, *3*(10), 688–695.] and in PMOS this balance shifts toward LH, driving theca-cell androgen production while reduced FSH arrests follicular development[ref: Chang, R. J. (2007). The reproductive phenotype in polycystic ovary syndrome. *Nature Clinical Practice Endocrinology & Metabolism*, *3*(10), 688–695.; Jonard, S., & Dewailly, D. (2004). The follicular excess in polycystic ovaries, due to intra-ovarian hyperandrogenism, may be the main culprit for the follicular arrest. *Human Reproduction Update*, *10*(2), 107–117.]. Furthermore, it has been tested *in vivo* that prenatal exposure to androgens can reproduce the phenotype[ref: Sullivan, S. D., & Moenter, S. M. (2004). Prenatal androgens alter GABAergic drive to gonadotropin-releasing hormone neurons: Implications for a common fertility disorder. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *101*(18), 7129–7134.], as does elevated prenatal anti-Müllerian hormone[ref: Tata, B., Mimouni, N. E. H., Barbotin, A. L., Malone, S. A., Loyens, A., Pigny, P., et al. (2018). Elevated prenatal anti-Müllerian hormone reprograms the fetus and induces polycystic ovary syndrome in adulthood. *Nature Medicine*, *24*(6), 834–846.]. Whether the neuroendocrine change is cause or consequence remains unresolved[ref: Ruddenklau, A., & Campbell, R. E. (2019). Neuroendocrine impairments of polycystic ovary syndrome. *Endocrinology*, *160*(10), 2230–2242. https://doi.org/10.1210/en.2019-00428], but it definitely has a big impact on PMOS.

</SectionItem>

</WheelSelector>

</ExpandableText>

## How is the gut microbiome involved?

From what we've seen, the two sides of PMOS, metabolic and reproductive, have largely been studied separately, and there are multiple links between endocrine and metabolic symptoms, but the overall pathways are very complex and differ significantly between patients.

This gave us the idea to look into the influence of something that could be the link between multiple symptoms but also manifest differently between individuals … THE GUT MICROBIOME.

The human intestinal microbiome and its many metabolites have been widely recognized to influence human physiology[ref: Flint, H. J., Duncan, S. H., Scott, K. P., & Louis, P. (2014). Links between diet, gut microbiota composition and gut metabolism. *Proceedings of the Nutrition Society*, *74*(1), 13–22. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0029665114001463]. The field focusing on microbiome research has gained significant traction over the years.

The intestinal microbiome composition of healthy women compared to those with PMOS is very different[ref: Giampaolino, P., Foreste, V., Di Filippo, C., Gallo, A., Mercorio, A., Serafino, P., Improda, F. P., Verrazzo, P., Zara, G., Buonfantino, C., Borgo, M., Riemma, G., Angelis, C., Zizolfi, B., Bifulco, G., & Della Corte, L. (2021). Microbiome and PCOS: State-of-art and future aspects. *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, *22*(4), Article 2048. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms22042048]. The more we searched through the literature, the more consistently one unique difference emerged: the overabundance of *Phocaeicola vulgatus*[ref: Giampaolino, P., Foreste, V., Di Filippo, C., Gallo, A., Mercorio, A., Serafino, P., Improda, F. P., Verrazzo, P., Zara, G., Buonfantino, C., Borgo, M., Riemma, G., Angelis, C., Zizolfi, B., Bifulco, G., & Della Corte, L. (2021). Microbiome and PCOS: State-of-art and future aspects. *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, *22*(4), Article 2048. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms22042048].

<ExpandableText title="How different is the gut microbiome composition?">

Women with PMOS show a consistently altered gut microbiome compared with healthy controls, characterized by reduced α diversity (species richness) and a shifted β diversity, alongside an imbalance in the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio[ref: Torres, P. J., Siakowska, M., Banaszewska, B., et al. (2018). Gut microbial diversity in women with polycystic ovary syndrome correlates with hyperandrogenism. *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism*, *103*(4), 1502–1511.][ref: Liu, R., Zhang, C., Shi, Y., et al. (2017). Dysbiosis of gut microbiota associated with clinical parameters in polycystic ovary syndrome. *Frontiers in Microbiology*, *8*, Article 324.][ref: Giampaolino, P., Foreste, V., Di Filippo, C., Gallo, A., Mercorio, A., Serafino, P., Improda, F. P., Verrazzo, P., Zara, G., Buonfantino, C., Borgo, M., Riemma, G., Angelis, C., Zizolfi, B., Bifulco, G., & Della Corte, L. (2021). Microbiome and PCOS: State-of-art and future aspects. *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, *22*(4), Article 2048. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms22042048]. Beneficial, anti-inflammatory genera such as *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* are depleted[ref: Guo, Y., Qi, Y., Yang, X., et al. (2016). Association between polycystic ovary syndrome and gut microbiota. *PLOS ONE*, *11*(4), Article e0153196.], together with faecal short-chain fatty acids, weakening gut barrier function and increasing endotoxin translocation[ref: Ratajczak, W., Rył, A., Mizerski, A., et al. (2019). Immunomodulatory potential of gut microbiome-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). *Acta Biochimica Polonica*, *66*(1), 1–12.]. Conversely, *Escherichia*/*Shigella*, *Bacteroides vulgatus*, and *Catenibacterium* and *Kandleria* are enriched. The best-characterized difference regards *P. vulgatus*, which carries a higher abundance of bile salt hydrolase (BSH) genes in PMOS. This has been linked to insulin resistance, disrupted oestrous cycling and ovarian dysfunction in recipient mice[ref: Qi, X., Yun, C., Sun, L., et al. (2019). Gut microbiota–bile acid–interleukin-22 axis orchestrates polycystic ovary syndrome. *Nature Medicine*, *25*(8), 1225–1233. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-019-0509-0]. Changes are more pronounced in insulin-resistant PMOS, which shows the highest Bacteroidaceae, a greater loss of Prevotellaceae, and altered Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae[ref: Zeng, B., Lai, Z., Sun, L., et al. (2019). Structural and functional profiles of the gut microbial community in polycystic ovary syndrome with insulin resistance (IR-PCOS): A pilot study. *Research in Microbiology*, *170*(1), 43–52.].

Disclaimer: These findings, however, are not uniform, because microbiome research is complex. Some studies have been found to contradict one another, so we looked into multiple papers to come to the best conclusion.

</ExpandableText>

## *Phocaeicola vulgatus* and its nature

*P. vulgatus* is a Gram-negative, anaerobic bacterium and one of the most abundant commensal members of the human gut microbiota, known for being able to use a wide range of substrates[ref: Lück, R., & Deppenmeier, U. (2022). Genetic tools for the redirection of the central carbon flow towards the production of lactate in the human gut bacterium *Phocaeicola* (*Bacteroides*) *vulgatus*. *Applied Microbiology and Biotechnology*, *106*(3), 1211–1225. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-022-11777-6].

Its overabundance in the gut microbiome of women affected by PMOS does not necessarily mean that this bacterium is causing the condition. There's a big misconception within the scientific community when it comes to gut microbiome association and causation.

<ExpandableText title="Read more about the misconception within the scientific community">

One of biggest misconceptions in microbiome discussions is observing a bacterium that goes up in a disease and concluding it causes the disease. Although *P. vulgatus* levels are enriched in PMOS, the same condition of dysbiosis appears in other diseases too, but not always. In the context of inflammatory bowel disease, overabundance of *P. vulgatus* proteases may worsen the disease and inhibiting them was reported as a possible therapeutic route[ref: Mills, R. H., Dulai, P. S., Vázquez-Baeza, Y., Sauceda, C., Daniel, N., Gerner, R. R., Batachari, L. E., Ochoa, M. M., Zhu, Q., Weldon, K., Humphrey, G., Carrillo-Terrazas, M., Goldasich, L. D., Bryant, M., Raffatellu, M., Quinn, R. A., Gewirtz, A. T., Chassaing, B., Chu, H., Sandborn, W. J., Dorrestein, P. C., Knight, R., & Gonzalez, D. J. (2022). Multi-omics analyses of the ulcerative colitis gut microbiome link *Bacteroides vulgatus* proteases with disease severity. *Nature Microbiology*, *7*(2), 262–276. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-021-01050-3]. However, its role in obesity is reversed, and it was reported as protective since its metabolites provide improvement in lipid profiles for high-fat-diet-induced obesity[ref: Wen, X., Feng, X., Xin, F., An, R., Huang, H., Mao, L., Liu, P., Zhang, J., Huang, H., Liu, X., & Wang, W. (2024). *B. vulgatus* ameliorates high-fat diet-induced obesity through modulating intestinal serotonin synthesis and lipid absorption in mice. *Gut Microbes*, *16*(1), Article 2423040. https://doi.org/10.1080/19490976.2024.2423040]. But these are just 2 examples out of many...

We don't have enough data to classify *P. vulgatus* as a bad bacterium. It is a commensal and one of the most abundant species in the healthy human gut. Its role depends on the context and cannot be given as a causal element for PMOS.

In case of PMOS, it is also worth remembering that it's not a single-cause condition. Whether the gut microbiome contributes or adapts to this remains an unresolved question, but it is only one of numerous reasons that are a part of the whole self-sustaining cycle.

</ExpandableText>

What causes a whole debate around *P. vulgatus* is its nature. This bacterium is a primary degrader, meaning that it can use a wide range of substrates in our gut. This is very helpful when it comes to adapting to new conditions, whether they're good or bad. Its success at surviving stress caused by Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), better than other closely related bacteria[^ros-survival], and presence of certain enzymes is important to consider in our context.

<ExpandableText title={<>History of the name change from <em>Bacteroides</em> to <em>Phocaeicola</em></>}>

If you look up the bacteria with the name *Phocaeicola vulgatus*, you will encounter only a small number of papers written in the past years. However, if you search for *Bacteroides vulgatus*, you will find plenty. Initially, the name was given in 1933[ref: Eggerth, A. H., & Gagnon, B. H. (1933). The Bacteroides of human feces. *Journal of Bacteriology*, *25*(4), 389–413. https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.25.4.389-413.1933] by Eggerth and Gagnon after isolating it from human faeces and naming it vulgatus (common). Bacterial genera were defined based on what could be observed physically and measured like cell shape, oxygen tolerance... However, when bioinformatic methods arrived the classification of bacteria shifted toward analyzing the 16s rRNA gene which consists of around 1500nt. In 2019, García-López et al.[^garcialopez2019] claimed Bacteroidetes classification is limited to interpretation of poorly resolved 16S rRNA gene trees and a limited number of phenotypic features. They have used draft genome sequences from over Bacteroidetes and outgroup type strains to phylogenetic trees from genome-scale data.

On those trees, *Bacteroides vulgatus* and 13 other *Bacteroides* species turned out to be more closely related to *Phocaeicola* than to *B. fragilis*, the type species that defines the genus *Bacteroides*. Before this study between the two genera, the mainly accepted phenotypic difference was their motility. The authors claim *Phocaeicola* was wrongfully interpreted as having a flagellum because the method used to stain the bacteria was not appropriate to identify it[^garcialopez2019]. Hence, they have rejected the motility distinction and rebuilt the boundary between both genera with no consistent phenotypic difference but rather based on where the organisms sit on the genome tree. They measured relatedness by comparing the complete protein content of every genome available. As a result, *B. vulgatus* name was changed to *P. vulgatus* by correcting its place on the phylogenetic tree. The name later became formally valid in 2020.

</ExpandableText>

## Link to PMOS through bile acid pool

In women with PMOS, *P. vulgatus* abundance comes alongside genes encoding Bile Salt Hydrolases (BSH) which actively modify bile acids in the human gut. This is our point of focus because bile acids are crucial molecules, involved in the digestion and absorption of dietary fibers, reuptake of cholesterol, and many other metabolic processes. In our gut, they're perceived as signaling molecules.

Bile acids are the body's own detergents, but they are also important signaling molecules. Beyond emulsifying dietary lipids in the gut lumen, they activate specific receptors with distinct physiological effects across organ systems, such as the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) and the membrane receptor Takeda G-protein-coupled Receptor 5 (TGR5).[^fmicb2025][^apsb2024]

The two primary bile acids made in the human liver are cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), produced through a series of cytochrome P450 enzymes with CYP7A1 as the rate-limiting step. They are then conjugated to taurine or glycine by bile acid–CoA ligase and bile acid–CoA amino acid N-acyltransferase. This step is crucial for turning bile acid into a bile salt, keeping it ionised and trapped in the lumen.[^fimmu2022]

The healthy bile salt human pool is roughly three to five grams, of which about 95% is actively reclaimed in the distal ileum and only ~5% (~0.5 g) through the faeces. That small escaping fraction is where the microbiome takes over.[^fimmu2022]

Gut-derived BSH catalyzes the reaction of the wider bile acid modification pathway, shaping the bile acid landscape and mediating crosstalk between the microbiome and host. Only after deconjugation can the rest follow. First, 7α-dehydroxylation generates the secondary bile acids deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), while hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases oxidize and epimerize the ring hydroxyls. Bacteria are also able to re-conjugate a class of microbially conjugated bile acids, linked to amino acids such as phenylalanine, leucine and tyrosine, which has recently been described and found elevated in Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) and cystic fibrosis.[^fimmu2022]

Why we care. The pool arriving at any receptor is not a fixed liver output but a shape, set largely by which microbial enzymes are present. BSH sits at the top of that cascade and is an enzyme *P. vulgatus* Bv46 encodes. It shows deconjugating activity on plate assay, with supplementation shifting the faecal pool toward unconjugated and secondary bile acids in hyperlipidaemic rats[ref: Xu, H., Li, M., Yang, S., & Jiang, D. (2026). Immune cytokines as a bridge linking the gut–liver–ovary axis in the pathogenesis of premature ovarian failure. *Frontiers in Endocrinology*, *17*, Article 1758707. https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1758707]. Downstream, bile acid signaling reaches well beyond the gut: bile acid metabolism and gut dysbiosis have been proposed as components of a gut–liver axis linked to ovarian function, and TGR5 is an established mediator of metabolic effects.[^apsb2024]

</ExpandableText>

## Our hypothesis

Based on the emerging evidence in the literature, we hypothesize that communication between the gut microbiome and immune system occurs through bile acid metabolism. Bile acids exert different effects through dedicated host receptors, which translate shifts in pool composition into intracellular signaling in both epithelial and immune cells of the gut.

Among various receptors, the Takeda G-protein coupled receptor (TGR5) is one of the most potent bile acid receptors[ref: Zangerolamo, L., Carvalho, M., & Barbosa, H. C. L. (2025). The critical role of the bile acid receptor TGR5 in energy homeostasis: Insights into physiology and therapeutic potential. *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, *26*(14), Article 6547. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26146547] present on immune cells, and on certain enterocytes[^chiang2017].

<ExpandableText title="More about TGR5">

This receptor can respond to both conjugated and unconjugated bile acids, with lithocholic acid and taurolithocholic acid being the most potent endogenous agonists[ref: Sato, H., Macchiarulo, A., Thomas, C., Gioiello, A., Une, M., Hofmann, A. F., Saladin, R., Schoonjans, K., Pellicciari, R., & Auwerx, J. (2008). Novel potent and selective bile acid derivatives as TGR5 agonists. *Journal of Medicinal Chemistry*, *51*, 1831–1841.], and signals through Gαs-mediated adenylate cyclase activation, raising intracellular cAMP and engaging Protein Kinase A (PKA) and Exchange Protein Directly Activated by cAMP (EPAC)[ref: Pols, T. W., Noriega, L. G., Nomura, M., Auwerx, J., & Schoonjans, K. (2011). The bile acid membrane receptor TGR5 as an emerging target in metabolism and inflammation. *Journal of Hepatology*, *54*, 1263–1272.]. In the intestine, this cascade drives secretion of Glucagon-like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) and Peptide Tyrosine Tyrosine (PYY) from enteroendocrine L-cells, while TGR5 activation in immune cells dampens macrophage activation and reinforces epithelial barrier integrity[ref: Thomas, C., Gioiello, A., Noriega, L., Strehle, A., Oury, J., Rizzo, G., Macchiarulo, A., Yamamoto, H., Mataki, C., Pruzanski, M., et al. (2009). TGR5-mediated bile acid sensing controls glucose homeostasis. *Cell Metabolism*, *10*, 167–177.].

<ZoomableImage src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6299/wiki/project/description/proj-descrip-img-top-part.avif" alt="Diagram of bile acid signaling through TGR5 up to GLUT4, showing the top part of the GLP-1 pathway figure." />

</ExpandableText>

Specifically, binding of glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) and tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) to TGR5 stimulates a downstream pathway which activates the transcription factor GATA3 and therefore Interleukin 22 (IL-22) expression[^s41591]. This anti-inflammatory cytokine can bind its corresponding receptors in the ovaries, specifically granulosa cells, leading to the expression of ovulation-related genes via JAK-STAT signaling[ref: Liao, B., Chen, W., Qi, X., Yun, C., & Pang, Y. (2024). Interleukin-22 improves ovulation in polycystic ovary syndrome via STAT3 signaling. *Molecular Human Reproduction*, *30*(10), Article gaae037. https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaae037] and a healthy state of the ovaries.

<Tgr5Ilc3Figure />

To understand how this influences the body in the case of PMOS, we need to consider both metabolic and reproductive consequences. In inflamed conditions, there's an excess of ROS. Most *Bacteroides* are sensitive to this and die. However, *P. vulgatus* manages to survive even though it's strictly anaerobic[^ros-survival]. Following this, more BSH activity is present and consequently there are fewer conjugated bile acids. As TGR5 binds conjugated bile acids with a higher affinity than deconjugated, the entire pathway is downregulated when this bile acid pool changes[ref: Zangerolamo, L., Carvalho, M., & Barbosa, H. C. L. (2025). The critical role of the bile acid receptor TGR5 in energy homeostasis: Insights into physiology and therapeutic potential. *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, *26*(14), Article 6547. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26146547]. Less activity of TGR5 means that IL-22 levels drop, contributing to overall inflammation of the ovaries.

## Our approach

Focusing on one target upstream of multiple symptoms is the core of our EMPOWER approach! We are on a mission to engineer *P. vulgatus* by replacing the native BSH gene with one of its homologs with a preference for different bile acids.

The choice of *P. vulgatus* as our chassis relies on the fact that it's a commensal bacteria powerful enough to resist inflamed and disbalanced conditions in order to deliver the desired therapeutic effect. But in order to work with this non-model organism we need the right genetic tools...

With the right synthetic biology tools and identified BSH of interest, we want to ensure successful engineering and therapy which will ensure complete restoration of the bile acid pool and proper activation of TGR5. This approach will then mitigate multiple symptoms at once: inflammation, insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism, all challenging fertility, which is one of the most consequential symptoms of PMOS.

<ExpandableText title="How can we make sure our engineered strain colonizes the gut...?">

Delivering an engineered microbial therapeutic requires more than a working genetic circuit. The circuit has to function inside a hostile luminal environment, carried by an organism competing against microbes that have adapted to that specific niche over thousands of generations. A well-designed construct in a poorly adapted chassis will be outcompeted before it can act.

Autologous cell therapies such as Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy deliver a therapeutic effect without provoking the immune response that other approaches risk. The same logic has been gaining traction in microbiome research: engineer bacteria isolated from the patient's own stool rather than a laboratory strain. Russell et al. (2022)[ref: Russell, B. J., Brown, S. D., Siguenza, N., Mai, I., Saran, A. R., Lingaraju, A., Maissy, E. S., Dantas Machado, A. C., Pinto, A. F. M., Sanchez, C., Rossitto, L. A., Miyamoto, Y., Richter, R. A., Ho, S. B., Eckmann, L., Hasty, J., Gonzalez, D. J., Saghatelian, A., Knight, R., & Zarrinpar, A. (2022). Intestinal transgene delivery with native *E. coli* chassis allows persistent physiological changes. *Cell*, *185*(17), 3263–3277.e15. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.06.050] investigated this directly, arguing that native bacteria are already maximally adapted to the luminal environment of their host and therefore make an ideal chassis for transgene delivery. They engineered *E. coli* strains isolated from mouse stool to carry a transgene (BSH or IL-10) and returned them to the same animals. The native isolates engrafted permanently in 100% of mice for over 110 days, while a standard laboratory chassis failed to establish.

Considering these results, using the patient's own isolate as the chassis is an approach adopted by EMPOWER. We wouldn't have taken this route if it wasn't for our meeting with <Link to="/human-practices#weston-whitaker">Weston</Link>, who proposed this approach and shared relevant scientific papers with us.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="...and how do we keep control of it once it does?">

Using a patient-derived chassis solves one problem and creates another. *P. vulgatus* is already abundant in the gut microbiota, so our engineered strain competes directly with a resident population that is equally well adapted and already consuming the available resources. Without some advantage, the engineered strain risks being washed out before reaching the density needed for a therapeutic effect, and reproducible colonization becomes impossible.

<Link to="/human-practices#weston-whitaker">Whitaker</Link> et al. (2025)[ref: Whitaker, W. R., Russ, Z. N., Shepherd, E. S., Popov, L. M., Louie, A., Lam, K., Zong, D. M., Gill, C. C. C., Gehrig, J. L., Rishi, H. S., Tan, J. A., Buness, A., Godoy, J., Banta, D., Jaidka, S., Wilson, K., Flood, J., Bukshpun, P., Yocum, R., Cook, D. N., Warsi, T., McLean, L., Sonnenburg, J. L., & Deloache, W. C. (2025). Controlled colonization of the human gut with a genetically engineered microbial therapeutic. *Science*, *389*(6757), 303–308. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adu8000] addressed exactly this by programming engineered strains to metabolize a nutrient that resident strains cannot. They engineered *P. vulgatus* so that its colonization level became tunable with porphyran, a seaweed-derived polysaccharide chosen because only around 2% of Western individuals carry a resident microbe capable of digesting it[ref: Pudlo, N. A., Pereira, G. V., Parnami, J., Cid, M., Markert, S., Tingley, J. P., Unfried, F., Ali, A., Rechner, K., Schnizlein, M. K., Young, M. N., Schmidt, T. S. B., Zheng, H., Hehemann, J.-H., Abbott, D. W., Mirande, M., Terrapon, N., Henrissat, B., Young, V. B., ... Martens, E. C. (2022). Diverse events have transferred genes for edible seaweed digestion from marine to human gut bacteria. *Cell Host & Microbe*, *30*(3), 314–328.e311. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2022.02.007], and the engineered strain therefore occupies an exclusive niche.

Based on this study, in a Phase 1 trial in healthy volunteers, 11 of 12 participants engrafted (7 of 8 in one dosing group and 4 of 4 in another) once gastric acid protection was included, with strain density rising roughly 10-fold for every 5-fold increase in porphyran intake. Withdrawing porphyran dropped the strain below the limit of detection in 15 of 19 engrafted volunteers. Engraftment was therefore not only achievable but tunable and reversible through diet alone.

Together these strategies address complementary halves of the same problem: the autologous chassis gives the engineered strain the fitness to survive, and the orthogonal nutrient gives it a reason to persist, and a switch to turn it off.

</ExpandableText>

In parallel, we characterized 15 <Link to="/results">BSH homologs</Link> from a multitude of related species to better understand the enzymes at the center of it all. By introducing a rewired bacterium already fit to live in a complex environment such as the gut microbiome of PMOS patients, we aim to rebuild the pool of bile acids and thereby restore health of women with PMOS.

<Callout title="Disclaimer: how to interpret the data">

- What we present here are **case counts**. Asia has the most cases largely because most of the world's population lives there. The numbers show where the most affected women live, not where PMOS is most common per person.
- The real number is much higher. GBD mostly counts diagnosed cases, however PMOS is known to be heavily underdiagnosed. The international consensus that renamed the condition estimates it affects about 1 in 8 women worldwide, which is well over 170 million people[^lancet2026]. The gap between ~68 million recorded cases and this estimate represent women who have never been diagnosed.
- Furthermore, underdiagnosis is not even. Regions with limited healthcare access record very low numbers. This most likely reflects missed diagnoses rather than fewer cases.
- Lastly, all GBD values are model-based estimates and come with a 95% uncertainty interval. For the global total, this means the true number of recorded cases lies between roughly 48 and 93 million women.
