<ExpandableText title="Want to know more about the history of PMOS and name change?">

This condition was first identified in 1844, when Chereau described enlarged ovaries studded with small peripheral cysts. After this, multiple names followed (sclerocystic ovaries, follicular degeneration, chronic cystic oophoritis). Additionally, it was originally described as inflammation of the female genital organs (Chereau, 1844; Polk, 1895; reviewed in Adashi et al., 2022).

In 1935, Stein and Leventhal linked the ovarian pathology to menstrual dysfunction, hirsutism and infertility. They argued that the cause was hormonal, which led to a new name, Stein-Leventhal Syndrome (Stein & Leventhal, 1935). Afterwards, Polycystic Ovary Disease became Polycystic Ovary Syndrome once it was clear the disorder was not a single specific disease[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29995717/], but the term "ovary" stayed at the center of the name.

After years of advocacy on this matter, in May 2026, following a global consensus process involving more than 22,000 patients and health professionals, endorsed by 56 professional and patient organizations, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome was formally renamed Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome[^lancet2026]. The old name was completely misleading, as it pointed to ovarian cysts as the main symptom, but this was just the tip of the iceberg. Polycystic ovarian morphology is not universal nor the source of the condition's main burden. The previous name obscured the endocrine and metabolic dysfunction that drives long-term complications. The new name is a great step forward, however, it's being phased in over three years, with full implementation in the 2028 International Guideline update[^lancet2026]. This means that most of the literature we cite still uses PCOS. We are very eager to help implement this change and will be using Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) throughout this wiki!

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What are the Rotterdam criteria for diagnosing PMOS?">

The Rotterdam criteria are the diagnostic standard for PMOS. They were agreed at a 2003 ESHRE/ASRM consensus workshop in Rotterdam and require that two of three features are present, considering that other causes with same symptoms are excluded. These features are irregular or absent ovulation; hyperandrogenism, either clinical (hirsutism, acne) or biochemical; and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound[ref: https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article-abstract/19/1/41/690226?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false]. Based on this PMOS is also divided into different subgroups (A-D), with A being the most difficult one.

The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline retained this two-of-three structure and refined it, notably by allowing elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) as an alternative to ultrasound in adults, and by not applying the ovarian morphology criterion to adolescents at all[ref: https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/108/10/2447/7242360]. Because any two of the three features suffice, eight different combinations can lead to the same diagnosis, which is why two patients with PMOS may share almost no symptoms, and a large part of why the condition is so difficult to study.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Learn more about the different hypotheses of PMOS etiology">

As the actual cause of this condition remains unknown, over the years there have been many hypotheses created around the root cause of PMOS. Nisa et al. group them into five[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10784448/]:

- **Environmental hypothesis** — This hypothesis proposes how under ancestral conditions, such as heavy physical labour, periods of famine and epidemic disease, genotype favoring efficient energy storage would have been advantageous[^thrifty]. In a modern environment of continuous food availability and sedentary living, the same genotype is hypothesized to contribute to the rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes, and possibly to PMOS[^thrifty].
- **Genetic hypothesis** — PMOS tends to run in families, which strongly suggests that genes play a part in who develops it. Because the condition affects so many systems at once, researchers have looked at genes involved in hormone production, insulin signalling, inflammation, blood sugar control and normal ovarian function[^pmos-genes]. So far, the clearest links involve genes that affect how the body handles insulin and fat, and genes that control androgen and reproductive hormone levels. Together these may explain common features like insulin resistance[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9100625/], weight gain, excess androgens and irregular ovulation[ref: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1007813].
- **Insulin hypothesis** — Insulin does more than control blood sugar: it also helps regulate how the ovaries grow and how much androgen they produce[ref: Dapas M, Lin FT, Nadkarni GN, Sisk R, Legro RS, Urbanek M, et al. Distinct subtypes of polycystic ovary syndrome with novel genetic associations: an unsupervised, phenotypic clustering analysis. PLoS Med. 2020;17(6):e1003132. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003132]. Many women with PMOS, roughly 50–70%, have higher-than-normal insulin levels[ref: Poretsky L, Cataldo NA, Rosenwaks Z, Giudice LC. The insulin-related ovarian regulatory system in health and disease. Endocr Rev. 1999;20(4):535–582. https://doi.org/10.1210/edrv.20.4.0374]. Muscle and fat respond less to insulin, so the body compensates by making more of it[ref: Diamanti-Kandarakis E, Papavassiliou AG. Outstanding questions. Trends Mol Med. 2006;7(12):324–332. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmed.2006.05.006][^petersen2018]. This extra insulin pushes androgen levels up in several ways. It works alongside LH to boost androgen production in the ovaries[^petersen2018], stimulates the adrenal glands, shifts the hormone balance toward LH over FSH[ref: Tosi F, Negri C, Brun E, Castello R, Faccini G, Bonora E, et al. Insulin enhances ACTH-stimulated androgen and glucocorticoid metabolism in hyperandrogenic women. Eur J Endocrinol. 2011;164(2):197. https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-10-0782], and lowers SHBG so that more testosterone circulates freely[ref: Feng C, Jin Z, Chi X, Zhang B, Wang X, Sun L, et al. SHBG expression is correlated with PI3K/AKT pathway activity in a cellular model of human insulin resistance. Gynecol Endocrinol. 2018;34(7):567–573. https://doi.org/10.1080/09513590.2017.1411474]. Insulin resistance and excess androgens therefore feed into each other, and this cycle is thought to be one of the main drivers of PMOS.
- **Environmental hypothesis** — More and more evidence suggests that environmental toxins may play a role in PMOS, with endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) being the most studied. Because their structure resembles natural hormones, these chemicals can bind hormone receptors, disrupting metabolic and reproductive processes. Everyday exposure comes largely from plastics, including food packaging, cosmetics, baby bottles and receipts, which release compounds like BPA and phthalates.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="More about the name change from Bacteroides to Phocaeicola?">

If you look up the bacteria with the name Phocaeicola vulgatus, you will encounter only a small number of papers written in the past years. However, if you search for Bacteroides vulgatus, you will find plenty. Initially, the name was given in 1933[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC533498/] by Eggerth and Gagnon by isolating it from human faeces and naming it vulgatus (common). Then bacterial genera were defined by what could be observed physically and measured based on cell shape, oxygen tolerance... However, when bioinformatic methods arrived the classification of bacteria shifted toward analyzing the 16s rRNA gene which consists of around 1500 nucleotides. In 2019 García-López et al.[ref: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02083/full] claimed Bacteroidetes classification is limited to interpretation of poorly resolved 16S rRNA gene trees and a limited number of phenotypic features. They have used draft genome sequences from over Bacteroidetes and outgroup type strains to phylogenetic trees from genome-scale data.

On those trees, Bacteroides vulgatus and thirteen other Bacteroides species turned out to be more closely related to Phocaeicola than to B. fragilis, the type species that defines the genus Bacteroides. Before this study between the two genera, the mainly accepted phenotypic difference was their motility. The authors claim Phocaeicola was wrongfully interpreted as having a flagellum although the method used to stain the bacteria was not appropriate to identify it. Hence, they have rejected the motility distinction and rebuilt the boundary between both genera with no consistent phenotypic difference but rather based on where the organisms sit on the genome tree, measuring relatedness by comparing the complete protein content of every genome against every other by BLAST. As a result, B. vulgatus name was changed to P. vulgatus by correcting its place on the phylogenetic tree. The name later became formally valid in 2020.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="Association vs. causation — a misconception in the scientific community">

One of biggest misconceptions in microbiome discussions is observing a bacterium that goes up in a disease and concluding it causes the disease. Although P. vulgatus levels are enriched in PMOS, the same condition of dysbiosis appears in other diseases too but not always in the same role. In the context of inflammatory bowel disease, overabundance of P. vulgatus proteases may worsen the disease and even inhibiting them[ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8852248/] was reported as a possible therapeutic route similar to our approach for BSH enzyme. However, its role in obesity is reversed, and it was reported as protective since its metabolite cholic acid provides improvement in lipid profiles for high-fat-diet-induced obesity[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39569932/]. Hence, P. vulgatus should not be classified as a pathogenic bacterium. It is a commensal and one of the most abundant species in the healthy human gut. Its role depends on the context and cannot be given as a causal element for PMOS. Hence, in human cases increased levels of the same bacteria may cause disease in one model and protection in the other.

Hence, the association observed in human remains correlative and abundance of bacteria may already reflect a gut environment that has already changed due to several factors. Additionally, it is also worth remembering that PMOS is not a single-cause condition. Even if the gut microbiome contributes, it is only one of the many reasons that are apart of the whole self-sustaining cycle. The direction of association remains unsolved since while the mice study tests bacteria causing the disease, another study by Kelley et al. (2016)[ref: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0146509] highlights inducing hyperandrogenism in mice caused shift in gut microbiome without any change in diet. As explained thoroughly, change in human gut microbiota cannot imply a causation with PMOS. Therefore, instead of aiming the dysbiosis in the gut, we aim targeting the enzymatic activity as a potential treatment method.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="What are bile acids and how are they synthesized?">

Bile acids are the body's own detergents, but they are also important signaling molecules. Beyond emulsifying dietary lipids in the gut lumen, they activate specific receptors with distinct physiological effects across organ systems, such as FXR and the membrane receptor TGR5.[^fmicb2025][^apsb2024]

The two primary bile acids made in the human liver are cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), produced through a series of cytochrome P450 enzymes with CYP7A1 as the rate-limiting step. They are then conjugated to taurine or glycine by bile acid–CoA ligase and bile acid–CoA amino acid N-acyltransferase, this step is crucial for turning bile acid into a bile salt, keeping it ionised and trapped in the lumen.[^fimmu2022]

The healthy bile salt human pool is roughly 3–5 g, of which about 95% is actively reclaimed in the distal ileum and only ~5% (~0.5 g) through the faeces. That small escaping fraction is where the microbiome takes over.[^fimmu2022]

Gut-derived bile salt hydrolase (BSH) catalyses the reaction of the wider bile acid modification pathway, shaping the bile acid landscape and mediating crosstalk between the microbiome and host. Only after deconjugation can the rest follow. First, 7α-dehydroxylation generates the secondary bile acids deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), while hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases oxidise and epimerise the ring hydroxyls. Bacteria are also able to re-conjugate a class of microbially conjugated bile acids, linked to amino acids such as phenylalanine, leucine and tyrosine, which has recently been described and found elevated in IBD and cystic fibrosis.[^fimmu2022]

Why we care. The pool arriving at any receptor is not a fixed liver output but a shape, set largely by which microbial enzymes are present. BSH sits at the top of that cascade and is an enzyme P. vulgatus Bv46 encodes and shows deconjugating activity on plate assay, with supplementation shifting the faecal pool toward unconjugated and secondary bile acids in hyperlipidaemic rats[ref: https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1758707]. Downstream, bile acid signaling reaches well beyond the gut: bile acid metabolism and gut dysbiosis have been proposed as components of a gut–liver axis linked to ovarian function, and TGR5 is an established mediator of metabolic effects.[^apsb2024]

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="More about this pathway">

Bacteroides, specifically P. vulgatus, is enriched in patients with PMOS[ref: https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2017-02153][ref: https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2017.00324][ref: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0168390]. These high P. vulgatus levels are negatively associated with the levels of bile acids, especially GDCA and TUDCA[^s41591]. TGR5 (Takeda G-protein coupled receptor) is the most active bile acid receptor present on ILC3 (innate lymphoid cells type 3), but also on certain enterocytes[^chiang2017]. When bile acids bind TGR5, it activates the transcription factor GATA3 and thus IL-22 expression[^s41591]. This IL-22 can bind its receptors in the ovaries, leading to the expression of ovulation-related genes via JAK-STAT signaling[ref: https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaae037].

As P. vulgatus is upregulated in PMOS, bsh expression is increased as well[^s41591]. Bile salt hydrolases deconjugate bile acids, so women with PMOS have a disrupted bile acid pool. As TGR5 binds conjugated bile acids with a higher affinity than deconjugated bile acids, the entire pathway is downregulated[ref: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26146547]. This can lead to anovulation.

</ExpandableText>

<ExpandableText title="How is TGR5 involved in insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism?">

Insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism are symptoms of PMOS that interplay with each other. By binding the TGR5 receptor, conjugated bile acids lead to the production of GLP-1[^apsb2024][^chiang2017][^fmicb2025]. This leads to hyperinsulinemia, which in turn causes less Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin (SHGB) synthesis in the liver and more androgen production in ovarian theca cells[ref: Plymate, Stephen R., Louis A. Matej, Robert E. Jones, and Karl E. Friedl. "Inhibition of Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin Production in the Human Hepatoma (Hep G2) Cell Line by Insulin and Prolactin." The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism 67, no. 3 (1988): 460-64. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-67-3-460]. A dysregulated HPA axis also contributes to hyperandrogenism[^hpa-axis].

</ExpandableText>

### How many people live with PMOS?

In order to understand the scale of the problem we're addressing, finding the most accurate estimate of how many women are affected by this condition and how these cases are distributed across the world is crucial[ref: Lin T, Xie B, Yang J, et al. Changes in the global burden of polycystic ovary syndrome from 1990 to 2021. Reproductive Health 22, 86 (2025).]. Most published studies report results for large statistical regions rather than specific continents, so we calculated the continent totals ourselves from openly available data.

We used the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Results, made available by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)[ref: GBD 2023, Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME); GBD Results Tool. https://ghdx.healthdata.org/]. GBD is the most comprehensive dataset on disease burden worldwide. It provides standardized estimates for 204 countries and territories, which makes numbers comparable between countries.

Every search we used the same settings:

| Field | Selection |
| --- | --- |
| GBD estimate | Cause of death or injury |
| Measure | Prevalence |
| Metric | Number |
| Cause | Polycystic ovarian syndrome |
| Sex | Female |
| Age | All ages |
| Year | **2023** (most recent available) |

What we changed between each search was the location. GBD groups countries into 21 regions that do not follow continental borders. We therefore ran one search per continent and selected regions or individual countries so that every country was counted in the right continent.

| Continent | Locations selected |
| --- | --- |
| Africa | African Union (all African countries) |
| Asia | East Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, High-income Asia Pacific, Central Asia, and 15 Middle Eastern countries (Afghanistan, Bahrain, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Türkiye, United Arab Emirates, Yemen) |
| Europe | Western Europe, Central Europe, Eastern Europe |

| Oceania | Australasia, Oceania |
| Global | Global |

For each continent, we summarized the case numbers of the selected locations. As a reference, we searched for global total data separately. The six continent totals add up to 67.93 million, compared to the global GBD value of 67.91 million, a difference of 0.03%. This confirms that no region was missed or counted twice.

<Callout title="Disclaimer: how to interpret the data">

- What we present here are **case counts**. Asia has the most cases largely because most of the world's population lives there. The numbers show where the most affected women live, not where PMOS is most common per person.
- The real number is much higher. GBD mostly counts diagnosed cases, however PMOS is known to be heavily underdiagnosed. The international consensus that renamed the condition estimates it affects about 1 in 8 women worldwide, which is well over 170 million people[^lancet2026]. The gap between ~68 million recorded cases and this estimate represent women who have never been diagnosed.
- Furthermore, underdiagnosis is not even. Regions with limited healthcare access record very low numbers. This most likely reflects missed diagnoses rather than fewer cases.
- Lastly, all GBD values are model-based estimates and come with a 95% uncertainty interval. For the global total, this means the true number of recorded cases lies between roughly 48 and 93 million women.

</Callout>
