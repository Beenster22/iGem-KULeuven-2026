<ExpandableText title="Want to know more about the history of PMOS and name change?">

This condition was first identified in 1844, when Chereau described enlarged ovaries studded with small peripheral cysts. After this, multiple names followed (sclerocystic ovaries, follicular degeneration, chronic cystic oophoritis). Additionally, it was originally described as inflammation of the female genital organs (Chereau, 1844; Polk, 1895; reviewed in Adashi et al., 2022).

In 1935, Stein and Leventhal linked the ovarian pathology to menstrual dysfunction, hirsutism and infertility. They argued that the cause was hormonal, which led to a new name, Stein-Leventhal Syndrome (Stein & Leventhal, 1935). Afterwards, Polycystic Ovary Disease became Polycystic Ovary Syndrome once it was clear the disorder was not a single specific disease[ref: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29995717/], but the term "ovary" stayed at the center of the name.

After years of advocacy on this matter, in May 2026, following a global consensus process involving more than 22,000 patients and health professionals, endorsed by 56 professional and patient organizations, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome was formally renamed Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome[^lancet2026]. The old name was completely misleading, as it pointed to ovarian cysts as the main symptom, but this was just the tip of the iceberg. Polycystic ovarian morphology is not universal nor the source of the condition's main burden. The previous name obscured the endocrine and metabolic dysfunction that drives long-term complications. The new name is a great step forward, however, it's being phased in over three years, with full implementation in the 2028 International Guideline update[^lancet2026]. This means that most of the literature we cite still uses PCOS. We are very eager to help implement this change and will be using Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) throughout this wiki!

</ExpandableText>

## Strain selection

Choosing which P. vulgatus strains to work with was a crucial part of the project. We chose 6 strains, aiming to compare a well-characterized type-strain from literature with clinically relevant fecal isolates.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Antibiotic selection">

## Antibiotic selection

Strain-specific literature on antibiotic susceptibility in P. vulgatus is sparse. So, we gathered the data, completing an extensive MIC panel across two strains, separating the antibiotics that stopped growth from those that didn't have any effect.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Reporter gene output">

## Reporter gene output

Promoters are essential tools for tuning gene expression. To visualize promoter expression, we measured GFP, mCherry, KOFP-7, and NanoLuc, hoping to test their compatibility with P. vulgatus and functionality in anaerobic conditions.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Medium comparison">

## Medium comparison

Finding which medium gives reproducibility and strong growth was done by testing four different media types. Growth curves gave us insight into which components are essential for P. vulgatus survival!

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Conjugation efficiency testing">

## Conjugation efficiency testing

To be able to deliver constructs, we had to know how and if conjugation works into P. vulgatus as heat shock and electroporation have a low success rate. Different plasmids were transferred from E. coli S17-1 λpir into our six strains.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Promotor screening">

## Promotor screening

There is almost no characterized promoter toolbox for P. vulgatus, so we assembled a library of literature-derived promoters into a reporter construct and carried each one through to the organism itself.

</SectionItem>

<SectionItem label="Codon Optimization & Harmonization tool">

## Codon Optimization & Harmonization tool

Expression in a non-model organism depends on the codons its ribosomes recognize, so we built a tool that adapts a gene sequence to P. vulgatus rather than simply maximizing codon frequency.

</SectionItem>
