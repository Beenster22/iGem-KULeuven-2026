## Phylogenetic analysis of the candidates

---

To understand how our candidates relate to one another and to enzymes with confirmed bile salt
hydrolase activity, we reconstructed a phylogeny of the full candidate panel together with
characterized reference sequences (24 sequences in total). The reference set was chosen to span the
taxonomic range over which BSH activity have been described: *Bifidobacterium*, *Collinsella*,
*Lactobacillus* and other related genera such as *Blautia*, *Bacteroides*, *Phocaeicola* and
*Parabacteroides*. In our analysis we have also included the biochemically characterized bile salt
hydrolase of *Bifidobacterium longum* (UniProt Q9KK62) as a reference point.

Prior to generating our tree we aligned the sequences with MAFFT version 7 and G-INS-1 as strategy,
after trimming we were left with 309 aligned positions, of which 289 were parsimony-informative. A
maximum likelihood tree was inferred in IQ-TREE 3.1.2., substitution models were screened with
ModelFinder and Q.PFAM+I+G4 selected by BIC, with the proportion of invariable sites estimated at
0.040 and a Gamma shape parameter of 1.96 across four rate categories. Branch support was assessed
by performing 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and 1000 SH-aLRT replicates.

The resulting tree was created using iTOL and separates the candidate set into two broad groups. One
contains the characterised *Bifidobacterium* enzyme together with the *Collinsella*, *Lactobacillus*
and *Blautia* sequences. The second contains the Bacteroidota sequences: *Bacteroides dorei*,
*Phocaeicola vulgatus*, *Phocaeicola plebeius* and *Parabacteroides merdae*, and is separated from
the first by substantially longer branches.

Two observations follow from this. First, sequence similarity to a known BSH is a weak guide within
our candidate set: the Bacteroidota candidates are distant enough from the characterised enzymes that
homology alone does not establish what reaction they catalyse. This is the concern that motivated our
docking analysis, we discuss further in the wiki. Second, the two *P. vulgatus* sequences in our set
do not group together. `HMPREF1058_00373` resolves alongside *B. dorei*, while `HMPREF1058_02605`
resolves with *P. merdae* on a long branch, indicating that this single strain carries two divergent
members of the choloylglycine hydrolase family rather than two copies of one enzyme.

## BSH–bile salt interaction using AutoDock Vina

---

Sequence-level evidence alone is not enough to call a gene a bile salt hydrolase (BSH). BSHs belong
to the cholylglycine hydrolase family, which also contains enzymes with different substrate
preferences, so homology searches can return proteins that share the fold and the catalytic
machinery without acting on bile salts. To address this before committing wet lab capacity, we asked
a structural question: does each candidate actually have a binding site capable of accommodating a
bile salt?

Bile salt hydrolases (BSHs) are N-terminal nucleophile hydrolases (Ntn) and their catalytic
nucleophile is a cysteine at position 2, exposed by autocatalytic cleavage[^bsh-1][^bsh-2]. A
candidate without a cysteine at this position does not match the canonical BSH catalytic
architecture. We therefore checked this residue in all 15 predicted structures. Eleven candidates
carry Cys2 and were treated as canonical. Four (bdor, pmer, ppleb and pvul1) have no cysteine at
position 2. Instead they carry an N-terminal extension with the profile of a signal peptide (a
hydrophobic stretch followed by a polar region), and the first cysteine downstream of it sits at
position 20–26. For these four we took that cysteine as the putative catalytic residue, assuming the
extension is cleaved to expose it. The assignment is inferred from sequence features and has not been
confirmed experimentally, so we flag these candidates as divergent from canonical BSHs regardless of
their docking results.

We predicted the structure of each of the 15 candidates with AlphaFold3 and docked them against a
panel of bile salts using AutoDock Vina, run in Google Colab so that the entire pipeline is
reproducible by anyone without local installation or licensed software. The docking search box was
centered on the catalytic cysteine of each protein. For each protein-ligand pair we recorded the
binding affinity of the top-scoring pose and inspected the pose relative to the catalytic residue,
since a favorable score in the wrong pocket says nothing useful.

Docking scores are a ranking tool, not a measurement. Vina affinities do not translate into kinetic
parameters, and the scoring function was not developed with steroid-scaffold ligands in mind. We
therefore treated the results as a prioritization step rather than a prediction of activity:
candidates that dock poorly across the entire bile salt panel, that bind in a pose incompatible with
catalysis, or that lack the Cys2 nucleophile are deprioritized, while the ranking is used to
interpret the BSH assay and LC-MS results rather than to replace them.
